There is a serious gap between how AMR is understood medically and how it is experienced by patients. Insight from the physicians confirm literature findings that the burden of AMR extends far beyond treatment failure. Patients may experience uncertainty, isolation, stigma, repeated screening, communication gaps, and barriers created by prolonged or resource-intensive treatment. These burdens can be amplified by healthcare-system constraints and socioeconomic circumstances. Addressing AMR requires not only better antimicrobials and stewardship, but also accessible education, patient-centred communication, and more emphasis on empathy and psychosocial support.

As our next steps, we aimed to investigate what the barriers are related to AMR. To do this, we conducted integrated human practices interviews with healthcare providers, including Dr. Hancock, Dr. Lester, Dr. Mroz, Dr. Blondel-Hill, Dr. Chan, and Lesley Kooner, a registered nurse. With the suggestion from Dr. Mroz that we should prioritize patient engagement and lived experiences rather than relying only on clinicians or literature review, we also conducted an interview with an AMR patient, Norma Washburn, who we were connected to through BCCDC.
Through the interview with healthcare providers, we identified that there is a major communication problem related to AMR. Dr. Hancock explained that public understanding of AMR was limited because antibiotics still appeared to work most of the time (due to backup plans), masking the growing resistance problem underneath. This problem was echoed by Dr. Lester, who noted that patients often misunderstood what resistance actually meant and may think themselves are resistant to antibiotics, not the bacteria. Dr. Blondel-Hill observed that patients often did not understand their diagnosis, the reason for screening, or why certain precautions were being taken around them. Dr. Liu told us the reasoning behind this communication problem: the more specialized physicians become, the less they know about the basics, naturally debriefing the patient less thoroughly. Both Dr. Liu and Lesley Kooner, a registered nurse, agreed that nurses need to be educated further on the topic of AMR, as they spend the most time caring for patients, in order to resolve this problem.
Norma’s story gave us a tangible example of this case. She described going through multiple rounds of antibiotics for her wound infection without being given a clear explanation of what she was taking or why. She told us that when her second antibiotic began causing pain in her Achilles tendon, it was not her care team but her own GP who eventually connected the symptom to the medication. She also observed a difference in care quality that lined up with what Dr. Liu had described. Nurses at the hospital repeated the same wound dressing approach even as her infection worsened, while the specialists at her dedicated wound clinic adjusted their technique when something wasn't working. She said that without her experience as a nurse, she "would be really really scared," because "people have no time to explain to patients."
In addition to Norma’s fear, our provider interviews pointed to a burden of AMR that extended beyond treatment failure. Dr. Lester noted the anxiety, stigma, fear, uncertainty, and social vulnerability that resistant infections could produce, including feelings of personal responsibility for having developed a resistant infection in the first place. Dr. Blondel-Hill highlighted isolation as a major burden in particular, as patients were kept apart from partners, newborns, and family for fear of transmission and often experiencing fewer and shorter interactions with healthcare providers as a result and even experiencing a delay in treatment. Lesley Kooner added that infection precautions could reduce not only visitor access but staff interaction as well, due to both fear of infection and the added burden of PPE requirements.
Worse, these burdens can be amplified by healthcare-system constraints and socioeconomic circumstances. Addressing AMR requires not only better antimicrobials and stewardship, but also accessible education, patient-centred communication, and more emphasis on psychosocial support to reduce barriers.
This directly informed our decision to build plain-language educational pamphlets so that patients would not be left to piece together their own diagnosis the way Norma had to. From our interview with Dr. Chan, we learned that pamphlets are valuable even more so than speaking to physicians directly as nurses are with patients all the time. Thus, if nurses are able to give out pamphlets to patients before their first consultation, patients would be able to come in with questions already. This could also help alleviate the psychosocial strains of frustration with “not knowing how to remove colonization or when/if infection will recur, etc…” if patients know sufficient information to begin with.
We partnered up with Fraser Health Authorities to create and distribute our pamphlets.
