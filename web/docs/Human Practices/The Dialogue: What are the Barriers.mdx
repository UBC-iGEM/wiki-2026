***

<a id="3fad65dd82be80c7b83af6049b89ff30" />There is a serious gap between how AMR is understood medically and how it is experienced by patients. Insight from the physicians confirm literature findings that the burden of AMR extends far beyond treatment failure. Patients may experience uncertainty, isolation, stigma, repeated screening, communication gaps, and barriers created by prolonged or resource-intensive treatment. These burdens can be amplified by healthcare-system constraints and socioeconomic circumstances. Addressing AMR requires not only better antimicrobials and stewardship, but also accessible education, patient-centred communication, and more emphasis on empathy and psychosocial support to reduce barriers.

<a id="3fad65dd82be80a88652c4e98dddac4d" />Through the interview with healthcare providers, we identified that there is a major communication problem and lack of education related to AMR. All the healthcare providers we spoke to explained to us that public understanding of AMR was limited. For instance, it was noted from the interview with Dr. Lester, infectious disease physician, that patients often misunderstood what resistance actually meant and may think themselves are resistant to antibiotics, not the bacteria. Dr. Blondel-Hill observed that patients often did not understand their diagnosis, the reason for screening, or why certain precautions were being taken around them. This was evident in the experiences of our patient partners as well: Norma recalled going through multiple rounds of antibiotics for her wound infection without being given a clear explanation of what she was taking or why. This directly informed our decision to build plain-language **educational pamphlets** so that patients would not be left to piece together their own diagnosis the way Norma had to. To widen access to education, we hosted workshops, partnered with the Geering-Up program, to educate the youth about AMR. Notably, it was also identified in the literature that misinformation was concentrated among young adults of ages 18-24. Thus, we aimed to educate the youth in the hopes that this trend of misinformation will not carry onto the next generation of young adults. We hosted a total of 7 **workshops** over the summer. Informative sessions explaining the mechanisms of AMR were hosted at the start of workshops, followed up with activity that allowed students to conceptualize the learned content. In addition to the workshops, we decided to work on an Instagram campaign to increase public awareness on AMR. We decided that **Instagram** was the best platform, as not only does it reach our young audience, but can reach global populations as well. This was especially important, as AMR is a global problem. In addition, documentaries and videos were created to further support the learning.

<a id="3fad65dd82be8085b396ecb55f8d195f" />Our provider interviews pointed to another burden of AMR that extended beyond treatment failure and lack of education. Dr. Lester noted the anxiety, stigma, fear, uncertainty, and social vulnerability that treatment-resistant infections can stir up in patients. Dr. Blondel-Hill highlighted isolation as a major burden in particular, as patients were kept apart from partners, newborns, and family for fear of transmission and often experiencing fewer and shorter interactions with healthcare providers as a result and even experiencing a delay in treatment. Lesley Kooner added that infection precautions could reduce not only visitor access but staff interaction as well, due to both fear of infection and the added burden of PPE requirements. The patient partners, Norma and Sunny, also noted the feeling of isolation when they were first diagnosed and difficulties in finding a sense of community. To better support the patients, we developed a **website that patients can freely access to communicate with each other** and find resources related to AMR, so they would not need to spend hours searching for credible information online.
