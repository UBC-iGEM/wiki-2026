<a id="3f3d65dd82be805f8639c0d6457ea7f9" />Our literature review gave us a starting point which revealed the scientific nature and clinical management of AMR. Although it was very informative and successfully familiarised our team with AMR, there was a significant gap of knowledge in what it is actually like for patients to live with AMR; what the psychological effects are, and how social factors can play a part in the experience as well. To close this gap, we turned to Integrated Human Practices. We spoke directly with clinicians, researchers, and a patient partner to understand how AMR affects people locally, and integrated the conversations to shape our project as it developed.

- <a id="3f3d65dd82be800fa4f1cca173007dc8" />Sunny Loo
- <a id="3f3d65dd82be80cd8adff05466695408" />Sunny Loo is a patient representative at the BCCDC, and has lived with vasculitis (GPA) for the past 18 years. As a retired pharmacist with over 40 years of experience developing patient support programs, and as a patient who had experienced antibiotic use firsthand, he offered us a perspective that bridged both the clinical and lived-experience sides of patients.

<a id="3f7d65dd82be80eb93fde85802927c75" />

***

<a id="3f3d65dd82be800082eedd58ad66e041" />For Sunny, the most lasting effect of his condition has been fear. As a patient who went through many invasive treatments, and as a result became immunocompromised, he feared infection, how often he might fall sick, which kinds of sickness he might suffer from, and whether antibiotics will aid in treatment or he will only develop resistance from it. His illness completely reshaped his life; he shared with us that his social and professional network narrowed down to his immediate family, he could no longer travel as he once had, and he ultimately had to change career paths. Worse, he mentioned that support was hard to find initially, as there was very little information available on GPA when he was diagnosed. He also emphasized that while healthcare providers focus on the illness and its treatment, it would make a world of difference for patients to be provided care that is coupled with empathy. Sunny told us that patients living with chronic illness are often "just asking to be understood, to be supported." He also pointed to barriers that make care harder, such as long waits for specialists, family physicians who may not recognize rare conditions, and overwhelming amounts of information online that makes it difficult to discern trustworthy advice. As for technical aspects, Sunny mentioned the value of point-of-care diagnostic tools that could help ensure patients receive the right treatment the first time to reduce the trial-and-error antibiotic use that contributes to resistance, especially for immunocompromised patients like himself.

<br id="3ecd65dd82be809c962eccb13ab605e7" />

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80d797b7f40167e11b4a" />Dr. Lester

<a id="3f9d65dd82be801e8785cff844868b56" />Dr. Lester is an infectious disease physician, clinician-scientist, and associate professor at the UBC Division of Infectious Diseases located at Vancouver General Hospital and the University of British Columbia.

<a id="3f9d65dd82be809bbb64f01d8c6de4c0" />

***

<a id="3f9d65dd82be8086bb32f935a16f7d57" />Dr. Lester informed us that improvement of physician-patient interaction is crucial for tackling AMR. He called for greater training of healthcare professionals in how to communicate to patients: why a treatment plan was chosen, and how antibiotics work. Furthermore, he suggested that we should create accessible resources to address common misconceptions, such as patients thinking themselves are resistant to antibiotics rather than the bacteria or thinking all bacteria are harmful, thus leading to misuse of antibiotics. Dr. Lester also brought a very important scope into our view: the psychosocial impacts of AMR. He mentioned that AMR can give rise to anxiety and uncertainty, stigma, fear of transmitting infections to family/friends, feelings of personal responsibility for developing resistant infections, individuals facing long-term resistant infections (ex. resistant HIV or TB), and prolonged psychosocial burdens because of extended treatment and side effects. These psychosocial burdens are often overlooked during care, as constant medical procedures must be carried out due to the severity of the illness. Moreover, additional vulnerability from pre-existing mental illness, substance use disorders, unstable housing, or lower socioeconomic status can exacerbate the burden of AMR on patients.

<br id="3ecd65dd82be80068f98e06021c1d99a" />

- <a id="3f3d65dd82be80aeba3cd3f2341eb50e" />Dr. Larry Mroz
- <a id="3f3d65dd82be801c8632ca55aaed7757" />Dr. Larry Mroz works at Health Research BC in the BC Support Unit as a Patient and Community Engagement Lead. He advised researchers in their research projects as they navigate discussions with patients with unique lived experiences.

<a id="3f9d65dd82be805db865dd1985df59cb" />

***

<a id="3f3d65dd82be807e8a5cdeab79f0e000" />\< We initially were hoping that we would speak to him about connecting us with patient partners, but our meeting with him ended up familiarizing us with a lot more. Dr. Mroz suggested that we create a website that outlines our project; that we include on there what we are trying to do and how people can get involved. We can also use this website as a platform to share our learning while leveraging our own social media to get the outreach started. He kindly suggested for us to compose a one-pager outlining our project and he would send it to his network of patients willing to share their lived experiences. He also shared with us a variety of resources and articles on the difference between patient engagement and qualitative research. Moving forward, we decided to frame our project as he suggested and look for patient partners as well as starting up our own instagram outreach campaign while building a website that better projects our mission.>

<br id="3f9d65dd82be801c9032c681ede0eb1a" />

- <a id="3f3d65dd82be809b9563ca5f1a243927" />Lesley Kooner
- <a id="3f3d65dd82be805a879dda80b8a54ed4" />Lesley is a registered nurse working with Providence Health. She has previous experience as a bedside nurse.

<a id="3f9d65dd82be805a8cc8c3447343584d" />

***

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80e8b8c1c8d8e803cff0" />Lesley raised patient isolation during hospitalisation as a significant psychosocial concern regarding AMR. She explained that to avoid contracting drug-resistant pathogens, contact between healthcare providers and patients become limited; the required usage of PPE and stigma surrounding nurses acquiring an infection from their patient discourages staff from visiting patients frequently. As a result, patients do not feel adequately supported, which strongly affects their mental health and in turn relationships with loved ones as well. These challenges are carried into the household when the patient is discharged; family members or caretakers must take additional precautions, such as bleaching washrooms, to contain the spread of antimicrobial resistance. Lesley told us that the best way to avoid all these barriers is to be careful to begin with, so that the chances of contracting pathogens are decreased. It was noted that education regarding hygiene, personal care, and the proper usage of antibiotics can help to reduce the prevalence of AMR. We learned that pamphlets would be the best way to deliver education to patients, as Lesley describes, “they are starved for visual stimuli”.

<br id="3f7d65dd82be80889a4eea1aba03e8a1" />

- <a id="3f3d65dd82be8061aa17f8c982dc0753" />Dr. Liu
- <a id="3f3d65dd82be8093be1def4eafa9509c" />Dr. Anthony Liu is a paediatric haematologist/oncologist at The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids) in Toronto. He completed his training in Hong Kong and obtained a fellowship in paediatric neuro-oncology at St. Jude Children's Research Hospital, where he led landmark research on childhood brain tumours.

<a id="3f9d65dd82be8078a636fbfaf3abb579" />

***

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80d1b6dfc642e985ce21" />\<Dr. Liu further expanded on the concept of patient isolation. He explained that patients are isolated and labelled in single rooms, as well as family members or healthcare providers who come to visit having to wear PPE and precautions. This leads to psychosocial distress for patients and as well as less assessment by the physician and reduced quality of care. Furthermore, he emphasized that doctors and nurses should be better educated on how to conduct patient interactions, such as debriefing the patient properly to reduce educational barriers and uncertainty for the patient. To clear up the stigma around AMR in general, he suggested explaining that different regions are naturally prone to different rates of transmission and managing consistent communication with the patient.>

<br id="3f9d65dd82be8098975efdca8371979b" />

- <a id="3f3d65dd82be80e28f6fce02997d56b8" />Dr. Chan
- <a id="3f3d65dd82be8051843ee554178c0dc0" />Dr. Chan is an infectious disease physician.

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80feb641edb69946bf20" />

***

<a id="3fad65dd82be80ae9cfff399d577ad69" />Firstly, Dr. Chan shared with us some psychosocial challenges she heard from her patients. She described how patients with AMR often return with the same infection and are frustrated as they do not know why it recurs or how to clear it. One case highlighted how AMR can affect family relationships; a patient’s wife avoided sleeping in the same bed for fear of catching MRSA, which the patient was receiving recurrent treatment for. She mentioned that family members in the household often are unaware of safety measures to protect themselves, such as proper usage of PPE or environmental cleaning, which are especially important in crowded homes. She also identified an important system gap that contribute to the frustration of patients: there is no clear protocol for removing an MRSA (or AMR?) label from a patient’s record. This may lead to quality of care being disrupted for patients under contact precautions, as doctors find gowning (usage of PPE) to be time-consuming. Dr. Chan elaborated on the socioeconomic factors affecting AMR as well. She explained that financial issues can pressure many people to live in one house, allowing infections to spread more easily. Moreover, people from lower-income households may rely on cheaper meat raised with antibiotics, which increase their exposure. Dr. Chan highlighted common practices that increase risk for AMR. These include washing raw meat, which can spread bacteria to vegetables and increasing exposure to pathogens, as well as patients not following guidelines of physicians and stopping antibiotics once they start to feel better. She emphasized that clear communication between healthcare providers and patients is essential to prevent misuse of antibiotics. Dr. Chan’s main recommendation was simple, printable government pamphlets in plain English and patients’ first languages to educate the public. These would introduce key organisms, how they spread and what infections they cause, as well as covering decolonization, cleaning, when to see a doctor and why certain precautions are in place. She mentioned that QR code or audio version could help to decrease risk of contamination. Because VGH has no central catalogue of patient materials, she suggested approaching wards in person and having nurses distribute pamphlets.

<br id="3f9d65dd82be80beb023c4a3b88ca378" />

- <a id="3f3d65dd82be806087a3ea14617cb0ab" />Norma Washburn
- <a id="3f3d65dd82be803fb2c1f2d90b2118ab" />Norma is a patient we were able to connect with via the BCCDC; she had an AMR infection from a wound on her ankle that she was hospitalized for. She is still healing from home. We hoped to speak with her to learn from her experiences and co-design educational posts and videos with her.

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80dc8c8ed67bc8ff89ef" />

***

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80bc8e51fd5e4d7f0ffe" />Based on the feedback from Dr. Mroz, she became our AMR patient partner. Before the interview, Norma reviewed and signed an informed consent form. We also informed her that she could skip any question or withdraw at any time from the interview. We maintained her privacy by limiting her exposure on our instagram campaign. She shared with us going through multiple rounds of antibiotics for her wound infection without being given a clear explanation of what she was taking or why. She told us that when her second antibiotic began causing pain in her Achilles tendon, it was not her care team but her own GP who eventually connected the symptom to the medication. She also observed a difference in care quality that lined up with what Dr. Liu had described. Nurses at the hospital repeated the same wound dressing approach even as her infection worsened, while the specialists at her dedicated wound clinic adjusted their technique when something wasn't working. She said that without her experience as a nurse, she "would be really really scared," because "people have no time to explain to patients." Norma gave us feedback on the website, which we created for AMR patients to chat to each other to provide to patients a sense of community.
