<a id="3f3d65dd82be805f8639c0d6457ea7f9" />Our literature review gave us a starting point which revealed the scientific nature and clinical management of AMR. Although it was very informative and successfully familiarised our team with AMR, there was a significant gap of knowledge in what it is actually like for patients to live with AMR; what the psychological effects are, and how social factors can play a part in the experience as well. To close this gap, we turned to Integrated Human Practices. We spoke directly with clinicians, researchers, and a patient partner to understand how AMR affects people locally, and integrated the conversations to shape our project as it developed.

\<Sunny Loo is a patient representative at the BCCDC, and has lived with vasculitis (GPA) for the past 18 years. As a retired pharmacist with over 40 years of experience developing patient support programs, and as a patient who had experienced antibiotic use firsthand, he offered us a perspective that bridged both the clinical and lived-experience sides of patients.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be800082eedd58ad66e041" />For Sunny, the most lasting effect of his condition has been fear—of infection, of how often he might get sick, and of whether repeated antibiotic use could leave him facing infections that no longer respond to treatment. His illness also reshaped his life outside the clinic; he shared with us that his social and professional network narrowed down to his immediate family, he could no longer travel as he once had, and he had to ultimately change career paths.  Although he was eventually able to find a sense of community through volunteering in patient-oriented research, he mentioned that support was hard to find, as there was little information on GPA when he was diagnosed and few patients to connect with. He also emphasized that while healthcare providers tend to focus on the illness and its treatment, it does not take what burden patients carry day to day into account. He told us that patients living with chronic illness are often "just asking to be understood, to be supported." He also pointed to barriers that make care harder, such as long waits for specialists, family physicians who may not recognize rare conditions, and overwhelming amount of information online that makes it difficult to discern trustworthy advice. Sunny saw real value in point-of-care diagnostic tools that could help ensure patients receive the right treatment the first time, reducing the trial-and-error antibiotic use that contributes to resistance, especially for immunocompromised patients like himself.>\

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80bdb080dc7f727add28" />\<divider>\
\
\<Dr. Lester is an infectious disease physician, clinician-scientist, and associate professor at the UBC Division of Infectious Diseases located at Vancouver General Hospital and the University of British Columbia. He informed us that physician-patient interaction needs to be improved; he called for greater training of healthcare professionals in how to communicate to patients why a treatment plan was chosen and how antibiotics worked. Furthermore, he also suggested that we should create accessible resources to address common misconceptions, such as patients thinking themselves are resistant to antibiotics rather than the bacteria, thinking all bacteria are harmful, thus misusing antibiotics. Dr. Lester brought a very important scope into our view: the psychosocial impacts of AMR. He mentioned that AMR can give rise to anxiety and uncertainty, stigma, fear of transmitting infections to family/friends, feelings of personal responsibility for developing resistant infections, individuals facing long-term resistant infections (ex. resistant HIV or TB), and prolonged psychosocial burdens because of extended treatment and side effects. These are often not addressed due to the severity of illness as constant medical procedures must be carried out. Moreover, additional vulnerability from pre-existing mental illness, substance use disorders, unstable housing, or lower socioeconomic status can exacerbate the psychosocial tolls of AMR on patients. >\

\<Through conducting an interview with her, we learned that pamphlets would be helpful for patients, as they would like more information on their condition and their treatment plan. Lesley also raised patient isolation during hospitalisation as a significant concern. Due to the nature of AMR, contact with patients becomes limited, thus patients do not feel adequately supported, affecting their mental health and relationships with loved ones. These problems persist when the patient is discharged; family members or caretakers must take additional precautions, such as bleaching washrooms, to contain the spread of antimicrobial resistance. She told us that the best way to avoid all these barriers is to be careful to begin with.>\

\<Dr. Liu further expanded on the concept of patient isolation. He explained that patients are isolated and labelled in single rooms, as well as family members or healthcare providers who come to visit having to wear PPE and precautions. This leads to psychosocial distress for patients and as well as less assessment by the physician and reduced quality of care. Furthermore, he emphasized that doctors and nurses must be better educated on how to conduct patient interactions, such as debriefing the patient properly to reduce educational barriers and uncertainty for the patient. To clear up the stigma around AMR in general, he suggested explaining that different regions are naturally prone to different rates of transmission and managing consistent communication with the patient.>\

\<Our key takeaway from Dr. Chan was that pamphlets could be even more valuable in some ways compared to speaking directly to physicians. This was because nurses spent far more consistent time with patients than physicians did, who tended to rotate between patients and shifts. If nurses were able to hand patients pamphlets before their first consultation, patients could walk into that consultation already equipped with questions. We had originally considered delivering a speech directly to physicians, but as Dr. Chan pointed out, the reality was that many physicians already had their own established practices, which we had no ability to control. What we could control was the educational resources we gave directly to patients. Dr. Chan also highlighted that patients commonly experienced frustration around several recurring questions: How do you remove colonization? Why does it keep coming back? Will I pass it on to the people I love? She traced all of this back to a single root cause: a lack of education. This made it clearer to us that an accessible information pamphlet provided before their consultation was the best way forward, since it would let both patients and their families walk in already knowing what questions to ask. She further noted that having nurses distribute these materials made the most practical sense, since nurses were with patients consistently, while physicians were not. In conclusion, the alignment between what our patient (Norma), Lesley, and Dr. Chan all independently told us made it clear that building these pamphlets was the right direction to pursue.>\
%% END

- <a id="3f3d65dd82be803fb2c1f2d90b2118ab" />Norma is a patient we were able to connect with via the BCCDC; she had an AMR infection from a wound on her ankle that she was hospitalized for. She is still healing from home. We hoped to speak with her to learn from her experiences and co-design educational posts and videos with her.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80bc8e51fd5e4d7f0ffe" />\<divider>\
\
\<Based on the feedback from Dr. Mroz, she became our AMR patient partner. Before the interview, Norma reviewed and signed an informed consent form. We also informed her that she could skip any question or withdraw at any time from the interview. We maintained her privacy by limiting her exposure on our instagram campaign. She shared with us going through multiple rounds of antibiotics for her wound infection without being given a clear explanation of what she was taking or why. She told us that when her second antibiotic began causing pain in her Achilles tendon, it was not her care team but her own GP who eventually connected the symptom to the medication. She also observed a difference in care quality that lined up with what Dr. Liu had described. Nurses at the hospital repeated the same wound dressing approach even as her infection worsened, while the specialists at her dedicated wound clinic adjusted their technique when something wasn't working. She said that without her experience as a nurse, she "would be really really scared," because "people have no time to explain to patients." Norma gave us feedback on the website, which we created for AMR patients to chat to each other, instilling a sense of community.>\
