<a id="3f3d65dd82be805f8639c0d6457ea7f9" />Our literature review gave us a starting point which revealed the scientific nature and clinical management of AMR. Although it was very informative and successfully familiarised our team with AMR, there was a significant gap of knowledge in what it is actually like for patients to live with AMR; what the psychological effects are, and how social factors can play a part in the experience as well. To close this gap, we turned to Integrated Human Practices. We spoke directly with clinicians, researchers, and a patient partner to understand how AMR affects people locally, and integrated the conversations to shape our project as it developed.

<br id="3f3d65dd82be804cb689f0b14848556e" />

<a id="3ecd65dd82be80919425eab1ed9a08ef" />Dr. Hancock advised us that misconceptions are common regarding AMR. He explained that public understanding of AMR was limited because antibiotics still appeared to work most of the time, since doctors had backup treatment plans, and this masked the underlying resistance problem from patients. He argued AMR education should lead with the real-world consequences of losing antibiotics, rather than statistics alone, especially since antibiotics are foundational to modern medicine, including surgeries, transplants, neonatal care, and treatment of common infections. Furthermore, he noted that certain populations faced greater AMR risk, including women, who sometimes received prescriptions from a pharmacy without proper follow-up, and catheterized patients, who were often wheelchair-bound.
