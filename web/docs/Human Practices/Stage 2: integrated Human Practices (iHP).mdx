Dr. Hancock advised us that misconceptions are common regarding AMR. He explained that public understanding of AMR was limited because antibiotics still appeared to work most of the time, since doctors had backup treatment plans, and this masked the underlying resistance problem from patients. He argued AMR education should lead with the real-world consequences of losing antibiotics, rather than statistics alone, especially since antibiotics are foundational to modern medicine, including surgeries, transplants, neonatal care, and treatment of common infections. Furthermore, he noted that certain populations faced greater AMR risk, including women, who sometimes received prescriptions from a pharmacy without proper follow-up, and catheterized patients, who were often wheelchair-bound.
Sunny Loo was a patient representative at the BCCDC, and had lived with vasculitis for the past 12 years. As a pharmacist with over 40 years of experience developing patient support programs, and as a patient who had experienced antibiotic use firsthand, he offered us a perspective that bridged both the clinical and lived-experience sides of patients.

Dr. Lester is an infectious disease physician, clinician-scientist, and associate professor at the UBC Division of Infectious Diseases located at Vancouver General Hospital and the University of British Columbia. Through the interview, he informed us that physician-patient interaction needs to be improved; he called for greater training of healthcare professionals in how to communicate to patients why a treatment plan was chosen and how antibiotics worked. Furthermore, he also suggested that we should create accessible resources to address common misconceptions, such as patients thinking themselves are resistant to antibiotics rather than the bacteria, thinking all bacteria are harmful, thus misusing antibiotics. Dr. Lester brought a very important scope into our view: the psychosocial impacts of AMR. He mentioned that AMR can give rise to anxiety and uncertainty, stigma, fear of transmitting infections to family/friends, feelings of personal responsibility for developing resistant infections, individuals facing long-term resistant infections (ex. resistant HIV or TB), and prolonged psychosocial burdens because of extended treatment and side effects. These are often not addressed due to the severity of illness as constant medical procedures must be carried out. Moreover, additional vulnerability from pre-existing mental illness, substance use disorders, unstable housing, or lower socioeconomic status can exacerbate the psychosocial tolls of AMR on patients.

Dr. Larry Mroz works at Health Research BC in the BC Support Unit as a Patient and Community Engagement Lead. He advised researchers in their research projects as they navigate discussions with patients with unique lived experiences. We initially were hoping that we would speak to him about connecting us with patient partners, but our meeting with him ended up familiarizing us with a lot more. Larry suggested that we create a website that outlines our project; that we include on there what we are trying to do and how people can get involved. We can also use this website as a platform to share our learning while leveraging our own social media to get the outreach started. He kindly suggested for us to compose a one-pager outlining our project and he would send it to his network of patients willing to share their lived experiences. He also shared with us a variety of resources and articles on the difference between patient engagement and qualitative research. Moving forward, we decided to frame our project as he suggested and look for patient partners as well as starting up our own instagram outreach campaign while building a website that better projects our mission.

Lesley is a registered nurse working with Providence Health. Through conducting an interview with her, we learned that pamphlets would be helpful for patients, as they would like more information on their condition and their treatment plan. She suggested that we create two versions of pamphlets, where one is in braille to cater blind patients struggling with AMR, and the other as a digital version on the website with audio for accessibility. Lesley also raised patient isolation during hospitalisation as a significant concern. Due to the nature of AMR, contact with patients becomes limited, thus patients do not feel adequately supported, affecting their mental health and relationships with loved ones. These problems persist when the patient is discharged; family members or caretakers must take additional precautions, such as bleaching washrooms, to contain the spread of antimicrobial resistance. She told us that the best way to avoid all these barriers is to be careful to begin with.

Dr. Liu further expanded on the concept of patient isolation. He explained that patients are isolated and labelled in single rooms, as well as family members or healthcare providers who come to visit having to wear PPE and precautions. This leads to psychosocial distress for patients and as well as less assessment by the physician and reduced quality of care. Furthermore, he emphasised that doctors and nurses must be better educated on how to conduct patient interactions, such as debriefing the patient properly to reduce educational barriers and uncertainty for the patient. To clear up the stigma around AMR in general, he suggested explaining that different regions are naturally prone to different rates of transmission and managing consistent communication with the patient.

Our key takeaway from Dr. Chan was that pamphlets could be even more valuable in some ways compared to speaking directly to physicians. This was because nurses spent far more consistent time with patients than physicians did, who tended to rotate between patients and shifts. If nurses were able to hand patients pamphlets before their first consultation, patients could walk into that consultation already equipped with questions. We had originally considered delivering a speech directly to physicians, but as Dr. Chan pointed out, the reality was that many physicians already had their own established practices, which we had no ability to control. What we could control was the educational resources we gave directly to patients. Dr. Chan also highlighted that patients commonly experienced frustration around several recurring questions: How do you remove colonization? Why does it keep coming back? Will I pass it on to the people I love? She traced all of this back to a single root cause: a lack of education. This made it clearer to us that an accessible information pamphlet provided before their consultation was the best way forward, since it would let both patients and their families walk in already knowing what questions to ask. She further noted that having nurses distribute these materials made the most practical sense, since nurses were with patients consistently, while physicians were not. In conclusion, the alignment between what our patient (Norma), Lesley, and Dr. Chan all independently told us made it clear that building these pamphlets was the right direction to pursue.
