<a id="3f3d65dd82be805f8639c0d6457ea7f9" />Our literature review gave us a starting point which revealed the scientific nature and clinical management of AMR. Although it was very informative and successfully familiarized our team with AMR, there was a significant gap of knowledge in what it is actually like for patients to live with AMR, what the psychological effects are, and how social factors can play a part in the experience as well. To close this gap, we turned to integrated Human Practices. We spoke directly with clinicians, researchers, and a patient partner to understand how AMR affects people locally and integrated the conversations to shape our project as it developed.

        <div class="ihp-content">
          <a id="3f9d65dd82be8025a6d4db3316082f80" />Dr. Hancock advised us that misconceptions are common regarding AMR. He explained that public understanding of AMR was limited because antibiotics still appeared to work most of the time, since doctors had backup treatment plans, and this masked the underlying resistance problem from patients. He argued AMR education should lead with the real-world consequences of losing antibiotics, rather than statistics alone, especially since antibiotics are foundational to modern medicine, including surgeries, transplants, neonatal care, and treatment of common infections. Furthermore, he noted that certain populations faced greater AMR risk, including women, who sometimes received prescriptions from a pharmacy without proper follow-up, and catheterized patients, who were often wheelchair-bound.

        <div class="ihp-profile">
          <div class="ihp-desc">
            Dr. Edith Blondel-Hill is a Medical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases specialist with Interior Health. She is also a Clinical Professor in the Department of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine at the University of British Columbia. Dr. Blondel-Hill founded the Do Bugs Need Drugs program, and she continues to serve as its medical director. The program aims to integrate antibiotic education into primary health care as a routine part.

        <div class="ihp-content">
          <a id="3f3d65dd82be808d98b4c874b00efc0b" />Dr. Blondel-Hill highlighted aspects of patient experience in the realm of infection control. To begin with, she explained that screening and isolation processes can be very taxing on patients — invasive swabbing and the wait for results leads to distress. After screening, isolation can lead to further problems; she explained that patients may receive lower-quality care during isolation. Moreover, she told us that screening often takes a toll on family relationships, such as refusing to be in contact with their loved ones. Dr. Blondel-Hill also highlighted the stigma and problems in equity tied to screening: Risk factors of travelling or receiving healthcare in certain regions, such as Southeast Asia, Italy, Greece, and Mexico, can make patients feel targeted because of their ethnicity. A key challenge to overcome in solving this problem is expanding screening. She suggested including travel history or family contacts in the process without it being perceived as judgmental, especially when the questions are asked by non-clinical staff. Adding on, she described the gaps in the current screening system; it is unclear if high-risk patients are well-screened as well as standard methods unable to detect dangerous organisms like *Acinetobacter*. To strengthen public education, Dr. Blondel-Hill suggested leveraging trusted channels, such as the *Do Bugs Need Drugs?* Program hosted by BCCDC.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

          <div class="ihp-desc">
            Sunny Loo is a patient representative at the BCCDC, and has lived with vasculitis (GPA) for the past 18 years. As a retired pharmacist with over 40 years of experience developing patient support programs, and as a patient who had experienced antibiotic use firsthand, he offered us a perspective that bridged both the clinical and lived-experience sides of patients.
          </div>

        <div class="ihp-content">
          <a id="3f3d65dd82be800082eedd58ad66e041" />For Sunny, he shared with us the feeling of isolation and fear resulting from a vasculitis diagnosis when it was not very well known and with resources being scarce. He also emphasized that while healthcare providers focus on the illness and its treatment, it would make a world of difference for patients to be provided care that is coupled with empathy. Sunny told us that patients living with chronic illness are often "just asking to be understood, to be supported." He also pointed to barriers that make care harder, such as long waits for specialists, family physicians who may not recognize rare conditions, and overwhelming amounts of information online that makes it difficult to discern trustworthy advice. As for technical aspects, Sunny mentioned the value of point-of-care diagnostic tools that could help ensure patients receive the right treatment the first time to reduce the trial-and-error antibiotic use that contributes to resistance.

      <div class="ihp-card-body glow-panel-body">
        <div class="ihp-name">Dr. Richard Lester</div>

        <div class="ihp-profile">
          <div class="ihp-desc">
            Dr. Lester is an infectious disease physician, clinician-scientist, and associate professor at the UBC Division of Infectious Diseases located at Vancouver General Hospital and the University of British Columbia.
          </div>
        </div>

        <div class="ihp-content">
          <a id="3f9d65dd82be8086bb32f935a16f7d57" />Dr. Lester informed us that improvement of physician-patient interaction is crucial for tackling AMR. He called for greater training of healthcare professionals in how to communicate to patients: why a treatment plan was chosen, and how antibiotics work. Furthermore, he suggested that we should create accessible resources to address common misconceptions, such as patients thinking themselves are resistant to antibiotics rather than the bacteria or thinking all bacteria are harmful, thus leading to misuse of antibiotics. Dr. Lester also brought a very important scope into our view: the psychosocial impacts of AMR. He mentioned that AMR can give rise to anxiety and uncertainty, stigma, fear of transmitting infections to family/friends, feelings of personal responsibility for developing resistant infections, individuals facing long-term resistant infections (ex. resistant HIV or TB), and prolonged psychosocial burdens because of extended treatment and side effects. These psychosocial burdens are often overlooked during care, as constant medical procedures must be carried out due to the severity of the illness. Moreover, additional vulnerability from pre-existing mental illness, substance use disorders, unstable housing, or lower socioeconomic status can exacerbate the burden of AMR on patients.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="ihp-card glow-panel" data-index="2">
      <div class="ihp-card-body glow-panel-body">
        <div class="ihp-name">Lesley Kooner</div>

        <div class="ihp-profile">
          <div class="ihp-desc">
            Lesley is a registered nurse working with Providence Health. She has previous experience as a bedside nurse.
          </div>

        <div class="ihp-content">
          <a id="3f9d65dd82be80e8b8c1c8d8e803cff0" />Lesley raised patient isolation during hospitalization as a significant psychosocial concern regarding AMR. She explained that to avoid contracting drug-resistant pathogens, contact between healthcare providers and patients become limited; the required usage of PPE and stigma surrounding nurses acquiring an infection from their patient discourages staff from visiting patients frequently. As a result, patients do not feel adequately supported, which strongly affects their mental health and in turn relationships with loved ones as well. These challenges are carried into the household when the patient is discharged; family members or caretakers must take additional precautions, such as bleaching washrooms, to contain the spread of antimicrobial resistance. Lesley told us that the best way to avoid all these barriers is to be careful to begin with, so that the chances of contracting pathogens are decreased. It was noted that education regarding hygiene, personal care, and the proper usage of antibiotics can help to reduce the prevalence of AMR. We learned that pamphlets would be the best way to deliver education to patients, as Lesley described to us.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="ihp-card glow-panel" data-index="3">
      <div class="ihp-card-body glow-panel-body">
        <div class="ihp-name">Dr. Anthony Lieu</div>

        <div class="ihp-profile">
          <div class="ihp-desc">
            Dr. Anthony Lieu is a dual microbiologist and infectious disease physician from VGH and actively conducts research on transplant infectious diseases and molecular diagnostics.
          </div>
        </div>

        <div class="ihp-content">
          <a id="3fad65dd82be80ccb576f62e1cc445cb" />Dr. Lieu further enlightened us on the psychosocial burdens of AMR. He described how patients with resistant organisms are labelled and isolated in single rooms and visiting family members are required to wear PPE, creating a hostile environment. He noted that isolation may also lead to fewer assessments by physicians and lower quality of care. Moreover, he explained that resistant organisms are more common in people who have travelled to or come from certain regions, particularly lower-income countries, which can add stigma for patients who may already feel marginalized by the healthcare system. Dr. Lieu emphasized that he treats all patients the same regardless of their isolation status, and reassures them that isolation is meant to protect others, as their main concern is often passing the infection to their family.

          <a id="3fad65dd82be80f3ae70fda9440ba656" />He also identified common misconceptions among patients, such as believing that a “superbug” alludes to absolutely no treatment available. Moreover, he noted that patients are often unaware that they may remain colonized even after the infection clears.

          <a id="3fad65dd82be8033ba3ff21651486fbd" />Dr. Lieu elaborated on the social factors affecting AMR care as well. He explained that patients with language barriers often hesitate to raise concerns in busy healthcare settings. As a solution, he suggested an increase in nurse-to-patient ratios to account for these barriers. Since at-risk patients are less likely to use social media, and online information is often unfiltered and inaccurate, he suggested that education should be personalized and given directly to patients upon admission. He mentioned that care teams are shifting towards oral antibiotics, which evidence suggests work just as well as IVs and further accessible. Moreover, long-acting single doses are used for patients who may not return for follow-up to reduce risks of AMR. Dr. Lieu also highlighted that AMR is a global issue. Though difficult to implement, he suggested that tackling AMR requires coordination across institutions, agriculture and veterinary medicine. Lastly, he emphasized that both nurses and doctors need to be well-educated on AMR, as provider fatigue when caring for long-stay patients can lead to lapses in PPE use thus increase in transmission.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <div class="ihp-card glow-panel" data-index="4">
      <div class="ihp-card-body glow-panel-body">
        <div class="ihp-name">Dr. Chan</div>

        <div class="ihp-profile">
          <div class="ihp-desc">
            Dr. Chan is an infectious disease physician.
          </div>
        </div>

        <div class="ihp-content">
          <a id="3fad65dd82be80ae9cfff399d577ad69" />Firstly, Dr. Chan shared with us some psychosocial challenges she heard from her patients. She described how patients with AMR often return with the same infection and are frustrated as they do not know why it recurs or how to clear it. One case highlighted how AMR can affect family relationships; a patient’s wife avoided sleeping in the same bed for fear of catching MRSA, which the patient was receiving recurrent treatment for. She mentioned that family members in the household often are unaware of safety measures to protect themselves, such as proper usage of PPE or environmental cleaning, which are especially important in crowded homes. She also identified an important system gap that contribute to the frustration of patients: there is no clear protocol for removing an MRSA (or AMR?) label from a patient’s record. This may lead to quality of care being disrupted for patients under contact precautions, as doctors find gowning (usage of PPE) to be time-consuming. Dr. Chan elaborated on the socioeconomic factors affecting AMR as well. She explained that financial issues can pressure many people to live in one house, allowing infections to spread more easily. Moreover, people from lower-income households may rely on cheaper meat raised with antibiotics, which increase their exposure. Dr. Chan highlighted common practices that increase risk for AMR. These include washing raw meat, which can spread bacteria to vegetables and increasing exposure to pathogens, as well as patients not following guidelines of physicians and stopping antibiotics once they start to feel better. She emphasized that clear communication between healthcare providers and patients is essential to prevent misuse of antibiotics. Dr. Chan’s main recommendation was simple, printable government pamphlets in plain English and patients’ first languages to educate the public. These would introduce key organisms, how they spread and what infections they cause, as well as covering decolonization, cleaning, when to see a doctor and why certain precautions are in place. She mentioned that QR code or audio version could help to decrease risk of contamination. Because VGH has no central catalogue of patient materials, she suggested approaching wards in person and having nurses distribute pamphlets.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

She told us that when her second antibiotic began causing pain in her Achilles tendon, it was not uncalled for and she had not been warned by her healthcare practitioners of potential side effects of her medications. She also highlighted the lack of adequate briefing before invasive procedures, where without her experience as a nurse, she "would be really really scared” about some of the procedures used during her hospital stay. This is consistent with information Dr. Blondel-Hill shared with us, where patients can feel invaded if not properly debriefed on AMR testing procedures. Norma also gave us feedback on the website, which we created for AMR patients to chat to each other to provide to patients a sense of community.
