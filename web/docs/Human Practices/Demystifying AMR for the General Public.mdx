<a id="3ddd65dd82be8037b447f59837cec2dd" />Education became an important extension of our work. As we explored the social dimensions of AMR through our literature review and iHPs, we began thinking more broadly about how education can contribute to greater awareness and understanding of the issue.

<a id="3ddd65dd82be8064bb7eef597b2e5bdf" />Our literature review helped us identify adolescents as an important audience for AMR education. Research showed that misinformation surrounding antimicrobial mechanisms was concentrated among young adults of ages 18-24 as well as those with a high school degree or less (Crago et al, 2022). These findings shaped an important direction for this branch of our work: building an understanding of AMR early in life. To explore this, we partnered with UBC Geering Up Engineering Outreach during their K-12 summer camps to introduce AMR in an accessible and engaging environment.

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80d69505cdf6bb45dde6" />However, we wanted our workshops to be more than just a presentation of information. We took an active learning approach, encouraging question asking and incorporating interactive activities. For example, during our Biomedical Engineering Jr workshop, students drew their own channel designs on paper and tested them with coloured water to simulate the mechanism of a microfluidic chip.

# <a id="3f5d65dd82be80d6937bc309ccb91c16" />Bioinformatics Workshop

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80a9afe9cd06a2a5bcdd" />In this workshop, students followed an infection from start to finish. We began with the basics of bacteria and how antibiotics like amoxicillin and azithromycin target specific bacterial structures, then introduced AMR and how bacteria gain resistance through random mutations and gene sharing. Students applied this in a mutation game, translating DNA sequences and sorting mutations into antibiotic-responsive or antibiotic-resistant categories, and toured the Hallam Lab to see how gel electrophoresis works.

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80f0a2c2e8412ecdbba4" />The centerpiece was a treatment plan activity set during a hospital outbreak. Each group cared for a patient facing a real-world barrier, such as cost, distance from a pharmacy, or a penicillin allergy, and had to choose an antibiotic and plan how the patient would complete treatment. In the debrief, students saw that AMR isn’t just a biology problem: it’s shaped by access, affordability, and who gets listened to in healthcare. We closed with a bioinformatics activity linking resistance genes (genotype) to how bacteria respond to antibiotics (phenotype).

# <a id="3f5d65dd82be80339ca3e429da7078d2" />Explorations in Engineering Jr Workshop

<a id="3f5d65dd82be8032a726cbf561717679" />This workshop showed students how engineers help tackle AMR. After the same introduction to bacteria, antibiotics, and resistance, students learned how bioreactors work and stepped into the role of bioengineers, adjusting temperature, pH, oxygen, and agitation speed to compete for the highest yield of antimicrobial peptides. We then introduced microfluidic chips, whose tiny channels let fluids mix quickly, making them useful for rapid antibiotic susceptibility testing. Students designed their own chips by drawing channels in crayon on paper and testing how well coloured water mixed, while our team gave feedback to help them improve their designs. We found the paper absorbed fluid poorly and leaked, which shaped how we ran the activity next time.

# <a id="3f5d65dd82be804a91f9d9515cd9a1ce" />STEM Leadership Program Workshop

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80dda77bc7b678a022d3" />On July 29, we ran a workshop for Geering Up’s STEM Leadership Program, a year-long initiative that trains high school students to become STEM ambassadors. Building on the Bioinformatics workshop format, students learned about bacteria, antibiotics, and AMR, toured the Hallam Lab, and tackled the mutation game and treatment plan as one team challenge, dividing roles and reporting their findings together. We closed with a hands-on introduction to SnapGene, showing how scientists use bioinformatics tools to design and visualize plasmids before heading into the lab.

# <a id="3f5d65dd82be80a197cdda5311c28337" />Building BioBricks Booth (with BMEG 591G)

<a id="3f5d65dd82be808881e0e74cc8a7b5d3" />At our booth with BMEG 591G, we introduced visitors to synthetic biology and how scientists design plasmids to give bacteria new functions. We explained the key parts of a gene circuit (promoter, ribosome binding site, gene of interest, and terminator) using the iGEM BioBrick idea of parts that snap together like Lego. Visitors then put this into practice with a hands-on challenge: using Lego bricks, they built a bacterial biosensor that glows red when microplastics are detected in drinking water, picking the right parts from a set of distractors and arranging them in the correct order.

# <a id="3d7d65dd82be80af98c2e03d01651ffd" />General Public

<a id="3f4d65dd82be8042ac19c9f87ff5a1c3" />As identified in our literature review and further corroborated by healthcare professionals in our iHP interviews, gaps in public understanding of AMR remain an important barrier to public education. Although many Canadians understand basic facts about antibiotics and resistance, misconceptions persist. These knowledge gaps have been particularly identified in some demographics, including young adults aged 18-24. Our literature review also highlighted how patients can have difficulty finding accessible, reliable information about AMR, contributing to feelings of uncertainty regarding their diagnoses and treatment.

<a id="3f4d65dd82be80a6bde7d029a2d7a563" />To address these educational needs, we developed an Instagram campaign, @amrexplained, to make AMR more approachable by translating complex scientific concepts into accessible and engaging educational resources. Drawing from our literature review and insights from our iHP interviews, we explored different ways to introduce AMR to the general public, from foundational science to the impacts of resistant infections.

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80e6958cd9e48dc45741" />Instagram Educational Posts:

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80729e1cd8b7569dc793" />Our Instagram carousel posts introduced key concepts related to AMR through a series of short, visually engaging slides. Topics included the definition of AMR and bacteria, common bacterial infections, and how AMR can affect different populations unequally. By breaking down these topics into manageable sections, we aimed to make scientific information easier to understand.

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80b4a8eafa35c7fe4c60" />**Short-Form Reels and Crash Courses:**

<a id="3f4d65dd82be80518515c22e2ffdc148" />In addition to static posts, we developed various short-form Instagram reels as an audiovisual format for explaining AMR, in hopes of reaching a wider audience, providing a “crash course”. The Human Practices team collaborated with the Design team to make engaging visual effects that complemented the narration and helped illustrate the concepts being discussed.

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80039415e2cf8c90486e" />Alongside introduce AMR-related concepts, our reels explored the psychosocial impacts of resistant infections on individuals. This focus came from an important aspect of our Human Practices work: understanding AMR as not only a clinical challenge, but also as an experience that can affect patients’ lives. By incorporating multiple perspectives into our public education materials, we aimed to encourage a broader understanding of the challenges associated with AMR.

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80fcb22ac24df5291b23" />**Documentary-Style Interview:**

<a id="3f4d65dd82be803d8582eff90b698f34" />While the Instagram campaign was aimed at providing concise introductions to AMR, we also wanted resources available for individuals seeking more in-depth information. Following our initial iHP interview with Dr. Richard Lester, an infectious disease physician with the Vancouver Coastal Health Institute and UBC, we produced a longer, sit-down interview about his clinical perspective on AMR.

<a id="3f5d65dd82be806a8b8cc1befafcacb0" />The interview allowed us to share insights from a healthcare professional directly with the general public, taking the information learned beyond our team. Topics included common public misconceptions about AMR and the importance of accessible information in improving patient understanding.

<a id="3f5d65dd82be80e4a613f3bca904e9dd" />Together, our posts, short-form reels, and long-form interview offered different ways for the public to engage with AMR education. The carousel posts introduced foundational concepts, the reels presented information in an engaging audiovisual format, and the interview provided an opportunity to hear a clinician’s perspective in greater depth. By using these complementary directions, we aimed to make AMR information more accessible  while addressing both the scientific concepts underlying resistance and the experiences of people affected by it.
