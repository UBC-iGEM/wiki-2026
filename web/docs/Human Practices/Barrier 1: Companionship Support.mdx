## <a id="3d7d65dd82be804e9d24ee1d248ecfca" />The Website [AMR — the human side](https://beyondthediagnosis.netlify.app/)

<a id="3ddd65dd82be80f78d43ebfadca7ccbc" />What became clear to be a need for patients through our discussions was a sense of community, and the actions we took to help bring that about became not about just making a website, but rather addressing the need for a community while in isolation. Speaking to others with similar experiences and exchanging evidence-based knowledge about AMR as guided by information we have amalgamated through discussions with professionals was the primary goal of the website, given that isolation is a secondary barrier to patients obtaining adequate informational support about AMR. Every section of this website traces backs to a voice: That of a healthcare provider or patient. We heard them, we felt the gap and we tried our best to fill it.

<a id="3ded65dd82be80e397bdd33e3c3e78ad" />The first voice, Dr. Richard Lester, emphasized that patients who understand their diagnosis and treatment are more likely to participate in their own care, which improves their overall outcome, and that patients often carry misconceptions: Believing they themselves become "immune" to antibiotics instead of the bacteria, thinking all bacteria are bad, or misusing antibiotics as a result. Thus, developing accessible education materials, in multiple languages, can improve health literacy. He also told us that alongside the clinical management of AMR, patients may experience anxiety, stigma, and fear of transmitting their infection to family or friends. Some feel personally responsible for developing a resistant infection, while others become vulnerable to pre-existing mental illness, substance use, unstable housing, or lower socioeconomic status. But knowing that other people have lived with AMR and managed it helps. Educational resources, for patients and the general public alike, can be part of that. So we decided to gather credible knowledge and lived stories from patients and translate them into different languages that we can share on our web platform, and that is what we did.

<a id="3ded65dd82be80b4a029efab34e263e6" />The second voice, Sunny Loo, a vasculitis patient, told us: Further improving access to trustworthy, patient-centered health resources/information is a valuable step towards being inclusive to unique experiences and bringing them back into the community while in isolation. Patients have more information available to them than ever, especially through AI, but no reliable way to tell which of it actually applies to their own condition. Sunny also spoke about the fear, anxiety, uncertainty, depression, and sense of being "infected" that can come with chronic illness, especially for immunocompromised patients navigating infection risk and AMR, and about expanding psychosocial support beyond one-size-fits-all support groups, since patients at different stages or severities of a disease can find the same discussion overwhelming rather than helpful. Sunny put it simply: "I want others to understand how I feel and stop long enough to allow me to share how I feel, instead of going straight into…treatment." From this, we understood that our educational content needed the patient's own perspective built in a narrative of people living with AMR, in their own words, not our assumptions or the literature's.

<a id="3ded65dd82be80db8b44d282fd561066" />The third voice, Dr. Larry Mròz, told us directly: to be accessible to whoever has the condition, make a website that outlines the project. He kindly offered to compose a one-pager with us and send it to his own network of patients willing to share their lived experience, and that's why we also built out the Mission and Team pages.

<a id="3ded65dd82be80a28c9ee6941ae617fd" />Putting these together, we understood there's much more to living with AMR than the clinical picture. Trying to put ourselves in the shoes of someone who just received the diagnosis, we realized we needed to build them a safe place, somewhere they could share their experience, somewhere that tells the story in their own voice rather than ours, somewhere people could actually live the experience rather than just read about it. . And, because Dr. Lester told us healthcare providers sometimes simply forget to share enough information with a patient — and that gap causes real stress — we created a tab for patients that they can use for their consults: A set of questions that they might want to ask if unsure where to start.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80b9a711fa52936eb18a" />**Methodology**

<a id="3ded65dd82be805b9e28d54f0ede9e6e" />Rather than defaulting to a generic AMR-awareness site, each section of the site was scoped to one of these evidenced needs:

<a id="3ded65dd82be80b480dac076b4fc5e2e" />

| <a id="3ded65dd82be80b59acdfac07fb0d49d" />Section          | Evidenced need it addresses                                                                                                             | the voice                  |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| <a id="3ded65dd82be804c9c11c8206e247327" />Home (narrative) | Corrects the resistance misconception early; documents the emotional and social reality of living with AMR, in patients' own words      | Norma Washburn & Sunny Loo |
| <a id="3ded65dd82be80df8449ccee5cd2c402" />Visit-Prep tool  | A structured, client-side visit-prep record patients can bring to appointments, a direct countermeasure to the dismissal pattern.       | Dr. Richard Lester         |
| <a id="3ded65dd82be80ef9bd3fcd2dc4823e2" />Learn            | Provider and literature-sourced descriptions of mechanisms, precautions, and psychosocial burden                                        | Dr. Richard Lester         |
| <a id="3ded65dd82be805f92f5f15ac315c9fc" />Stories          | Curated, attributed patient testimonies which reduce isolation without requiring a live public forum                                    | Norma Washburn & Sunny Loo |
| <a id="3ded65dd82be80cb8793cc938eecbea3" />Community        | A moderated peer space, responding directly to Norma and Sunny both independently telling us no such patient community currently exists | Norma Washburn & Sunny Loo |
| <a id="3ded65dd82be8081919ac501d15210f8" />Team / Mission   | States who is asking these questions and why, and what the three-stage IHP process (literature → interviews → action) actually was.     | Dr. Larry Mròz             |

1. <a id="3ded65dd82be800fb2ebc3949e757bfd" />**Evidence gathering:**\
   Physicians, nurses, patients, and organizations were contacted. Interviews were carried out with Dr. Bob Hancock, Dr. Richard Lester, Sunny Loo (patient partner), Dr. Larry Mròz, Dr. Edith Blondel-Hill, Dr. Anthony Liu, Lesley Kooner, and Norma Washburn (patient partner), alongside a structured literature review. Creating the content of the website purely based on the iHP meetings and the literature review.
2. <a id="3f9d65dd82be805ebbdcd8da2febb434" />**Specification before implementation**. A written technical and content specification was produced and revised across multiple passes before any code was written. The hard constraints, such as no personal health data collected anywhere on the site, comments never auto-published as well as the exact architecture.
3. <a id="3f9d65dd82be809f9b1bdf14c5467490" />**Primary design**: AI-assisted coding for a demo front end, using Html, CSS, Java Script and React. Several iterations on how exactly we imagined the website before building it.
4. <a id="3f9d65dd82be8074842ced213edbc28e" />**Actual development** : AI-assisted coding of different parts of the website with languages TypeScript, JavaScript, CSS and HTML. project version managed by using Git.
5. <a id="3fdd65dd82be806f9431d3f065273103" />**Privacy By-design Implementation** :\
   Most importantly the privacy for our website, is by design , meaning no personal information is asked or stored, no tracking the activity or third party embedded. For the Visit-prep section, the document created is not stored anywhere in our data base, it’s fully on the user’s browser. the community forum doesn’t need any accounts, each person is given a made-up username, agrees to our privacy guideline and is warned about sharing any personal information upon any story sharing. more specifically  we used the obscenity library, a  robust, zero-dependency TypeScript and Node.js publicly available library that uses a transformer-based architecture specifically to filter hate speach, offensive terms including Swering and insult, slurs wether racially, ethnitically or anti LGBTQ, Sexual words and body parts, in addition we added a list of words we came up with such as "kill yourself", "leftover antibiotics" or "stop taking your antibiotics”, It checks for them,  if any of them comes up, it is flagged, temporarily removen from the community forum and then checked by the team to permnantly being removed . for control and removal authors can permanently delete their own posts instantly, from the browser they posted from and if anyone lost that browser, they can report that the comment is theirs and they wanted removed. In total the whole website is desinged in a way to collect as little infromation as possible.
6. <a id="3f9d65dd82be80d6a236ed0a7fe0fff4" />**Validation before wider release**. Before the site reached patients broadly, it was reviewed first by Dr. Mróz,  the person who had proposed building it in the first place, and only after his feedback was incorporated was it shown to an actual AMR patient, Norma Washburn. Detail on both in the DBTL section below.
7. <a id="3f9d65dd82be80a4a88ec2b7b02265b2" />**Deployment: I**n-canada resident data base due to privacy policies and deployment on Netlify.
8. <a id="3fdd65dd82be80eda2d5fa074a7e386a" />**Inclusivity changes** : To make the website accessible to more people, we combined machine translation with review by native speakers to offer the content in French, Punjabi, Farsi, and Mandarin. We also reviewed the website’s colour choices to make the content easier to read for people with color blindness.

# <a id="3fdd65dd82be80708346ea99415f93f8" />First DBTL with Dr.Mròz

<a id="3ddd65dd82be8015b4b4ff17a8aeee98" />

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="3ddd65dd82be80de8179cce9534d389f" />On July 23rd, ahead of any patient outreach, the team consulted Dr. Mròz. His recommendations shaped the entire approach: frame outreach as co-designing with patient partners rather than interviewing subjects; given the project has no ethics approval, frame it explicitly as a patient engagement project rather than qualitative research; collect no physical or medical patient data under any circumstance; and the existing approach used with Sunny Loo, open-ended questions about feelings and resource needs, without presupposing the answer, was validated as sound.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="build">
    <a id="3ddd65dd82be801e8fffd98903ffa8fe" />Following that guidance, the team built a one-pager describing the patient engagement mission (sent to Dr. Mròz's own network of patients), an Instagram outreach campaign, and a first version of the website,  a narrative homepage paired with an anonymous interactive tab for direct patient engagement. The one-pager made the ethical framing concrete: consent forms and topics shared in advance, an explicit statement that no medical or physical information would be collected.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="test">
    <a id="3ddd65dd82be80298ff5c19eac19e737" />Once that first version was live, team member Zahra Savoji sent it back to Dr. Mròz for review, flagging a specific concern: that the tone of the psychosocial-challenges content could cause a patient reading about experiences they were actively living through to feel exposed or vulnerable rather than supported.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="learn">
    <a id="3ddd65dd82be80248393e8f17a9e9288" />Dr. Mròz's response validated the approach,  "very well done... don't see any obvious areas of misinterpretation",  while surfacing a real gap: the site wasn't clear enough about what would actually happen to the information patients provided. That specific feedback established the requirement, carried into every later version of the site, that the Community section state plainly what happens to anything submitted. He gave the go-ahead to launch and asked to be kept posted as the forums developed.
  </Fragment>
</Dbtl>

# <a id="3fdd65dd82be80ffbd84de9c366ea66b" />Second DBTL With Dr.Mròz

<a id="3fdd65dd82be8072b9dfd83d73734f0f" />

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80048045de58c5ba894d" />Following Dr.Mròz initial interview, we needed to Understand what would be the perspective of him about what was only an idea before. We wanted to have an overall overview of the website specially getting answers to our specific questions :  who would be the primary audience for our website?  How usefull the idea of having a community foroum for sharing patients and care givers ? how useful our visit-prep tool is in real clinical setting?
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="build">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be803aa039e685354d1988" />We walked Dr.Mròz through our website for a second review, the narrative on home page from the openning to the resources guide at the end, and from there to the existing version of visit-prep, learn and community pages. at the time, the printible visit-prep, and community forum was built but the privacy consideration, specifically the library for filtering hate speach. thus one of the important parts of this review was making the boundary between what is considered as the border between sharable information and comments that needs to be deleted or removed.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="test">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80a596e0d3c893b47e2a" />Dr. Mròz identified audience clarity as a priority. The homepage needed to explicitly address patients, their families, and the general public, rather than expecting visitors to infer its relevance.  He suggested to write why should someone read the narrative, making moving between different parts of the website very intuitive with a short explanation about how to use the website. Also make sure that after finishing the narrative there is different resources easily accessible at the end. \
    He described the community and the idea of sharing story and experience by patients wonderful. However, he mentioned issues  that needs to be addressed : not being clear about who is moderating the website, the disclosure policy and term of use. \
    at the end He reminded to go over it with a patient since they are the actual audience for this website.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="learn">
    <a id="3f3d65dd82be809990c3f3ded3844232" />We learned that the website needed to make both its reader experience and its  terms of use and  privacy considerations explicit. A visitor should not have to work out who the website serves, whether they have completed the narrative, or what happens to information they submit. So we added the guideline for using the community forum, a clear warning to not share any personal information as it will be public, adding a team tab introducing the team  that moderates the website and adding the resources easily accessible just after the narrative ends. This produced an intermediate version for further review, rather than a finished response to every recommendation. We carried the remaining questions about audience clarity, the visitor journey, and responsible sharing into the next discussion, allowing patient feedback to inform the changes still to be made.
  </Fragment>
</Dbtl>

# <a id="3fdd65dd82be80519b3ffeab8bca37c1" />DBTL with sunny loo (patient)

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80a6806de72f5d6ffeb7" />

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80d08c47f7956c5b46ca" />After  incorporating Dr. Mròz’s feedback, we wanted to understand whether the revised website worked from a patient’s perspective. Our focus was on the patient experience while using our website. More explicitly wether the visuals and reading are easy to follow, well representative of the patients, if they are written in a respectful manner, and do they include what the patients really want  everyone to know and  communicated the intended emotions clearly. Most importantly, How the privacy lines should be set, wether should we add the ability to have profiles and direct messages for private chats between users or should we keep in the way it is using a given username and without storing any personal information.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="build">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80009f92e071eb963d6b" />The version shown to Sunny contained the partial revisions made after Dr. Mròz’s second interview. It retained the moving narrative and an available still version, allowing us to discuss two ways of presenting the same experiences. The community section was having the recommended guidlines and warnings as well as a glossary for general public for respectfull communication.  At this point we had  added the tool  for reviewing the comments and filtering comments to the website. Thus sunny reviewed a intermediate version of the website while the decisions about the style, privacy and outreach to patients were on the table.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="test">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be8093888bcc339f9f1736" />Sunny ‌was ‌positive ‌about the graphics, the neutral color  combination, and  the way the narrative showed feelings such as fear and isolation. But he kept coming back to one question: how are we making this website inclusive?  He gave examples on how designing the website for older people or for people with vision problems required specific decisions to be made on font size, color contrast and readability.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80a898aacacc7cee9088" />He suggested to increase the font size, adding  words to the images, and to decrease the panning speed and movements to let the audience focus more on the story without distraction. He asked us to elaborate more on what the “still version” was and to make sure the still images were clear and represented what the story was about. Although he liked the colors, he encouraged us to consider how they would look to someone with color blindness.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80e3bccaeca0431b5501" />For the community forum, Sunny suggested to make it small for a learning experience. To focus more on the sharing of experiences rather than contact exchange or direct connections and learn from how people use it. He also suggested to have an acknowledgement so that contributors will have to actively confirm they understand what's happening before going any further. Since  Sharing experiences could be the starting point; helping people exchange contact details or connect directly would bring responsibilities we needed to understand before going further. He advised seeking legal and regulatory guidance before expanding those features. He also wanted people to actively acknowledge that they understood what sharing involved, rather than simply being shown a privacy notice.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="learn">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be808e8d25dd14d016caf7" />Sunny's ‌feedback ‌helped ‌us identify things we needed to change to improve the website's user interface and user experience.\
    \
    We've made the text bigger and reduced  distracting quick  movements so people can focus on the story. We've ensured that the images depict the experiences they are describing and provided an explanation that the still version of the story is there for those who don't want to experience the 3D narrative and animations. We've added more context at the beginning of the site so users know who the site is for and what to expect. And at the end of the site, we've made it easier to access support resources, including the visit preparation tool.\
    \
    For the community section, users can post under a made-up username without creating a full user profile. Before posting, they are informed that what they post will be public and are required to acknowledge the terms before posting. We've added information about community guidelines and how moderation works. Following Sunny's feedback, we've kept the community centered around sharing experiences so we can learn more about how users the space in the future before adding more features for community interaction.\
    \
    We applied what we learned from both conversations when we made these changes. Now we'll listen to the users of the revamped website. Did the changes to the story make it easier to follow? Do users understand the implications of sharing something?
  </Fragment>
</Dbtl>
