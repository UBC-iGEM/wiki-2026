<a id="3ddd65dd82be80f78d43ebfadca7ccbc" />What we came clear to be a need for patients through our discussions was a sense of community, and the actions we took to help bring that about became not about just making a website, but rather addressing the need for a community while in isolation. Speaking to others with similar experiences and exchanging evidence-based knowledge about AMR as guided by information we have amalgamated through discussions with professional. every section of this website traces backs to a voice: That of a healthcare provider or patient. We heard them, we felt the gap and we tried our best to fill it.

<a id="3ded65dd82be80e397bdd33e3c3e78ad" />The first voice, Dr. Richard Lester, emphasized that patients who understand their diagnosis and treatment are more likely to participate in their own care, which improves their overall outcome, and that patients often carry misconceptions: Believing they themselves become "immune" to antibiotics instead of the bacteria, thinking all bacteria are bad, or misusing antibiotics as a result. Thus, developing accessible education materials, in multiple languages, can improve health literacy. He also told us that alongside the clinical management of AMR, patients may experience anxiety, stigma, and fear of transmitting their infection to family or friends. Some feel personally responsible for developing a resistant infection, while others face added vulnerability from pre-existing mental illness, substance use, unstable housing, or lower socioeconomic status, and that knowing other people have lived with AMR and managed it genuinely helps. Educational resources, for patients and the general public alike, can be part of that. So we decided to gather credible knowledge and lived stories from patients and translate them into different languages that we can share on our web platform, though we are still figuring out how best to present it.

<a id="3ded65dd82be80b4a029efab34e263e6" />The second voice, Sunny Loo, a vasculitis patient, told us: Further improving access to trustworthy, patient-centered health resources/information is a valuable step towards being inclusive to unique experiences and bringing them back into the community while in isolation. Patients have more information available to them than ever, especially through AI, but no reliable way to tell which of it actually applies to their own condition. Sunny also spoke about the fear, anxiety, uncertainty, depression, and sense of being "infected" that can come with chronic illness, especially for immunocompromised patients navigating infection risk and AMR, and about expanding psychosocial support beyond one-size-fits-all support groups, since patients at different stages or severities of a disease can find the same discussion overwhelming rather than helpful. Sunny put it simply: "I want others to understand how I feel and stop long enough to allow me to share how I feel, instead of going straight into…treatment." From this, we understood that our educational content needed the patient's own perspective built in a narrative of people living with AMR, in their own words, not our assumptions or the literature's.

<a id="3ded65dd82be80db8b44d282fd561066" />The third voice, Dr. Larry Mròz, told us directly: to be accessible to whoever has the condition, make a website that outlines the project. He kindly offered to compose a one-pager with us and send it to his own network of patients willing to share their lived experience, and that's why we also built out the Mission and Team pages.

<a id="3ded65dd82be80a28c9ee6941ae617fd" />Putting these together, we understood there's much more to living with AMR than the clinical picture. Trying to put ourselves in the shoes of someone who just received the diagnosis, we realized we needed to build them a safe place, somewhere they could share their experience, somewhere that tells the story in their own voice rather than ours, somewhere people could actually live the experience rather than just read about it. A place to learn about the psychosocial side of AMR, not only the clinical one. And, because Dr. Lester told us healthcare providers sometimes simply forget to share enough information with a patient — and that gap causes real stress — a tool for appointments: A set of guided questions patients can bring with them, and add to as they need.

<a id="3ded65dd82be80abb4ecc454784ff851" />## Methodology

<a id="3ddd65dd82be8015b4b4ff17a8aeee98" />%% START DBTL

<a id="3ddd65dd82be80de8179cce9534d389f" />On July 23rd, ahead of any patient outreach, the team consulted Dr. Mròz. His recommendations shaped the entire approach: frame outreach as co-designing with patient partners rather than interviewing subjects; given the project has no ethics approval, frame it explicitly as a patient engagement project rather than qualitative research; collect no physical or medical patient data under any circumstance; and the existing approach used with Sunny Loo, open-ended questions about feelings and resource needs, without presupposing the answer, was validated as sound.

<a id="3ddd65dd82be80a09152c0d68d71312f" />

***

<a id="3ddd65dd82be801e8fffd98903ffa8fe" />Following that guidance, the team built a one-pager describing the patient engagement mission (sent to Dr. Mròz's own network of patients), an Instagram outreach campaign, and a first version of the website,  a narrative homepage paired with an anonymous interactive tab for direct patient engagement. The one-pager made the ethical framing concrete: consent forms and topics shared in advance, an explicit statement that no medical or physical information would be collected.

<a id="3ddd65dd82be80909cf5c387d9bf3bf9" />

***

<a id="3ddd65dd82be80298ff5c19eac19e737" />Once that first version was live, team member Zahra Savoji sent it back to Dr. Mròz for review, flagging a specific concern: that the tone of the psychosocial-challenges content could cause a patient reading about experiences they were actively living through to feel exposed or vulnerable rather than supported.

<a id="3ddd65dd82be802c84b5d1e28f6a330f" />

***

<a id="3ddd65dd82be80248393e8f17a9e9288" />Dr. Mròz's response validated the approach,  "very well done... don't see any obvious areas of misinterpretation",  while surfacing a real gap: the site wasn't clear enough about what would actually happen to the information patients provided. That specific feedback established the requirement, carried into every later version of the site, that the Community section state plainly what happens to anything submitted. He gave the go-ahead to launch and asked to be kept posted as the forums developed.

### <a id="3f2d65dd82be809b9985d7ecc7b27db3" />DBTL: second review with Dr. Mròz

\
In our first round with Dr. Mròz, he told us to be clearer about what happens \
to the information people share. For this second review, three things were on the table: is it clear who the website is for, does a visitor know where to land \
and where the narrative ends, and what does he think of the share-an-experience \
tab.\
\---\
\
We brought him the version of the site with the narrative home page, which runs like a hallway and ends on a page leading to the practical resources, the visit-prep tool that patients can print, and the share-an-experience tab.\
\
\---\
\
The review followed a first-time visitor's path: land on the first page, go through the whole narrative, see where it ends, then move on to the other pages. Dr. Mròz gave his reaction to each of the three questions.\
\
\---\
\
\*\*What he liked\*\*\
\
\- Sharing experiences: "a wonderful idea"\
\- The hallway narrative, ending on a page that leads to resources: "a lovely idea"\
\- The printable visit-prep: "absolutely wonderful"\
\
\*\*What he raised, and what we changed\*\*\
\
\- \*\*Audience.\*\* He said the site should be clearer about who it is for, especially on the first page. The first page now says plainly that it is for patients, their family members and the general public.\
\- \*\*Before the narrative.\*\* We added a short opening block that says why you should read this, what will happen, and that the reader is not at any risk.\
\- \*\*Where the story ends.\*\* We made the ending of the narrative obvious, with a clear handoff to the other pages and the practical resources.\
\- \*\*The share-an-experience tab.\*\* He asked what the terms of use are, and what happens if someone comes on to cause trouble. We wrote terms of use and community guidelines (no personal information, no disrespect or hate speach). People post under made-up usernames, with a plain warning that anything posted is public. No profile or patient health information is stored on our end and there underlying libraries used that are used for detecting the hatspeach or specific words like sucide and killing that will alert the admins and flag and take out the comment for further investigation by the admins.\
\- \*\*Visit-prep tool.\*\* No changes. We kept the print option he called out.\
\
He also asked us to talk to Sunny and Norma before going further, which is why our next rounds were with them.\

### <a id="3f2d65dd82be8091a705c7d5e1117fa3" />DBTL: feedback loop with Sunny Loo

<br id="3f2d65dd82be80a8bb5dc0e3d9552782" />

<a id="3f3d65dd82be807da9dbf1f5582f5e56" />%% START DBTL\
\
&#x20;Dr. Mròz had asked us to talk to Sunny and Norma, as representatives of the patients this website is for, before going further. Our next round was with Sunny, whose interview in July shaped much of what we built. We asked for his reaction, as a patient, to the look of the site and a read through the content, with the narrative page and the community tab in particular.\
\
\---\
\
He saw the narrative page, with its graphics and movement, alongside the still-image version of it, and the community tab.\
\
\---\
\
Sunny went through the narrative page and the community tab. When he asked who the website is for, we said there is a theme for everyone living with a AMR health condition, and that we want the public and patients to see that other people have lived this. He pushed us to be more specific: if older people are part of the audience, the font and contrast have to work for people with visual difficulties.\
\
\---\
\
\*\*What worked\*\*\
\
\- The graphics, and the neutral colour palette with good contrast\
\- The story shows fear and isolation right away, and the message came through. "I like where you're heading."\
\
\*\*What he asked for, and what we changed\*\*\
\
\- \*\*Audience.\*\* We made it clear on the website itself.\
\- \*\*Readability.\*\* We made the text on the images larger and raised the base font size across the whole site. We checked the contrast and colours for colour blindness and low vision, and kept the palette.\
\- \*\*Movement.\*\* There was too much panning and zooming, and it pulled attention away from reading. We slowed it down.\
\- \*\*First image.\*\* It now shows what the story is about straight away.\
\- \*\*Still and animated versions.\*\* He wanted them to relate to each other so people don't get confused. We matched them and added a line explaining what the still version is.\
\
\*\*The community tab\*\*\
\
He told us not to worry about connecting people yet and to treat the tab as a small learning pilot. Sharing brings legal responsibility as well as privacy risk, so the less we release, the less we carry. He also said that even with anonymized information, people should click to agree before they proceed, and that we should talk to regulatory people before widening it.\
\
\- We are keeping the pilot small and not advertising it widely.\
\- People now accept a consent click-through before posting.\
\- No profile or patient health information is stored on our end. People are warned not to put personal information in comments, and anyone who still chooses to has to accept our terms first.\
\
