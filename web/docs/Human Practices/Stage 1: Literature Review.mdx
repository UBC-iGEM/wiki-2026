Scope:
After deciding on the project focus of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), our initial review of existing literature highlighted that AMR is a highly multifaceted issue that goes beyond the biological and clinical aspects, but can also be influenced by socioeconomic conditions, healthcare structures, access to healthcare, and more (Hinchliffe et al., 2018; Hamilton et al., 2024). Therefore, we determined that learning how to address AMR would require understanding the patient experience and the systems around them. To do this, we wanted to investigate key questions such as: What challenges do patients face? What psychosocial barriers do they experience? What does the general public understand about AMR? What information/support are patients receiving after their diagnosis?
Methods:
We began by searching across multiple databases and search engines like PubMed, Google Scholar, MEDLINE, and Embase, particularly looking for articles pertaining to the psychosocial impacts of AMR in patients as well as patients’ and the general public’s levels of understanding regarding AMR globally, in Canada, and specifically in British Columbia.
Disproportionate Populations:
Through this review, we identified a number of populations who experience burdens from AMR disproportionately, such as Indigenous populations, unhoused individuals, and patients in long-term rehabilitation facilities. Drivers of these burdens were largely structural, including inequitable access to primary care, under-resourced housing and sanitation infrastructure, and increased rates of exposure to healthcare environments, respectively. <span discussion-urls="discussion\://3d7d65dd-82be-804a-bcd2-faccce039b76/3ddd65dd-82be-802c-bfb9-e869fb6f8fd2/3ddd65dd-82be-808d-8172-001c64f4ed6e">(Hindmarch et al., 2024; Stefanovic et al., 2025; Grifoni et al., 2025</span>).
Knowledge Gaps:
Through the review, a key finding was the gaps in public knowledge, with many Canadians having some basic understanding of antibiotics and AMR, while some common misconceptions were brought up, for example, believing that antibiotics could treat viruses, colds, etc. Certain demographic groups showed greater knowledge gaps, particularly young adults between the ages of 18 and 24, those with a high school degree or less, and those from non-English-speaking households (Crago et al., 2022). More concerningly, we found evidence of patients with chronic AMR infections reporting that they did not understand their diagnosis, with some patients never having heard the term “antimicrobial resistance” even after years of treatment. This suggested that education was not only a public awareness issue but an important part of the patient experience post-diagnosis. Across these studies, we found that many patients struggled to find professional yet accessible information about AMR and that clinicians did not always ensure patients understood their diagnosis and treatment plans (Whittaker et al., 2023).
Psychosocial Burdens:
Beyond knowledge gaps, the literature described substantial psychological and social burdens among individuals living with AMR. Patients described uncertainty and anxiety regarding treatments and prognosis, alongside fear and helplessness (Hamilton et al., 2024). Alongside this, patients reported feeling judged, isolated, a sense of stigma from the public, as well as a lack of support (Whittaker et al., 2023; Hamilton et al., 2024). Whittaker et al. (2023) further described the absence of a recognized “AMR survivor” identity, in contrast to well-established survivorship communities as seen in conditions such as cancer, leaving patients without spaces for advocacy or peer support. Gaps in Literature:
One major gap found in our review was a lack of patient-oriented evidence examining the psychosocial impacts of AMR within Canada, and more specifically, BC. This left us with a limited understanding of what patients living with AMR may be experiencing in our own community and how different or similar this would be to the global literature from the perspective of the patient, practitioner, and healthcare system as a whole. Therefore, we aimed to seek firsthand perspectives from people involved in BC healthcare through our Integrated Human Practices (iHP).
