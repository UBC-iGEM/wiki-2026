go back to our initial goal and summarize what we’ve done
At the beginning of our project, we set out to look beyond the biological mechanisms of AMR and better understand understand the people living behind the stattisics. Our initial goal was to identify patient populations that may be underrepresented in conversations surrounding AMR, understand the medical, socioeconomic, and psychosocial barriers they experience, and explore how these experiences could inform more inclusive approaches to education, support, and care. Our literature review provided the foundation for this work, but it also revealed an important limitation: there was only so much we could understand about the experiences of patients in British Columbia through the existing literature alone. This led us to our iHPs, where we spoke with infectious disease physicians, nurses, researchers, and patients to place what patients may need alongside what is currently being provided and identify where gaps remain.
Most importantly, these conversations changed the direction of our project. Rather than designing solutions based solely on our own assumptions, we allowed the needs identified by the people closest to AMR to guide what we built. We had discussions with patients surrounding isolation, stigma, <span discussion-urls="discussion\://3d7d65dd-82be-8078-a43b-fd438b8af0ff/3ddd65dd-82be-80d6-a84d-ee872b2e853c/3dfd65dd-82be-8088-b5e7-001c3a09d6bc">and uncertainty, and with health care providers about misconceptions, communication, and access to information. Bridging the gap between literature and experience, and patient and provider, we strived to ex</span>plore how patients and their families need the knowledge and resources so that they can participate meaningfully in their care. The need for accessible patient education informed the development of resources designed to help patients and the public better understand AMR: a community engagement platform in the form of a website, pamphlets on the basics of AMR to be made available in hospitals, a documentary highlighting first-hand perspectives, and a social media campaign aimed at sharing reliable information in an eye-catching, easily digestible fashion. Furthermore, through our Geering Up workshops, we worked to make AMR understandable and relevant to a younger audience using accessible and interactive approaches.
What began as an effort to better understand the experiences surrounding AMR ultimately became an iterative process of listening and learning. Each stage informed the next: our literature review identified gaps that shaped the direction of our iHPs, our iHPs challenged and refined our assumptions, and those conversations directly informed and revised the educational resources we produced. Our project does not claim to resolve the complex issues surrounding AMR, but we do hope it highlights the importance of asking which perspectives may be missing from dialogues about AMR and what opportunities we can create to ensure these perspectives can shape our responses moving forward. We aimed to make our work not only about the people directly affected by AMR, but increasingly guided by them and their experiences.
