# <a id="3fed65dd82be80e99a46dc2ea61c5662" />Overview

<a id="3f3d65dd82be8081a965d9dd6472daca" />Our project aims to provide a simpler, faster, and more modular way to determine antibiotic resistance in a patient bacterial infection sample. We have engineered biosensor *E. coli strains* that detect the presence of antibiotics by linking antibiotic-induced stress responses to a fluorescent readout. This was achieved by constructing a reporter plasmid system using the fast-maturing superfolder green fluorescent protein (sfGFP), inducible by two stress-responsive promoters.

# <a id="3fed65dd82be80b88d31cb73021dfacb" />Antibiotic Selection

<a id="3f3d65dd82be804bacd6dbe5e0c527a9" />In the clinic, antibiotic treatments are selected based on whether the infection-causing bacterium is susceptible to it. Clinically, antibiotic susceptibility or resistance is determined using the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), which is the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent capable of inhibiting bacterial growth <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"kowalska-krochmalMinimumInhibitoryConcentration2021"}]} />. These laboratory-determined values are compared with a clinical breakpoint to determine whether standard antibiotic dosing can achieve sufficient drug concentrations at the site of infection to inhibit bacterial growth.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80918451c4e205cf2b88" />To develop our biosensor *E. coli* strains, we extensively reviewed literature to explore several antibiotic classes effective against gram-negative bacteria. This included β-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, glycopeptides, oxazolidinones, polymyxins, sulfonamides, and tetracyclines. These classes interfere with different aspects of bacterial physiology. For example, β-lactams disrupt cell wall synthesis, aminoglycosides and macrolides interfere with protein translation processes, fluoroquinolones target enzymes involved in DNA replication, and polymyxins disrupt the bacterial envelope <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"kohanskiHowAntibioticsKill2010"}]} />. These differences provided a starting point for identifying stress-responsive promoters that could be used to detect antibiotic activity.

<a id="3fed65dd82be8072a191e3330c8f0af4" />An additional constraint on antibiotic selection was imposed by the limitations of our final FAST application. We were required to select antibiotics which primarily were affected by enzymatic degradation-based or chemical modification-based resistance mechanisms rather than efflux pump resistance mechanisms. This is because our proposed microfluidic chip assay relies on the physical inactivation of antibiotics in order to distinguish a positive and negative readout for patient sample susceptibility.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80789b92cdda805fc497" />Discussions with the Hancock Lab helped us narrow down our focus to antibiotic classes effective against urinary tract infections (UTIs). As such, we decided to focus on the detection of β-lactams and aminoglycosides. We selected these antibiotic classes for their clinical relevance to UTIs. Additionally, their antibiotic resistance mechanisms of enzymatic degradation in β-lactams or chemical modification in aminoglycosides were compatible with the requirements of the FAST application.

# <a id="3f3d65dd82be8018ad86ff57d648cac9" />**Clinical Relevance to Urinary Tract Infections**

<a id="3f3d65dd82be805a8b5be365a4fe4c77" />β-lactams and aminoglycosides are complementary in terms of their clinical use. β-lactams are widely used in bacterial infection treatment but resistance by β-lactamases in many pathogens can compromise their activity. Aminoglycosides act on a different target and can remain useful against some β-lactam-resistant organisms depending on their susceptibility profile. Their role is particularly relevant in selected complicated or resistant UTIs, although toxicity limits their usage. Resistance to one class does not establish susceptibility to the other, making antibiotic-specific testing essential.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80d691e2cdaa82165c96" />For our application validation, we selected the most clinically relevant antibiotics used in UTI treatment, including amoxicillin and cephalexin to represent β-lactams, as well as gentamicin and tobramycin to represent aminoglycosides. Together, these antibiotics allowed us to investigate how different antibiotic-induced stress mechanisms translate into fluorescent outputs in our biosensor reporter strains.

## <a id="3f3d65dd82be80f29d78dbb65cff6ac3" />β-lactams

<a id="3f3d65dd82be808d97e6d5979aa516a1" />β-lactams share a four-membered β-lactam ring and include penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, and monobactams <Cite refs={[{"n":3,"key":"pandeyBetaLactamAntibiotics2023"}]} />. Their activity depends on binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), including the transpeptidases responsible for cross-linking peptidoglycan. By acylating these enzymes, β-lactams interfere with the formation of a stable cell wall, which can lead to loss of viability and cell lysis. Our selected representatives, amoxicillin and cephalexin, belong to the penicillin and cephalosporin subclasses, respectively.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80fdba24e6a29cae26fc" />Our review identified several routes to β-lactam resistance: antibiotic inactivation by β-lactamases, changes in PBPs, reduced permeability, and efflux. Of these, **β-lactamase-mediated inactivation** is particularly relevant to FAST. β-lactamases hydrolyse the antibiotic’s β-lactam ring, reducing its ability to inhibit its target. This gives us a potential correlation between resistance in the sample bacteria and a change in the antibiotic activity detected by our biosensor.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80b59dd6f1d48e09dd09" />The cellular effects of β-lactams also informed our choice of reporter. Disruption of peptidoglycan synthesis can activate envelope stress responses, including the Cpx pathway in *E. coli*. This connection led us to select the *cpxP* promoter as a candidate for converting antibiotic-induced envelope stress into fluorescence.

## <a id="3fad65dd82be80289d65d43c573d0776" />Aminoglycosides

<a id="3fad65dd82be80619bdcc8c8fa006b64" />Aminoglycosides act on the bacterial ribosome, disrupting accurate protein synthesis <Cite refs={[{"n":4,"key":"tsaiImpactAminoglycosidesDynamics2013"}]} />. Our project selected **gentamicin** and **tobramycin** as representatives of this class.

<a id="3fad65dd82be80f4a17dc525f3484616" />Although aminoglycosides received a lower priority during our initial literature review, they became part of our project’s focus as we turned to UTIs. They offer a second antibiotic class with a different cellular target and relevant enzymatic resistance mechanisms.

<a id="3fad65dd82be8047b992fb83a023bc99" />Aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes can reduce antibiotic activity through acetylation, phosphorylation, or adenylation. Unlike β-lactam hydrolysis, these mechanisms chemically modify the antibiotic rather than necessarily breaking it down. The resulting loss of activity depends on the particular antibiotic and enzyme involved <Cite refs={[{"n":5,"key":"wrightBacterialResistanceAntibiotics2005"}]} />.

# <a id="3f3d65dd82be807ca59efd79784e3a00" />**Stress Pathways**

## <a id="3f3d65dd82be80faba2ffe21d3402e90" />*cpxP* promoter

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80eca7b1fc9d538bf532" />Antibiotics can trigger bacterial stress responses as they disrupt essential cellular processes. The Cpx envelope stress response is a gram-negative bacterial stress-response pathway that is activated when the integrity of the cell envelope is disrupted <Cite refs={[{"n":6,"key":"raivioCpxEnvelopeStress1999"}]} />. This makes it a particularly relevant stress response when detecting the effects of β-lactam antibiotics, which act by binding to to and inactivating enzymes required for bacterial cell wall synthesis <Cite refs={[{"n":3,"key":"pandeyBetaLactamAntibiotics2023"}]} />.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be8090adb9cb16046b4b34" />When envelope stress is sensed, the Cpx defense system is activated in gram-negative bacteria through the CpxA/CpxR two-component system <Cite refs={[{"n":7,"key":"surmannMolecularProteomeAnalyses2016"}]} />. CpxA is a sensor kinase in the membrane and CpxR is a response regulator in the cytoplasm. Under normal, unstressed conditions, CpxP (a small periplasmic protein) interacts with CpxA and helps keep the pathway in an inactive state, preventing unnecessary responses. In the presence of envelope stress, CpxP is degraded, allowing CpxA to autophosphorylate and transfer a phosphate group to the response regulator CpxR. Phosphorylated CpxR then activates downstream stress-response genes, including *cpxP*.

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80f78f37f4fad5052e0f" />

<Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/7b4e54f8dde75825b9af85f34fc5548f.svg" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

<br id="3fdd65dd82be8094a83fdef34dfd3998" />

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80a6857df9cad611de32" />We leverage this bacterial response to construct a p**CpxP-sfGFP stress reporter**. The reporter contains the *cpxP* promoter ($\mathrm{P}_\text{CpxP}$) upstream of the **sfGFP** gene. Under this reporter system, envelope stress is sensed by the Cpx pathway. Subsequently, increased *cpxP* promoter activity leads to the expression of the *sfGFP* gene, producing a measurable increase in fluorescence.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80e69266c05d6f3be264" />Rather than detecting the antibiotic molecule itself, our reporter detects the cellular stress resulting from antibiotic exposure. This construct provides an indirect method of detecting β-lactam and aminoglycoside antibiotics which disrupt cell wall synthesis and cause cell wall protein misfolding, respectively <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"kohanskiHowAntibioticsKill2010"},{"n":8,"key":"FineTuningCpxEnvelope"}]} />. Ultimately, the $\mathrm{P}_\text{CpxP}$-*sfGFP* reporter is intended to provide a rapid readout of β-lactam and aminoglycoside susceptibility as part of our point-of-care UTI diagnostic platform.

## <a id="3f3d65dd82be809cb1f4dab13feb11a0" />*recA* promoter

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80a38b60cad0c7824b9b" />The cellular stress caused by antibiotic exposure can ultimately damage bacterial DNA. The bacterial SOS response is a conserved global DNA-damage response mechanism that allows both gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria to detect and respond to disruptions in DNA replication and repair <Cite refs={[{"n":9,"key":"zgur-bertokDNADamageRepair2013"}]} />. The RecA protein is the central protein sensor that activates it.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80859e6be6125bdcc7ae" />When DNA damage occurs due to the accumulation of reactive metabolites, which produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), regions of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) accumulate. RecA binds to to this ssDNA and forms an active nucleoprotein filament known as RecA\*. This activated RecA\* promotes the self-cleavage of the LexA protein, which is the transcriptional repressor that is normally bound to DNA and keeps SOS-response genes at low expression. LexA cleavage causes it to be released from its operator, which removes its repression on downstream LexA-regulated genes, such as the *recA* gene.

<a id="3fad65dd82be80fbb375fd8e95e74ea9" />

<Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/56c3196afabd57d9b35839e1980a39c3.svg" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

<br id="3fad65dd82be80208698eeff38a59bb6" />

<a id="3f3d65dd82be803394fecb88e04db777" />We leverage this pathway to construct a pRecA-sfGFP stress reporter. The reporter contains the *recA* promoter (pRecA), including its LexA-regulated SOS elements (SOS box), upstream of the sfGFP gene. When antibiotic exposure induces DNA damage and activates the SOS response, LexA repression is relieved, allowing for the upregulation of LexA-regulated genes. As such, pRecA activity increases <Cite refs={[{"n":10,"key":"shapiroAntimicrobialInducedDNADamage2015"}]} />.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80b78a7eca135d05033b" />Rather than detecting the antibiotic molecule itself, this reporter detects the downstream bacterial DNA-damage response. Under the pRecA-sGFP reporter system, *recA* promoter activation induces the expression of sfGFP to produce a measurable fluorescent output.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be803f97a0ca6eddc6654e" />

<Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/7b3b92df84e15269b3e67b0440bf7e11.svg" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

<br id="3fed65dd82be8068b4bbc63ac8a7126f" />

<a id="3fed65dd82be8034a37bdf17b4aafe96" />The RecA reporter is our secondary construct, originally used as a backup to the CpxP reporter. Some studies suggest that aminoglycosides might not directly activate the *recA* gene in *E. coli* <Cite refs={[{"n":11,"key":"baharogluIdentificationGenesInvolved2014"}]} /> while other studies show that antibiotic-related production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) ultimately leads to cell death <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"kohanskiHowAntibioticsKill2010"}]} />. However, other literature shows that most antibiotics lead to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby activating *recA.* As such, we wanted to test the pRecA-sfGFP reporter alongside the $\mathrm{P}_\text{CpxP}$-*sfGFP* reporter for both our antibiotic classes of interest.  Together, the $\mathrm{P}_\text{CpxP}$-*sfGFP* and $\mathrm{P}_\text{RecA}$-*sfGFP*  reporter systems allow us to link antibiotic exposure with different cellular stress responses, thereby indirectly detecting the presence of active antibiotics through a fluorescent output.
