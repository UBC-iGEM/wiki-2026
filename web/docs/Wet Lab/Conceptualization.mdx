<a id="3f3d65dd82be8081a965d9dd6472daca" />Our project aims to provide a simpler, faster and more modular way to determine antibiotic resistance from patient samples. As the wet lab team, we have engineered *E. coli* that links antibiotic-induced stress responses to fluorescent readout by constructing a plasmid system with superfolder green fluorescent protein (sfGFP) and two stress-responsive promoters.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be804bacd6dbe5e0c527a9" />Choosing an effective antibiotic depends on whether the infectious bacteria are susceptible to it meaning the lowest concentration that prevents their visible growth (minimum inhibitory concentration or MIC) is at or below a clinical breakpoint that standard dosing can reach at the site of infection. Our initial literature review explored several antibiotic classes, including β-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, glycopeptides, oxazolidinones, and polymyxins. These classes interfere with different aspects of bacterial physiology for example, β-lactams disrupt cell wall synthesis, aminoglycosides and macrolides interfere with translation, fluoroquinolones target enzymes involved in DNA replication; and polymyxins disrupt the bacterial envelope. These differences provided a starting point for identifying stress-responsive promoters that could report antibiotic activity.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80d9bedbf617a35946a1" />As our project focused more around urinary tract infections (UTIs), we narrowed our focus to β-lactams and aminoglycosides. We selected these classes for their clinical relevance and their enzymatic resistance mechanisms that could be investigated using FAST.

# <a id="3f3d65dd82be8018ad86ff57d648cac9" />**Clinical relevance to urinary tract infections**

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80aaa85aead2c8e38ea9" />Our literature review broadly focused on antibiotics effective against Gram-negative bacteria, particularly *E. coli*. Discussions with the Hancock Lab helped us narrow down our focus on antibiotics effective against urinary tract infections (UTIs).

<a id="3f3d65dd82be805a8b5be365a4fe4c77" />β-lactams and aminoglycosides have complementary areas in terms of its clinical use. β-lactams are widely used in bacterial infection treatment but resistance by β-lactamases in many pathogens can compromise their activity. Aminoglycosides act on a different target and can remain useful against some β-lactam-resistant organisms depending on their susceptibility profile. Their role is particularly relevant in selected complicated or resistant UTIs, although toxicity limits their use. Resistance to one class does not establish susceptibility to the other making antibiotic-specific testing essential.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80d691e2cdaa82165c96" />For our application validation, we selected the most clinically relevant antibiotics used in UTI treatment, including amoxicillin and cephalexin to represent β-lactams, as well as gentamicin and tobramycin to represent aminoglycosides. Together, these antibiotics allow us to investigate how different mechanisms of action translate into responses in our reporter strains.

## <a id="3f3d65dd82be80f29d78dbb65cff6ac3" />β-lactams

<a id="3f3d65dd82be808d97e6d5979aa516a1" />β-lactams share a four-membered β-lactam ring and include penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, and monobactams. Their activity depends on binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), including the transpeptidases responsible for cross-linking peptidoglycan. By acylating these enzymes, β-lactams interfere with the formation of a stable cell wall, which can lead to loss of viability and cell lysis. Our selected representatives, amoxicillin and cephalexin, belong to the penicillin and cephalosporin subclasses, respectively.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80fdba24e6a29cae26fc" />Our review identified several routes to β-lactam resistance: antibiotic inactivation by β-lactamases, changes in PBPs, reduced permeability, and efflux. Of these, **β-lactamase-mediated inactivation** is particularly relevant to FAST. β-lactamases hydrolyse the antibiotic’s β-lactam ring, reducing its ability to inhibit its target. This gives us a potential correlation between resistance in the sample bacteria and a change in the antibiotic activity detected by our biosensor.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80b59dd6f1d48e09dd09" />The cellular effects of β-lactams also informed our choice of reporter. Disruption of peptidoglycan synthesis can activate envelope stress responses, including the Cpx pathway in *E. coli*. This connection led us to select the *cpxP* promoter as a candidate for converting antibiotic-induced envelope stress into fluorescence.

## <a id="3fad65dd82be80289d65d43c573d0776" />Aminoglycosides

<a id="3fad65dd82be80619bdcc8c8fa006b64" />Aminoglycosides act on the bacterial ribosome, disrupting accurate protein synthesis. Our project selected **gentamicin** and **tobramycin** as representatives of this class.

<a id="3fad65dd82be80f4a17dc525f3484616" />Although aminoglycosides received a lower priority during our initial literature review, they became part of our project’s focus as we turned to UTIs. They offer a second antibiotic class with a different cellular target and relevant enzymatic resistance mechanisms.

<a id="3fad65dd82be8047b992fb83a023bc99" />Aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes can reduce antibiotic activity through acetylation, phosphorylation, or adenylation. Unlike β-lactam hydrolysis, these mechanisms chemically modify the antibiotic rather than necessarily breaking it down. The resulting loss of activity depends on the particular antibiotic and enzyme involved.

# <a id="3f3d65dd82be807ca59efd79784e3a00" />**Stress Pathways**

## <a id="3f3d65dd82be80faba2ffe21d3402e90" />*cpxP* promoter

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80eca7b1fc9d538bf532" />Antibiotic exposure can trigger bacterial stress responses when antibiotics disrupt essential cellular processes. The CpxP envelope stress response is a bacterial stress-response pathway that is activated when the integrity of the cell envelope is disrupted <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"raivioCpxEnvelopeStress1999"}]} />. This makes it a particularly relevant stress response when detecting the effects of β-lactam antibiotics, which act by binding to to and inactivating enzymes required for bacterial cell wall synthesis.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be8090adb9cb16046b4b34" />When envelope stress is sensed, the Cpx defense system is activated in Gram-negative bacteria through the CpxA/CpxR two-component system <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"surmannMolecularProteomeAnalyses2016"}]} />. **CpxA** is a sensor kinase in the membrane and **CpxR** is a response regulator in the cytoplasm. Under normal, unstressed conditions, CpxP (a small periplasmic protein) interacts with CpxA and helps keep the pathway in an inactive state, preventing unneeded responses. During envelope stress, CpxP is degraded, allowing CpxA to autophosphorylate and transfer a phosphate group to the response regulator CpxR. Phosphorylated CpxR then activates downstream stress-response genes, including *cpxP*.

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80f78f37f4fad5052e0f" />

<Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/7b4e54f8dde75825b9af85f34fc5548f.svg" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

<br id="3fdd65dd82be8094a83fdef34dfd3998" />

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80a6857df9cad611de32" />We leverage this bacterial response to construct a **CpxP-sfGFP stress reporter**. The reporter contains the *cpxP* promoter ( $P_\text{CpxP}$) upstream of the **superfolder GFP (sfGFP)** gene. When β-lactam exposure causes envelope stress and activates the Cpx pathway, increased *cpxP* promoter activity results in sfGFP production and a measurable increase in fluorescence.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80e69266c05d6f3be264" />This construct provides an indirect method of detecting the bacterial response to β-lactam antibiotics and aminoglycosides <Cite refs={[{"n":3,"key":"kohanskiHowAntibioticsKill2010"}]} />. Rather than detecting the antibiotic molecule itself, our reporter measures the cellular stress resulting from antibiotic exposure. Because β-lactams disrupt cell-wall synthesis, they can trigger the envelope stress response and consequently activate the CpxP-sfGFP reporter <Cite refs={[{"n":4,"key":"FineTuningCpxEnvelope"}]} />.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be806aa375ff13d922b9ca" />Our CpxP reporter is therefore intended to provide a rapid readout of β-lactam and aminoglycoside susceptibility as part of our point-of-care UTI diagnostic platform.

## <a id="3f3d65dd82be809cb1f4dab13feb11a0" />*recA* promoter

<a id="3f3d65dd82be80a38b60cad0c7824b9b" />The cellular stress caused by antibiotic exposure can ultimately damage bacterial DNA. To detect DNA damage, we developed another reporter, the RecA-GFP reporter, based on the bacterial SOS response. The bacterial SOS response is a conserved global DNA-damage response mechanism that allows bacteria to detect and respond to disruptions in DNA replication and repair, and RecA is the central protein sensor that activates it <Cite refs={[{"n":5,"key":"zgur-bertokDNADamageRepair2013"}]} />.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80859e6be6125bdcc7ae" />When DNA damage occurs due to the accumulation of reactive metabolites, which produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), regions of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) accumulate. RecA binds to to this ssDNA and forms an active nucleoprotein filament known as RecA\*. This activated RecA\* promotes the self-cleavage of the LexA protein, which is the transcriptional repressor that is normally bound to DNA and keeps SOS-response genes at low expression. LexA cleavage causes it to be released from its operator, which removes its repression on downstream LexA-regulated genes (e.g. *recA* gene).

<a id="3fad65dd82be80fbb375fd8e95e74ea9" />

<Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/56c3196afabd57d9b35839e1980a39c3.svg" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

<br id="3fad65dd82be80208698eeff38a59bb6" />

<a id="3f3d65dd82be803394fecb88e04db777" />We leverage this pathway to construct a RecA-sfGFP stress reporter. The reporter contains the *recA* promoter (pRecA), including its LexA-regulated SOS elements (SOS box), upstream of the superfolder GFP (sfGFP) gene. When antibiotic exposure induces DNA damage and activates the SOS response, LexA repression is relieved and pRecA activity increases. This results in increased sfGFP production and a measurable fluorescent signal <Cite refs={[{"n":6,"key":"shapiroAntimicrobialInducedDNADamage2015"}]} />.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be8084a078dec3b0ca1af4" />The RecA reporter is our secondary construct, originally used as a backup to the CpxP reporter. Some studies suggest that aminoglycosides might not activate the recA gene in *E. coli*  while other studies show that antibiotic-related production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) ultimately leads to cell death. However, other literature shows that most antibiotics lead to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby activating recA, and thus we wanted to test the recA reporter alongside the cpxP reporter in both our antibiotic classes of interest.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be8015a988e692b19f693b" />Similarly to our CpxP reporter construct, the RecA reporter does not detect antibiotics directly but rather does so indirectly via detecting the downstream bacterial DNA-damage response. The magnitude of fluorescence therefore reflects the physiological response of the reporter cells to antibiotic activity.

<a id="3f3d65dd82be808b889ff1d30d46cdbe" />Together with the CpxP-sfGFP reporter, the RecA-sfGFP reporter allows us connect antibiotic exposure to different cellular stress responses. While CpxP reports envelope stress, RecA reports DNA damage and activation of the SOS response.
