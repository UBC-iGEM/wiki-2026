# <a id="3fcd65dd82be80afb02ecf242a9db481" />What is antibiotic susceptibility?

<a id="3fdd65dd82be8022a20de8c7acf1478f" />**Antibiotic susceptibility is how easily a given antibiotic can stop a bacterium from growing or kill it.** It is measured by using **antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST)**. AST usually finds the lowest drug concentration that stops visible bacterial growth. The result sorts the bacteria into one of three categories: susceptible, intermediate, or resistant. That tells clinicians whether the drug is likely to work at a dose the patient can safely receive.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be802491bde9b890c66795" />**Susceptibility can change over time and antibiotic use favors bacteria that can survive it.** Bacteria can become resistant through genetic mutations and they also pick up resistance genes from other bacteria, often on small mobile pieces of DNA called **plasmids**. These resistant strains survive the treatment and can spread, making it difficult to control. Every unnecessary or poorly targeted prescription therefore speeds up the resistance process, decreasing the number of effective drugs for that kind of bacteria. Resistance differs between patients, pathogens, and regions, so the only reliable way to know whether an antibiotic will work for a specific infection is to test it. Faster, more widely available AST, with results ready before the prescription is written rather than days later, is key both to treating patients well and to slowing antimicrobial resistance (AMR).
