# <a id="3ecd65dd82be8033802ad61ebd2244fe" />Introduction

<a id="3ecd65dd82be800680c3ffb1d7790e21" />Using a novel protocol developed by our hardware team, we fabricated a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)-based microfluidic chip from fused deposition modelling (FDM)-printed molds. This approach enabled us to produce customizable, low-cost chips that could be quickly iterated upon, combining the rapid prototyping of a 3D printer with the capabilities of PDMS-based microfluidics, allowing us to use a small amount of patient sample in our point-of-care device.

## <a id="3ecd65dd82be806182b5e89574979a70" />What is a microfluidic chip?

<a id="3ecd65dd82be8021b470f88b3f0261b1" />Microfluidic chips are an emerging technology within the biomedical space <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"pattanayakMicrofluidicChipsRecent2021"}]} />. They are a chunk of material (such as PDMS or PMMA) engraved with channels less than a millimetre in diameter that move fluid to serve a purpose, such as chemical analysis <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"pattanayakMicrofluidicChipsRecent2021"},{"n":2,"key":"whitesidesOriginsFutureMicrofluidics2006"},{"n":3,"key":"sackmannPresentFutureRole2014"}]} />.

<br id="3fed65dd82be80d39a11d9902c8d6fce" />

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80cd85a7c6b8012e7fe8" />A microfluidic chip must consist of several components to be considered as such, a method of introducing reagents/samples, a method for transporting fluid throughout the channels, and a methof of mixing reagents, and various other devices such as valves <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"whitesidesOriginsFutureMicrofluidics2006"}]} />.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80028fa5c7260d64be1f" />Microfluidic chips are especially valuable in biomedical engineering due to the scale of the chips <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"whitesidesOriginsFutureMicrofluidics2006"}]} />. When fluid flows in small channels (100s of micrometres in length) turbulence is incredibly limited, leading to mixing which occurs through diffusion instead of convection and is easier to predict mathematically<Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"whitesidesOriginsFutureMicrofluidics2006"}]} />. Microfluidics allow a researcher to perform smaller-scale experiments with less reagent volume required, lower reaction times, and the possibility of parallel operation <Cite refs={[{"n":5,"key":"beebePhysicsApplicationsMicrofluidics2002"}]} />.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be8001a8e1ce05acdc033e" />The 2016 Technion Israel team cast PDMS in a two-part 3D-printed mold and incorporated a microscope slide into the chip, allowing it to be positioned under a microscope for imaging. Building on this approach, we used updated 3D-printing technology to produce smoother mold surfaces and plasma-bonded microscope slides to the bottoms of our PDMS chips. Our molds are single-part rather than two-part, but they are not yet reusable because their edges must be cut to remove the finished chips safely. Unlike the Technion team's design, which modifies a commercially available chip, all of our designs are novel to iGEM and unique to our team. Their work also does not report whether liquid flow through the chip was validated. We cannot confirm whether leakage was an issue, but their use of silicone glue raises questions about seal reliability that would benefit from experimental validation.

### <a id="3fdd65dd82be80deadb3f2bf3f8e6ca3" />2017 Munich <Cite refs={[{"n":7,"key":"TeamMunich2017igemorg"}]} />

<a id="3fdd65dd82be805a9f10d14e219a3caf" />They fabricated a PDMS microfluidic chip using Sylgard PDMS and soft lithography, incorporating 3D-printed negative molds to accelerate prototyping. Rather than bonding the PDMS to glass, they used plasma bonding to attach it to another piece of PDMS, allowing their chip to be used for imaging. Their fabrication process also involved creating an aluminum shell, which we were able to eliminate by using mold walls and disposing of the molds after use. Additionally, their choice of 3D-printing material was not necessarily PLA, potentially limiting their ability to place the molds in an oven. However, because their application differs significantly from ours, it is difficult to directly compare the effectiveness of our approaches. In particular, while incorporating glass into our design provides practical benefits for our application, it would not necessarily offer the same advantages for their imaging setup.

However, they treated their chips with silane to increase their hydrophobicity, an approach we were unable to explore this year that may help prevent leakage during storage. Ultimately, their limited documentation makes it difficult to fully understand their fabrication protocol and assess the effectiveness of their approach relative to ours.

## <a id="3ecd65dd82be80da9a8fcc670521c91a" />How does a microfluidic chip integrate into our project?

<a id="3ecd65dd82be805bbca8ec1ec15b7aca" />The microfluidic chip is the epicentre of the project, where everything comes together. Early on in the conception of the project, we realized that we needed a platform that could serve the purposes of:

- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80ccbc93d767ceb9b364" />Mixing the antibiotic and patient sample
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80529b43caa304dde353" />Hold the sample and antibiotic until the degradation time has elapsed
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80e1bc61ce995bb4f216" />Move the mixed antibiotic and patient sample to our sensor bacteria
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be802da9cfcca63d63161a" />Quantify the presence of fluorescence in a binary YES or NO

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80d69e3aced35483f3fb" />A microfluidic chip allows us to approach the above in the fewest procedure steps possible, so a clinician using the device would not need to monitor or work with it as closely, enabling them to run multiple experiments in parallel for different patients.

## <a id="3fdd65dd82be8042b2dfdef3fd87c4ab" />Design Considerations

<a id="3ecd65dd82be8039b3bedf46a9028251" />Our team decided to start the process by using a development framework developed by \[enter]. This helped ensure we made thoughtful decisions as we approached our microfluidic chip. We started the project by brainstorming the requirements our chip would have to meet to be considered a functional product.  They can be seen in the list below:

- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80c3a420f0ac793c4ef2" />Must be low cost
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be805f9c20fa4da2b317eb" />Must have zero leakage
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be8015aa00c7b0bfdd12f5" />Must minimize air bubbles in the manufactured material and input fluid
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80efb82dcca39c536cf0" />Must be able to be filled with a 1 mL of fluid
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80fc8c3bed5d831f7b5b" />Must be able to integrate a device that will output relevant data
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be8046a5ebf7b5f1f1e12c" />Must be manufactured from a material that is inert and biologically compatible
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80a2bc6ade7f4e80aef2" />Must be transparent to allow for viewing of fluorescence

<a id="3f9d65dd82be806babfacbf3c7c7fe61" />With this list in mind, we were able to approach the selection of a manufacturing process.

## <a id="3f9d65dd82be8063ab9fe12333edc041" />Manufacturing Decisions

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80658962f652cb40997e" />Before designing the channels of our microfluidic chip, we needed to select a suitable manufacturing process that balanced cost, optical transparency, and leak resistance.  We heavily prioritized cost, as a low fabrication cost was essential to support iterative design improvements. The chip also needed to be transparent for fluorescence imaging and reliably sealed to prevent fluid leakage. Based on our research of common microfluidic fabrication techniques, we compiled the comparison table below to evaluate each method and identify the most suitable approach for our application.

    <a id="3f9d65dd82be80039d81efeb55570457" />At this point in the process, we started to develop channel designs and cast them in epoxy to establish a baseline of how the process would work before we moved onto the more expensive PDMS. ## <a id="3ecd65dd82be80c0b3ffdc399eeb8f0e" />Mark Two: PDMS Attempt I

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80bd9dfcc6f1ad042ae4" />

<Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/c23e24da49d65ee7bd3399cc385ce8eb.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

<a id="3f9d65dd82be808db28ed6e5a299f61b" />

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="3fcd65dd82be8000aa5ecd4233d0f0ba" />Many of the adjustments that were made to this chip were related to the issues with removing the Epoxy chip in DBTL cycle 1. In order to remedy the issue with the cured chip bonding with the sides and base of the mold container, we attempted to make a microfluidic design without including any walls directly attached to the chip. This both removed the potential bonding issues that we anticipated with PDMS and also decreased printing time, allowing for a quicker build time for this chip. As an additional feature to attempt to lessen the effects of sticking, all of the microfluidic channels were chamfered at the top.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be8050a157f28d08862e1a" />With this new iteration of the chip, we wanted to experiment with alternative channel shapes and how effective they would be for manufacturing purposes. Using PDMS, we decided to fully cover the channels here, such that the channel pathways were only exposed on the bottom. With this, we are able to close the chips by using only one sheet of glass to be plasma-bonded to the chip rather than two that would have been needed otherwise.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80729ea6fb79fef1a699" />Along with this change, both the mixing and degradation sections of the chips were altered. For the mixing section, a repeated L-shaped herringbone intrusion was added instead of the original zigzag section. Each of these herringbone patterns works to jostle all the laminar fluid streams together semi-randomly such that there are many small antibiotic streamlines flowing adjacent to the patient sample. This is done such that diffusion that happens along the course of the chip will result in the mixing of these two fluids in laminar flow, and the effectiveness of this pattern has previously been tested for its mixing capabilities at low Re <Cite refs={[{"n":15,"key":"liuTwofluidMixingMicrochannel2004"}]} />. The design was appealing due to its flat base, meaning it is compatible with our manufacturing process with PDMS pouring.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80d283f4d2bb9a99a256" />The degradation section was changed to split into 4 separate sections. This was done as an alternative geometry to keep the small channel width while also maintaining high fluid volume with a shape that has wider gaps, which we believed would reduce the likelihood of a rip occurring. The change also allowed us to experiment with alternative chip designs, giving us better information on which designs work most effectively for FDM-based chip manufacturing, which we can use for future DBTL cycles.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="build">
    <a id="3f9d65dd82be80f8af46d108dd8a879a" />Build:

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80878a9bfdb57df63d8b" />This iteration of the chip used primarily the same CAD techniques as the previous iteration of the chip. A slight chamfer has been added to the edges of each of the channels. This was chosen, for this iteration, as 0.1mm of each of the side lengths cut off at the top corners of each of the channels. The inlet and outlet valves were also adjusted to be 4mm tall this iteration. This allows us, for the build phase, to have a section of the negative chip mold to protrude out of the PDMS cast that we are using for this iteration. This is important here, as it means that we are able to have an opening for the addition of sensor bacteria and antibiotics at either end of the chip. Without this alteration, an incision would have needed to be made to access the inner channels of the chip.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be808dabfae1a028c03111" />With this, the herringbone structure was created on the chip, with 40 iterations of the L shapes evenly spaced from each other along the first section of the chip. Each of these shapes creates a divot that digs 0.35mm into the channel, leaving 0.65mm of the height of the channel unchanged. All of the corners on this channel being at 90 degrees, and the angle between each end of the herringbone and the edge of the channel at 45 degrees.

    <a id="3f9d65dd82be808c8c37f1336cc4a8d1" />Design:

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80ca817bdc92ad952a9f" />To look more closely at the diffusion factors of the chips, and how much they play into the mixing quality within the chips, an alternative build design was synthesized to look into this. This “turnaround“ design does not have any channel techniques that are specifically made to intertwine fluid streamlines. This results in there being no clear mixing sections, and it instead relies on having a long fluid pathway in order to have an ample amount of space for diffusion and degradation to occur.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be803eabd5cac53478d9eb" />The outlets of this design are in a semicircle, which holds 7 different outlet channels. This was a change that was made to account for the utilization of more sensor bacteria in the outlet channels, giving flexibility for more efficient sample testing.     <a id="3fdd65dd82be8082be3bd8f0b1cbff39" />Although the herringbone microfluidic chip has been designed with the mixing section in mind, there is also alternative mixing methods which are worth exploring. All parts of these chip sections were designed with major differences from other models being made.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80d2b6afff68cc4815bd" />The mixing for this section was designed with small elliptical cuts made alongside three parallel channels. This has been shown to provide additional fluid mixing in previous microfluidic chips, with a 96% mixing efficiency found, compared to a 28% mixing found in a straight channel of the same length <Cite refs={[{"n":11,"key":"leeMicrofluidicMixingReview2011"},{"n":17,"key":"ScientificNet"}]} />.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80c49139edbdc6a3e7e4" />The degradation portion of the chip is a wide pocket in the center of the chip, which allows for a relatively larger volume to be used, while interactions occur between sample and antibiotic. This has the added benefit of reducing the risk that, if too much sample is added initially, there is less risk of excess sample being pushed all the way through the chip and to the outlet channels prematurely.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80358f78daab05ca7323" />The outlet channels on this chip design are again an equal distance from the edge of the microfluidic chip. For this, we designed each of the channel branches to be of the same length, such that there would be less of a bias in sample detection with this design. This was done by adding turns and curves to sections of the chip, such that the sum of all the lengths being used is equivalent between all the pathways. The same base plate and wall widths were used for this iteration of the chip as the other ones being designed.

## <a id="3f9d65dd82be80248af7fe362fb89f9b" />Mark Six: Linear Channels

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80d09733e1fd08769961" />

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="3f9d65dd82be809bb910fd33cfb5f9cd" />We changed the position of our channels to make integration with the fluorescence detector significantly easier as the angle between the wells on the half circle approach led to a very small angular difference in the wells and would require complex and highly accurate mechanics to differentiate between different wells.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="build">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be805db712e3a689d208d4" />

    <Figure imgs={[{"url":"https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/1b29d447260c5009836ad07894bba3b8.avif","alt":"","scale":0}]}>
      <Fragment slot="content">
        <a id="3fdd65dd82be80c3b26cdb1058ad01bc" />The dark circles on this image represent the wells, and the light grey rectangle represents the microfluidic chip. Our dimensions are visualized using the brackets.
      </Fragment>
    </Figure>
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="test">
    <a id="3f9d65dd82be80ed869af70d70f2625d" />To determine that our linear wells would not lead to a significant difference in filling (so that the bacteria are not exposed to different levels of antibiotic) we performed a series of calculations to see what the difference would be.

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be8039a978d3d2041a6d15" />Assumptions:

    - <a id="3fcd65dd82be805a8dbfd8e8767fe941" />main channel is 1\*1 mm\*2
    - <a id="3fcd65dd82be8096822ec1c247ed5f22" />outlets (7) are 0.5\*0.5 mm^2
    - <a id="3fcd65dd82be8034a073d6607e9ab062" />outlet length is 15 mm
    - <a id="3fcd65dd82be80a7a9c5ec76a30d78c6" />outlet spacing 8 mm apart
    - <a id="3fcd65dd82be8025bc32fe9c9bcc6e2f" />identical outlets
    - <a id="3fcd65dd82be805fbfc6d3849eb7f023" />There is no output from the wells (i.e. they never overflow)

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be8078a0befc14c9192da1" />Hydraulic Resistance of main channel:

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be804382d6f21b8f19f2bd" />

    $$
    \mu: \text{dynamic viscosity},
    L: \text{Length}, h: \text{length of channel side} \\ 
    R_{M} = \frac{28.4 \mu \cdot L}{h^{4}} \\
    R_{M} = \frac{28.4 (0.001) \cdot(0.008)}{(0.001)^{4}} \\
    R_{M} = 2.3 \cdot 10^8 \frac{Pa\cdot s}{m^{3}}
    $$

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be8066956df04399fbfbfe" />Hydraulic Resistance of the Outlet Channel

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be80168360e1a4d281f96e" />

    $$
    R_{O} = \frac{28.4 \mu*L}{h^{4}} \\
    R_{O} = \frac{28.4 (0.001)\cdot(0.0015)}{(0.0005)^{4}} \\
    R_{O} = 6.8 \cdot 10^{9} \frac{Pa \cdot s}{m^{3}}
    $$

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be80749853e50f1960f236" />The outlet channel is therefore 300x more resistive than the main channel.

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be803da847db7267f7b901" />

    $$
    \frac{R_{O}}{R_{M}} = 30
    $$

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be80f5804fe717aa249165" />To split our flow equally between wells we must divide the flow between the eight wells.

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be80ca97e1d6f6cd32db2d" />

    $$
    Q_{i}=\frac{Q_{O}}{7} = 0.143 Q_{O}
    $$

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be80a098ccd61c547c2ae9" />In the main channel, the flow will drop as it passes each successive well.

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be801184acd1a733986ae7" />

    $$
    Q= 1 \text{,} \frac{6}{7} \text{,} \frac{5}{7} \text{,} \frac{4}{7}\text{,} \frac{3}{7}\text{,} \frac{2}{7}\text{,}\frac{1}{7}
    $$

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be8041a2c7dfd8af79e1cf" />In the main channel, there were therefore be a pressure loss as the volume of fluid pushing forward will be lessened:

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be806faf91d61bbfce89d9" />

    $$
    \Delta P = QR_{M} \\
    \begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
    \hline
    \text{Well} & \text{Flow Rate} & \text{Pressure Drop} \\
    \hline
    1 & Q & QR_m \\
    2 & \frac{6}{7}Q & \frac{6}{7}QR_m \\
    3 & \frac{5}{7}Q & \frac{5}{7}QR_m \\
    4 & \frac{4}{7}Q & \frac{4}{7}QR_m \\
    5 & \frac{3}{7}Q & \frac{3}{7}QR_m \\
    6 & \frac{2}{7}Q & \frac{2}{7}QR_m \\
    7 & \frac{1}{7}Q & \frac{1}{7}QR_m \\
    \hline
    \end{array}
    $$

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be80b1a713e061cc2ce7b7" />You are then able to add up all of the pressure drops.

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be80acbb5cefe1ca9bf0b1" />

    $$
    \sum_{i=P_{1}}^{P_{8}} \Delta P_{Mi} = Q_{i}R_{M} \\
    \sum_{i=P_{1}}^{P_{8}} \Delta P_{Mi} = (1+\frac{6}{7}+\frac{5}{7}+\frac{4}{7}+\frac{3}{7}+\frac{2}{7}+\frac{1}{7})QR_{M} \\
    \Delta P_M =  9.2\cdot 10^8 Q Pa
    $$

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be80858440d9eae070c516" />To find the pressure drops across the outlets as fluid moves in we then:

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be8058a844d5f4fdc4bbed" />

    $$
    R_{O}=6.8 \cdot 10^{9} \\
    \Delta P_O = (7)(R_{O})(\frac{Q} {7})\\
    \Delta P_O = 6.8 \cdot 10^{9} Q Pa
    $$

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be80129549e5c56d71678f" />We can then compare the pressure difference across the main channel to into the outlets to find whether there is an unequal push.

    <a id="3fcd65dd82be8070addff8c6b0f05683" />

    $$
    \frac{\Delta P_M}{\Delta P_O}=\frac{9.2\cdot 10^8}{6.8 \cdot10^{9}} \\
    \frac{\Delta P_M}{\Delta P_O}= 0.14\\
    $$

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80638303cf04ac758c5f" />Therefore, most of the pressure comes from the outlet. Because the pressure at the outlet is so much larger than the pressure in the main channel, the flow tends to equalize.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be8081ae26cb5abf2fac45" />We can now compare individual wells to see if there is a difference in that pressure equalization. If there is a significant difference it implies that there is a significant difference in the filling of the wells.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80afb6abdcb439ecee95" />

    $$
    \frac{ \Delta P_{M}}{\Delta P_{O7}}=\frac{(Q/7)R_M}{R_OQ} \\
    \frac{\Delta P_{M}}{\Delta P_{O7}}=\frac{R_M}{7R_O} \\
    \frac{\Delta P_{M}}{\Delta P_{O7}}=0.00476
    $$

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be808182aed902a1bf1d5a" />We then do this for each outlet:

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be800e97ebf7d9a43c5a13" />

    $$
    \begin{array}{|c|c|}
    \hline
    \text{Section} &
    \text{Pressure Difference With Main Channel} \\
    \hline
    1 & 0.0333\\
    2 &  0.0167\\
    3 &  0.0111\\
    4 &  0.0083 \\
    5 &  0.0067\\
    6 &  0.0056\\
    7 &  0.0048\\
    8 &   0.0042\\
    \hline
    \end{array}
    $$

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be804da412cad0f9c2b3bf" />We can now determine the relative variation between the first outlet and the last outlet:

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be8049ba6af6653ee9eb7d" />

    $$
    \frac{P_{O7}}{P_{O1}}=\frac{0.0042}{0.0333} = 0.13
    $$

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80a3bce7cef3dbcdc4cb" />Therefore, the relative difference between the first well and the last well is 13%. Since pressure and flow have a direct relationship, we are able to infer that the flow between the wells is not significantly different and was deemed acceptable by the team.
  </Fragment>

# <a id="3ecd65dd82be803eb079c678c4df61d6" />Next Steps

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80f8bcd9f324e37d227c" />Our next steps with this project will be optimizing our input flow rates and updating our process so that we are able to get more than one chip out of the same mold.
