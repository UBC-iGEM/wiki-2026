# <a id="3ecd65dd82be8033802ad61ebd2244fe" />Introduction

<a id="3ecd65dd82be800680c3ffb1d7790e21" />Using a novel protocol developed by our hardware team, we fabricated a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)-based microfluidic chip from fused deposition modelling (FDM)-printed molds. This approach enabled us to produce customizable, low-cost chips that could be quickly iterated upon, combining the rapid prototyping of a 3D printer with the capabilities of PDMS-based microfluidics, allowing us to use a small amount of patient sample in our point-of-care device.

## <a id="3ecd65dd82be806182b5e89574979a70" />What is a microfluidic chip?

<a id="3ecd65dd82be8021b470f88b3f0261b1" />Microfluidic chips are an emerging technology within the biomedical space <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"pattanayakMicrofluidicChipsRecent2021"}]} />. They are a chunk of material (such as PDMS or PMMA) engraved with channels less than a millimetre in diameter that move fluid to serve a purpose, such as chemical analysis <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"pattanayakMicrofluidicChipsRecent2021"},{"n":2,"key":"whitesidesOriginsFutureMicrofluidics2006"},{"n":3,"key":"sackmannPresentFutureRole2014"}]} />.

<br id="3fed65dd82be80d39a11d9902c8d6fce" />

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80cd85a7c6b8012e7fe8" />A microfluidic chip must consist of several components to be considered as such: a method of introducing reagents/samples, a method for transporting fluid throughout the channels, and a method of mixing reagents, and various other devices such as valves <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"whitesidesOriginsFutureMicrofluidics2006"}]} />.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80028fa5c7260d64be1f" />Microfluidic chips are especially valuable in biomedical engineering due to the scale of the chips <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"whitesidesOriginsFutureMicrofluidics2006"}]} />. When fluid flows in small channels (hundreds of micrometres in length) turbulence is incredibly limited, leading to mixing which occurs through diffusion instead of convection and is easier to predict mathematically <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"whitesidesOriginsFutureMicrofluidics2006"}]} />. Microfluidics allow a researcher to perform smaller-scale experiments with less reagent volume required, shorter reaction times, and the possibility of parallel operation <Cite refs={[{"n":5,"key":"beebePhysicsApplicationsMicrofluidics2002"}]} />.

# <a id="3fcd65dd82be80f0a24ceb053365c15e" />FDM-PDMS Microfluidic Chips

## <a id="3fdd65dd82be8029b0eff96e305faa1a" />Our Chip

<a id="3ffd65dd82be800dbc14f471226de5fa" />Our microfluidic chips were fabricated using a single-part mold and cast in PDMS, following the protocol outlined in [PDMS Casting](/ubc-vancouver/hardware/protocol-database/pdms-casting). The PDMS chips were then plasma-bonded to microscope slides to create a sealed structure. To image the wells, we developed a custom design specifically for our microfluidic chip. The multi-outlet design allows us to compare sensor bacteria responses directly with a control group through a single fluid pathway. Finally, we used modified pipette tips to facilitate sample loading by leveraging Bernoulli’s principle, allowing us to distribute the sample evenly without the need for syringes.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be8001a8e1ce05acdc033e" />The 2016 Technion Israel team cast PDMS in a two-part 3D-printed mold and incorporated a microscope slide into the chip, allowing it to be positioned under a microscope for imaging. Building on this approach, we used updated 3D-printing technology to produce smoother mold surfaces and plasma-bonded microscope slides to the bottoms of our PDMS chips. Our molds are single-part rather than two-part, but they are not yet reusable because their edges must be cut to remove the finished chips safely. Unlike the Technion team's design, which modifies a commercially available chip, all of our designs are novel to iGEM and unique to our team. Their work also does not report whether liquid flow through the chip was validated. We cannot confirm whether leakage was an issue, but their use of silicone glue raises questions about seal reliability that would benefit from experimental validation.

### <a id="3fdd65dd82be80deadb3f2bf3f8e6ca3" />2017 Munich <Cite refs={[{"n":7,"key":"TeamMunich2017igemorg"}]} />

<a id="3fdd65dd82be805a9f10d14e219a3caf" />They fabricated a PDMS microfluidic chip using Sylgard PDMS and soft lithography, incorporating 3D-printed negative molds to accelerate prototyping. Rather than bonding the PDMS to glass, they used plasma bonding to attach it to another piece of PDMS, allowing their chip to be used for imaging. Their fabrication process also involved creating an aluminum shell, which we were able to eliminate by using mold walls and disposing of the molds after use. Additionally, their choice of 3D-printing material was not necessarily polylactic acid (PLA), potentially limiting their ability to place the molds in an oven. However, because their application differs significantly from ours, it is difficult to directly compare the effectiveness of our approaches. In particular, while incorporating glass into our design provides practical benefits for our application, it would not necessarily offer the same advantages for their imaging setup.

### <a id="3fdd65dd82be8021a61df06adc4e79d0" />2022 Iiser Tirupati <Cite refs={[{"n":8,"key":"IISERTirupati_IndiaDesignnew2022"}]} />

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80ed869ff1d7bb1efb9e" />This team discusses using 3D printing to manufacture their microfluidic chips, but do not clearly document the fabrication process or the specific steps involved. They also do not appear to have used PLA molds, making their approach difficult to compare directly with our project. However, they treated their chips with silane to increase their hydrophobicity, an approach we were unable to explore this year that may help prevent leakage during storage. Ultimately, their limited documentation makes it difficult to fully understand their fabrication protocol and assess the effectiveness of their approach relative to ours.

## <a id="3ecd65dd82be80da9a8fcc670521c91a" />How does a microfluidic chip integrate into our project?

<a id="3ecd65dd82be805bbca8ec1ec15b7aca" />The microfluidic chip is the epicentre of the project, where everything comes together. Early on in the conception of the project, we realized that we needed a platform that could serve the purposes of:

- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80ccbc93d767ceb9b364" />Mixing the antibiotic and patient sample
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80529b43caa304dde353" />Hold the sample and antibiotic until the degradation time has elapsed
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80e1bc61ce995bb4f216" />Move the mixed antibiotic and patient sample to our sensor bacteria
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be802da9cfcca63d63161a" />Quantify the presence of fluorescence in a binary YES or NO

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80d69e3aced35483f3fb" />A microfluidic chip allows us to approach the above in the fewest procedure steps possible, so a clinician using the device would not need to monitor or work with it as closely, enabling them to run multiple experiments in parallel for different patients.

## <a id="3fdd65dd82be8042b2dfdef3fd87c4ab" />Design Considerations

<a id="3ecd65dd82be8039b3bedf46a9028251" />Our team decided to start the process by using a development framework developed by \[enter]. This helped ensure we made thoughtful decisions as we approached our microfluidic chip. We started the project by brainstorming the requirements our chip would have to meet to be considered a functional product.  They can be seen in the list below:

- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80c3a420f0ac793c4ef2" />Must be low cost
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be805f9c20fa4da2b317eb" />Must have zero leakage
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be8015aa00c7b0bfdd12f5" />Must minimize air bubbles in the manufactured material and input fluid
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80efb82dcca39c536cf0" />Must be able to be filled with a 1 mL of fluid
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80fc8c3bed5d831f7b5b" />Must be able to integrate a device that will output relevant data
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be8046a5ebf7b5f1f1e12c" />Must be manufactured from a material that is inert and biologically compatible
- <a id="3f8d65dd82be80a2bc6ade7f4e80aef2" />Must be transparent to allow for viewing of fluorescence

<a id="3f9d65dd82be806babfacbf3c7c7fe61" />With this list in mind, we were able to approach the selection of a manufacturing process.

## <a id="3f9d65dd82be8063ab9fe12333edc041" />Manufacturing Decisions

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80658962f652cb40997e" />Before designing the channels of our microfluidic chip, we needed to select a suitable manufacturing process that balanced cost, optical transparency, and leak resistance.  We heavily prioritized cost, as a low fabrication cost was essential to support iterative design improvements. The chip also needed to be transparent for fluorescence imaging and reliably sealed to prevent fluid leakage. Based on our research of common microfluidic fabrication techniques, we compiled the comparison table below to evaluate each method and identify the most suitable approach for our application.

    <a id="482ea5e9a1c844c59293e6aaa9458baa" />At this point in the process, we started to develop channel designs and cast them in epoxy to establish a baseline of how the process would work before we moved onto the more expensive PDMS. This chip is designed to be an initial model that clearly indicates different important sections to a microfluidic chip, giving us a baseline to improve on for future designs.

    <a id="833404398933440ab7406174c3eb2520" />Three separate sections were designed to be put into the epoxy chip, corresponding to a fluid mixing section, a degradation section, and an outlet section.     <a id="ed0064a4e83e4aae807b2b29fb36b8af" />For this first iteration of chips, a zigzag pattern was chosen to represent the mixing section of the epoxy chip. ## <a id="3ecd65dd82be80c0b3ffdc399eeb8f0e" />Mark Two: PDMS Attempt I

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80bd9dfcc6f1ad042ae4" />

<Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/c23e24da49d65ee7bd3399cc385ce8eb.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

<a id="1a9fdbf11e554f6aae069f3dd2c5c565" />

<a id="ab4aea18c4ca4aecb661e8cdf1c526b3" />

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="547f70d47d324e8781fdd50208a31c37" />Many of the adjustments that were made to this chip were related to the issues with removing the epoxy chip in DBTL cycle 1. In order to remedy the issue with the cured chip bonding with the sides and base of the mold container, we attempted to make a microfluidic design without including any walls directly attached to the chip. This both removed the potential bonding issues that we anticipated with PDMS and also decreased printing time, allowing for a quicker build time for this chip. As an additional feature to lessen the effects of adhesion, all of the microfluidic channels were chamfered at the top.

    <a id="ce0a978d6e1346df9d4d57ca3e2a93ae" />With this new iteration of the chip, we wanted to experiment with alternative channel shapes and how effective they would be for manufacturing purposes. Using PDMS, we decided to fully cover the channels here, such that the channel pathways were only exposed on the bottom. With this, we are able to close the chips by using only one sheet of glass to be plasma-bonded to the chip rather than two that would have been needed otherwise.

    <a id="9aa3f3823079445b87867e81426c80d2" />Along with this change, both the mixing and degradation sections of the chips were altered. For the mixing section, a repeated L-shaped herringbone intrusion was added instead of the original zigzag section. Each of these herringbone patterns works to jostle all the laminar fluid streams together semi-randomly such that there are many small antibiotic streamlines flowing adjacent to the patient sample. This is done such that diffusion that happens along the course of the chip will result in the mixing of these two fluids in laminar flow, and the effectiveness of this pattern has previously been tested for its mixing capabilities at low Re <Cite refs={[{"n":15,"key":"liuTwofluidMixingMicrochannel2004"}]} />. The design was appealing due to its flat base, meaning it is compatible with our manufacturing process with PDMS pouring.

    <a id="410999bdc1804ba1a2de7e6f66ed1429" />The degradation section was changed to split into 4 separate sections. This was done as an alternative geometry to keep the small channel width while also maintaining high fluid volume with a shape that has wider gaps, which we believed would reduce the likelihood of a tear occurring. The change also allowed us to experiment with alternative chip designs, giving us better information on which designs work most effectively for FDM-based chip manufacturing, which we can use for future DBTL cycles.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="build">
    <a id="bc09930bcea54c4d8e6d035f0f854374" />This iteration of the chip used primarily the same CAD techniques as the previous iteration of the chip. A slight chamfer has been added to the edges of each of the channels. This was chosen, for this iteration, as 0.1 mm of each of the side lengths cut off at the top corners of each of the channels. The inlet and outlet valves were also adjusted to be 4 mm tall this iteration. This allows us, for the build phase, to have a section of the negative chip mold to protrude out of the PDMS cast that we are using for this iteration. This is important here, as it means that we are able to have an opening for the addition of sensor bacteria and antibiotics at either end of the chip. Without this alteration, an incision would have needed to be made to access the inner channels of the chip.

    <a id="02d0c797ce404f3e84dfb265dd35211c" />With this, the herringbone structure was created on the chip, with 40 iterations of the L shapes evenly spaced from each other along the first section of the chip. Each of these shapes creates a divot that digs 0.35mm into the channel, leaving 0.65mm of the height of the channel unchanged. All of the corners on this channel being at 90 degrees, and the angle between each end of the herringbone and the edge of the channel at 45 degrees.

    <a id="19daf2ed3af14f4798cb32c81e33eec0" />One option for future cycles was re-adding just a base plate, as this would allow us to glue it to the base of the Petri dish that we were using to cast PDMS in this cycle.     <a id="bac560dfa9e34abb89bc6d9b3e273058" />The herringbone design from above is reused here, this time including a base plate surrounding the sides of the channels.     <a id="9bed073cb1de477b9fe8324db7c1bff3" />Although the herringbone microfluidic chip has been designed with the mixing section in mind, there is also alternative mixing methods which are worth exploring.     <a id="86c1f27ff88e44de8cfbbc884dea755a" />The mixing for this section was designed with small elliptical cuts made alongside three parallel channels. This has been shown to provide additional fluid mixing in previous microfluidic chips, with a 96% mixing efficiency found, compared to a 28% mixing found in a straight channel of the same length <Cite refs={[{"n":11,"key":"leeMicrofluidicMixingReview2011"},{"n":17,"key":"ScientificNet"}]} />.

    <a id="56c705778f2445e2919a97874935ec8d" />The outlet channels on this chip design are again an equal distance from the edge of the microfluidic chip. The same base plate and wall widths were used for this iteration of the chip as the other ones being designed.

    <a id="a067c9f5fb7542eead57930f8a167aec" />Degradation for this chip is done in a grid of narrow fluid channels, which was done to give this area of the chip structural support for the PDMS while also maintaining fluid volume. ## <a id="3f9d65dd82be80248af7fe362fb89f9b" />Mark Six: Linear Channels

<a id="9b21b4ad890a437c870949ea9aa2f8dd" />

<a id="fa7db2dd608847b28a74840cffd116a4" />

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="36da2a546ba24100ad0b0ae34171c43a" />We changed the position of our channels to make integration with the fluorescence detector significantly easier as the angle between the wells on the half circle approach led to a very small angular difference in the wells and would require complex and highly accurate mechanics to differentiate between different wells.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="build">
    <a id="aeb771e207394b32965208c3186aa423" />A new fluid well structure was implemented into chip designs, ensuring even spacing of all wells along the length of the chip. Figure 20 showcases the general outline that was used for future chip modelling.

    <a id="6c5f99b4fd3a4e6ebe5c21f7ca78db06" />

    <Figure imgs={[{"url":"https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/1b29d447260c5009836ad07894bba3b8.avif","alt":"","scale":0}]}>
      <Fragment slot="content">
        <a id="c7ca6c86170c433e88b4a1557be0940a" />Figure 22: The dark circles on this image represent the wells, and the light grey rectangle represents the microfluidic chip. Our dimensions are visualized using the brackets.
      </Fragment>
    </Figure>
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="test">
    <a id="8d455307595549b3b16bb77a74a7d112" />To determine that our linear wells would not lead to a significant difference in filling (so that the bacteria are not exposed to different levels of antibiotic) we performed a series of calculations to see what the difference would be.

    <a id="66221d5261cb494c838a4c743af92c07" />Assumptions:

    - <a id="c765060eae264402bf912bd36facc692" />main channel is 1 x 1 x 2 mm
    - <a id="ec418a0c48fc415abeefbe4f75daa208" />outlets (7) are 0.5 x 0.5 mm^2
    - <a id="e3ccb0341d434ae6bd07fae1b9343ddb" />outlet length is 15 mm
    - <a id="873774892d104d8a8811aca2992441db" />outlet spacing 9.375 mm apart
    - <a id="32ead747dec24f879e04e437df3fa90c" />identical outlets
    - <a id="5ac054235da74094b6bca175a20eabaf" />There is no output from the wells (i.e. they never overflow)

    <a id="1cad278800ef413c935259377f03a9d2" />Hydraulic Resistance of main channel <Cite refs={[{"n":19,"key":"TheoreticalMicrofluidicsOxford"}]} />:

    <a id="7a9d5bb994fb49f2bde16cb6ff32356b" />

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    R_M &= \frac{28.4\cdot \mu \cdot L}{h^{4}} \\[6pt]
       &= \frac{28.4\,(0.001\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s})(0.009375\ \mathrm{m})}{(0.001\ \mathrm{m})^{4}} \\[6pt]
        &\approx 2.7 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{\frac{Pa\cdot s}{m^{3}}}
    \end{aligned}
    \\[10pt]
    \text{where } \mu = \text{dynamic viscosity},\quad
    L = \text{channel length},\quad
    h = \text{channel side length}
    $$

    <a id="5763234f0b004f62bb8a38ff8d3b8c27" />Hydraulic Resistance of the Outlet Channel

    <a id="d89588b027694cf988ed4ce4bb3a567d" />

    $$
    \begin{aligned} R_O &= \frac{28.4\,\cdot \mu  \cdot L}{h^{4}} \\[6pt] &= \frac{28.4\,(0.001\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s})(0.0015\ \mathrm{m})}{(0.0005\ \mathrm{m})^{4}} \\[6pt] &\approx 6.8 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{\frac{Pa\cdot s}{m^{3}}} \end{aligned}
    $$

    <a id="9d59b9077b3d4ff8a2972e3cda8cd2e1" />The outlet channel is therefore 3 times more resistive than the main channel.

    <a id="e954d6836c7d44f4aae637183a971226" />

    $$
    \frac{R_{O}}{R_{M}} \approx 2.56
    $$

    <a id="ea289c5a57b647d2b5b942a22ff6f44d" />To split the flow throughout the wells based on our hydraulic resistance calculation, we can set up an equivalent circuit with all of the main and outlet channel resistances listed

    <a id="bc71177381924ad0985320b787706a41" />

    <Figure imgs={[{"url":"https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/232c67c2e8b0515c9f489eb3256a8e2f.avif","alt":"","scale":0}]}>
      <Fragment slot="content">
        <a id="153df3f2488a47b0b5fadc59e0b27b6e" />Figure 23: Resistor network circuit parallel for the output section of the microfluidic chip
      </Fragment>
    </Figure>

    <a id="f4af36e3655843f9ac7287ff3c0f90c3" />We used this to solve for equivalent resistance in this portion of the chip.

    <a id="d6bda4ff0b7a4e67bf3e2a089ba035b0" />

    $$
    R_{T}=3.13 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{\frac{Pa\cdot s}{m^{3}}}
    $$

    <a id="d4b20675bce64540a8e885d02441b0a1" />Based on this value, we then solved for the flow rate going into this channel as a function of pressure drop

    <a id="045ba5f3b4a84ff489ae1b95e9cea588" />

    $$
    Q=\frac{\Delta p}{R_{T}}=3.19\cdot10^{-9}\cdot\Delta p
    $$

    <a id="4c33376da8e748999337dc051ea7ae6e" />From here, we can solve the resistor system for the pressure drop and flow rate over all of the wells for an arbitrary pressure difference.

    <a id="c36f2abb2b8d44a6b98a3542953c190d" />

    $$
    \begin{gathered} \def\arraystretch{1.6} \begin{array}{|c|c|c|} \hline \textbf{Well} & \textbf{Flow Rate} & \textbf{Pressure Drop} 
    \\ \hline 1 & 1.47\cdot10^{-9} \cdot\Delta p & \Delta p 
    \\ 2 & 7.94\cdot10^{-10 }\cdot\Delta p & 5.41\cdot\Delta p
    \\ 3 & 4.30\cdot10^{-10} \cdot\Delta p & 2.93\cdot\Delta p 
    \\ 4 & 2.35\cdot10^{-10} \cdot\Delta p & 0.160\cdot\Delta p 
    \\ 5 & 1.31\cdot10^{-10} \cdot\Delta p & 0.0892\cdot\Delta p 
    \\ 6 & 7.83\cdot10^{-11} \cdot\Delta p & 0.0534\cdot\Delta p 
    \\ 7 & 5.63\cdot10^{-11} \cdot\Delta p & 0.0384\cdot\Delta p \\ \hline \end{array} \end{gathered}
    $$

    <a id="b0eff463712f430bbab13a4c6e5ad21a" />From this, we are able to calculate the difference between the largest and smallest pressure drops along the wells

    <a id="d0709ac477954a38b64ed6d727657c4a" />

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    Difference &=\frac{P_{O1}-P_{O7}}{\frac{P_{O1}+P_{O7}}{2}}\cdot100\% \\[6pt]
    &=\frac{(1-0.0384)\Delta p} {\frac{(1+0.0384)\Delta p}{2}} \cdot 100\% \\[6pt] &= 185\%
    \end{aligned}
    $$

    <a id="e9e43a2589a44bb7a59d57d69300fc06" />Therefore, the relative difference between the first well and the last well is 185%. We can assume this relationship holds up for fluid output as well due to the direct relationship between pressure and flow. With the given parameters, we can see that there will be a noticeable difference between separate wells, however it is still worth testing the chips capability to deliver fluids to all the channels.

    <a id="407389a39a894e2d81d43006702b676d" />Furthermore, to verify the mathematical model above, we ran a fluid flow simulation. The objective was observe flow behavior at the millimeter scale, and confirm that channel size did not matter due to great resistance.

    <a id="991d707969a84b46ad0491e8d583c46d" />First, we made a simple microfluidics model to verify that the simulation parameters would yield a sensible result.

    <a id="1d86e5d4d66c46bc91abc2149d9a9bf8" />Then, we proceeded with the fluid mixing simulation. This involved the following assumptions and parameters:

    1. <a id="bd22d7711242427685e67ab414b95a8d" />Microfluidics chip is filled with even concentrations of fluids A and B.
    2. <a id="e8d51f3f569c46cab3fa07a35475e49c" />Both fluids are aqueous and represented using water. Both having the same viscoelastic properties.
    3. <a id="2760aad8d7e14bf49d5fe9b69982d886" />Inlet flow rate is 0.001 kg/min
    4. <a id="0df36d8872a746f9a0b22cb75ab8c539" />Outlet pressure is set at atmospheric ambient temperature and pressure (20 °C and 1 atm).
