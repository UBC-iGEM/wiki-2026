# <a id="3f9d65dd82be8075b6b2f8a74de1c8fd" />Principles of Fluorescence

<a id="3f9d65dd82be8050867be53a9a577c19" />Fluorescence is critical to many experiments and research applications, such as flow cytometry, fluorescence immunoassays, and more. Fluorescence helps us identify cellular structures, quantify colonies, and even detect cell susceptibility to antibiotics.

<a id="3fad65dd82be80059b4bf8b57504d9bd" />Cells fluoresce when a fluorophore absorbs light energy (a photon), causing its electrons to transition from the ground state (s0) to an excited state (s1). As some energy is lost during this process, the emitted photon carries less energy and therefore has a longer wavelength, as shown in Figure 1. This phenomenon is known as the Stokes shift <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"sandersonFluorescenceMicroscopy2014"}]} />  This indicates that when blue light is absorbed, green light of lower energy may be emitted, producing fluorescence.

# <a id="3f9d65dd82be8054b0a7da2a0caffc89" />Motivation for Fluorescence Detector

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80ef9515d6d95b2206a7" />Our microfluidic chips primarily utilize fluorescence to provide quantitative information on antibiotic resistance, enabling rapid preliminary screening before further assessments are conducted. However, we realized that detecting fluorescence still requires equipment that may be difficult to access in hospital settings, contradicting our goal of rapid, point-of-care detection. To address this limitation, we further refined our design through iterative development and brainstorming, eventually deciding to create a complementary fluorescence detector.

## <a id="3f9d65dd82be80b4bb54d4d68e2b0c45" />Conventional vs. Our Fluorescence Detector

<a id="3fad65dd82be8060b66ec7bc55f0ed97" />To identify the limitations of existing fluorescence detectors, we conducted preliminary market research. Based on our findings, fluorescence detection equipment can range in price from hundreds of dollars (i.e. \$630.39 <Cite refs={[{"n":4,"key":"DarkBoxUV"}]} /> ) to tens of thousands of dollars (i.e. \$42191 <Cite refs={[{"n":5,"key":"ltdFluorescenceSpectrophotometerNFS100"}]} /> ). Such costs make it financially impractical to equip every clinician with a fluorescence detector used only for preliminary screening. Furthermore, although both handheld and benchtop detectors are available on the market, their designs are not compatible with our microfluidic chips.

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80868e51fbc00a29dc5b" />To improve the accessibility of our point-of-care detection system, we incorporated several design features into our customized fluorescence detector: a holder tailored to our microfluidic chip, a user-friendly interface, a simplified fluorescence detection circuit to reduce material costs, and an innovative component assembly method that ensures both effective light shielding and ease of use.

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80519f6edb341fb10bad" />With our customized fluorescence detector, we aim to make antibiotic resistance screening accessible, fast, and affordable.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80ed88efe69ee7e79de6" />Before beginning the design of our fluorescence detector, we researched previous iGEM projects to explore existing approaches and achievements.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be806d9421fc5bc19b0502" />Team Munich’s 2017 design inspired us with its compact fluorescence detector, which used magnets to sandwich the detection components between 3D-printed parts <Cite refs={[{"n":6,"key":"TeamMunich2017igemorg"}]} />. This approach demonstrated the potential of magnets to improve user-friendliness and provide a convenient, efficient assembly method.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80a8a6bdca626dbf5c1e" />As demonstrated in the 3D model, the detector is divided into two halves: Part A and Part B. Part A serves as the base, housing a sliding track for the microfluidic holder and the circuit components responsible for fluorescence detection. Part B sits on top of Part A to form a completely dark enclosure. It also houses the illumination system and user interface for active control over the on and off of the light.

<a id="3fed65dd82be803da560c12777722089" />So, how exactly do our magnets improve the design's functionality?

<a id="3fed65dd82be809d8589f767f9e2b9c8" />**1. Open, lay flat, and access with ease**

<a id="3fed65dd82be80cd9a94f69c71cb51dc" />The two halves can be separated and laid completely flat on your workspace, allowing you to:

- <a id="3fed65dd82be80efb0f0f09b77c0ffb6" />Load the microfluidics with ease.
- <a id="3fed65dd82be80f7a02cda04a4ab29ae" />Freely interact with the integrated switch and other user-interface components without having to maneuver in a tight space.
- <a id="3fed65dd82be8087b344d76601de356d" />Clean and maintain the device with minimal hassle.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80e0aa56fa81ee7a3cc5" />**2. Snap and seal the detector and get a dark enclosure**

<a id="3fed65dd82be80868213e7b69574fcf4" />The magnets allow the two halves to snap together securely, this means no more:

- <a id="3fed65dd82be80969b81f14cf3fd789d" />Figuring out complex fitting mechanisms.
- <a id="3fed65dd82be80298259d4d1273dbaad" />Spending time on lengthy assembly and disassembly.
- <a id="3fed65dd82be801684fdde993175dce1" />Struggling to keep each component securely in place.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80d7b287ff35797612c5" />Our holder is designed to fit the microfluidic chip snugly, minimizing unwanted movement and ensuring reliable fluorescence readings. The extruded legs on the bottom of the holder fit into the sliding tracks, allowing it to move smoothly through the detector.

<a id="3fed65dd82be807e9ff2c8522bfd5408" />How does the user know when a well reaches the detection site?

<a id="3fed65dd82be80839acdfd0f06dc12e2" />We incorporated two intuitive cues to guide the user through the detection process:

- <a id="3fed65dd82be800fa35de029d6f52cdd" />**Tactile feedback:** Small bumps on the sliding track create gentle vibrations as the holder moves past them, notifying users when a well reaches the detection site and when to pause for fluorescence measurement.
- <a id="3fed65dd82be8002b4e3e543679f33d7" />**Visual indicators:** After the plate was printed, white lines were added on the holder's handle to mark the positions of individual wells. For example, when the second white line reaches the detector's wall, the second well is aligned with the fluorescence detection site.

<a id="3fed65dd82be804bb7c4fa0859d4fe48" />Together, these features make it easy to navigate between wells and complete fluorescence detection efficiently in a single session.

## <a id="3fbd65dd82be8055acb5c9d629e47f52" />Improved Detection Workflow

<a id="3ffd65dd82be8062bcf8d34a47219af3" />

<Figure imgs={[{"url":"https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/66115acffef1536f86d8d2cfab18a6e9.avif","alt":"","scale":0}]}>

  </Fragment>
</Figure>

<a id="3ffd65dd82be801889e2f3464c5d7594" />We studied the mechanisms behind conventional fluorescence detectors and simplified their designs to achieve the same functionality at a significantly lower cost.

<a id="3ffd65dd82be8035a5f9e396356a6726" />Most fluorescence detectors share a similar optical layout, with the illumination source, sample, and detection component arranged at 90° angles to one another. This configuration helps improve signal quality by minimizing background noise and reducing interference from the excitation light.<Cite refs={[{"n":3,"key":"DetectionMadeEasy2026"}]} /> . Excitation filters and emission filters permit a narrow band of wavelength, such that only the desired lights are filtered through and shone on the sample or detector <Cite refs={[{"n":7,"key":"ThorlabsFiltersFluorescence"}]} />.

<a id="3ffd65dd82be80ecb8d7d2d58e0d76a9" />While this conventional setup is widely adopted, one major limitation is the high cost of optical filters. Both excitation and emission filters must selectively transmit specific wavelength ranges, requiring precise optical properties that drive up their prices. Our research found that a single excitation filter can cost around \$300.

<a id="3ffd65dd82be80219df7e3300525a978" />To address this limitation, we identified the essential function of each component and reengineered the conventional detection workflow into a simpler, more cost-effective design. Retaining only the essential components, our customized detector uses a single-color LED positioned directly above the sample, with a long-pass filter and photodetector placed directly below it.

<a id="3ffd65dd82be807c95aee61f23aa9368" />The function of each component is explained in detail in the *Design Breakdown* section. Overall, by eliminating the need for two conventional optical filters, our design reduces material costs by at least 50%. Its compact configuration also minimizes the detector's size, allowing for better integration with our microfluidic system. <Cite refs={[{"n":8,"key":"DichroicLongpassFilters"}]} /> .

## <a id="3ffd65dd82be80cc86e3e2490f4ea650" />Build Process

<a id="3ffd65dd82be80b4b731ca3f0a41104a" />We began with a literature review of existing fluorescence detectors to understand their underlying mechanisms and the complexity of their designs. We found that most conventional detectors consist of several key components:

- <a id="3ffd65dd82be805a9e74c6f98d3ea731" />A light source, usually an LED or laser of a specific wavelength <Cite refs={[{"n":9,"key":"HowBuildFluorometer"},{"n":10,"key":"circuitsarduinoArduinoFluorometer"}]} />.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be80e3becbdbe342292d4e" />An excitation filter, to remove all light except the excitation wavelength from hitting the sample <Cite refs={[{"n":9,"key":"HowBuildFluorometer"}]} />.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be805d99e5fa5f8cda83f7" />A sample holder, to keep the sample still while it is being analyzed <Cite refs={[{"n":9,"key":"HowBuildFluorometer"}]} />.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be8094a6b3ce9d3063b29c" />A dichroic mirror, to split the beam into multiple parts <Cite refs={[{"n":9,"key":"HowBuildFluorometer"}]} />.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be809c83d5e47b297ba3b8" />An emission filter, to remove all light except that emitted from the sample <Cite refs={[{"n":9,"key":"HowBuildFluorometer"}]} />.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be80d2bac4ea9890f9f37a" />A detector, often a photomultiplier tube or silicone photodetector, to quantify the response electronically <Cite refs={[{"n":9,"key":"HowBuildFluorometer"}]} />.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be8021abb7faf3af4f973d" />A circuit to process the detected signal, often including an op-amp for amplification <Cite refs={[{"n":10,"key":"circuitsarduinoArduinoFluorometer"}]} />.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be8049a58beace456a37af" />A microcontroller, to analyze the results and report them to the user <Cite refs={[{"n":10,"key":"circuitsarduinoArduinoFluorometer"}]} />.

<a id="3ffd65dd82be80248f77e6ecd4e62570" />Since our goal was simply to determine the presence of fluorescence rather than perform sophisticated quantitative analysis, we recognized that our detector did not need to match the complexity of commercial systems. By identifying and removing non-essential components, we could significantly reduce both cost and design complexity while still meeting our detection needs.

### <a id="3ffd65dd82be80b1b88ce03295f17557" />Previous iGEM Detectors

<a id="3ffd65dd82be805a8a25e8c9ae503b0b" />We then reviewed fluorescence detectors developed by previous iGEM teams to explore how others had approached the design challenge.

#### <a id="3ffd65dd82be80dcb5b6ffe2b376d82f" />Munich 2017 <Cite refs={[{"n":6,"key":"TeamMunich2017igemorg"}]} />

<a id="3ffd65dd82be804a912edeed2892774e" />The Munich team used a blue LED and a photoresistor in their design, notably without incorporating any optical filters. However, their detection approach differed from our needs, as they were targeting different wavelengths. They used an Arduino Nano as their microcontroller, while our team used an Arduino Uno from 2025 that we could repurpose.

<a id="3ffd65dd82be8073ab44cea9fce1efcc" />Ultimately, we found limited overlap between their circuitry and our design requirements. We were concerned that a photoresistor would not provide sufficient sensitivity to detect fluorescence from the small volume of bacteria in our microfluidic chips, leading us to explore alternative detection components.

## <a id="3ffd65dd82be806ca46ee6779c342722" />Defining the Needs

<a id="3ffd65dd82be8062a879f6ca2101bc93" />We began the design process by defining the essential functions of our fluorescence detector and the performance criteria it should meet. Since the bacteria had not yet been transformed at this stage of the project, we lacked the experimental data needed to establish meaningful quantitative requirements. We therefore adopted qualitative design needs as an initial guide, allowing us to refine our requirements as the project progressed.

- <a id="3ffd65dd82be80ebac46e47a1168b8bd" />The detector's interior must minimize interference from external light to ensure reliable fluorescence detection.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be80c8bf16faf881e9ed7a" />The microfluidic chip must fit inside the device and be easily put in and brought out.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be8056a2bffea2add0e71a" />The sensor must be able to detect when there is GFP fluorescence occurring.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be8087b1dfeb693704459f" />The device must be smaller than a commercially available fluorescence detector.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f59090db5a66f33ecd" />The device must have a microcontroller which communicates the results to the user.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be80c98fb4d2a583d4b047" />The device must have a means of separating emitted light from excitation light.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f594defe0d82e7b3b8" />The device must be able to generate light at the correct wavelength.
- <a id="3ffd65dd82be80519a92e0e0d0856b0e" />The device must be able to differentiate between different wells.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f69f21d3443e776b56" />

    V_{TIA} =I_p \cdot R_2 \\
    V_{TIA} = 18 nA \cdot 1 MQ \\
    V_{TIA} = 18.0 mV
    $$

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8069a8e4e7994ed5f134" />V\_GAIN: This voltage node follows a non-inverting amplifier, meaning it multiplies V\_TIA by the value set by R5 (our potentiometer) and R3. The op-amp will adjust it’s output until the R5/R3 divider feeds back a voltage equal to V\_TIA.

    Gain = 1 + \frac{R_5}{R_3} \\
    Gain = 1 + \frac{20 k\Omega}{20 k\Omega} \\
    Gain = 2 \\
    $$

    V_{GAIN}=Gain \cdot V_{TIA} \\
    V_{GAIN}= 2 \cdot 18 mV \\
    V_{GAIN} = 36.0 mV
    $$

    ARD\_IN = V_{GAIN}+V_F \\
    ARD\_IN = 36 mV + 0.69 V \\
    ARD\_IN \approx0.726 V
    $$

  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80a8b320cf3178e0f17e" />All of the changes to the fluorescence detection circuit stem from two primary differences between the setup from the paper and our setup. These differences are the supply voltage rails and analog to digital conversion. We also use an op-amp designed for photodiode amplification that meets our system requirements, simplify the circuit by removing two unnecessary components, and better account for the sensitivity of the initial signal. The circuit was made using Altium Designer, a PCB design software and was manufactured through JLCPCB.

    1. <a id="3ffd65dd82be80d79957da9e7f773e74" />Adds reference voltage to account for unipolar supply and ensure no signal is lost due to clipping 0V rail
    2. <a id="3ffd65dd82be80ada213f169004e60f1" />Adds capacitors to help reduce noise picked up from USB cable and environment
    3. <a id="3ffd65dd82be8000b691db547e65f184" />Define changes between research paper setup and our setup
       1. <a id="3ffd65dd82be808c8dddce540e78d1cf" />Using an arduino,
          1. <a id="3ffd65dd82be800d8e6be9a633eea284" />building circuit around its rails +5V/0V
          2. <a id="3ffd65dd82be8008ac18f674e8dc0afa" />using the arduino’s internal reference voltage of 1.1V to improve resolution and reduce magnitude of gain needed to reach level of resolution (Calculations below)

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8021beb6d900c6221fee" />ADC Step Size:

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be807b85a3e9bbf9581391" />

    $$
    5V \text{ ref: } \frac{5V}{1024} = 4.88mV \text{ per count} \\
    1.1V \text{ ref: } \frac{1.1V}{1024} = 1.074 mV \text{ per count} \\
    $$

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80bba9fccf29be43f66f" />Gain needed for 1 pA = 1 count (4.5x finer)

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8050b962d1ecd6e5d3fc" />

    $$
    G = \frac{\text{step size}}{I \cdot R_F} \\
    5V \text{ ref: } G = \frac{4.88 mV}{10 \mu V} \\
    G = 488 \\
    1.1V \text{ ref: } G = \frac{1.074 mV}{10 \mu V} \\
    G = 107
    $$

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80abb1b8efc61340ca3b" />With the 1.1 V reference, we need less gain, which is to our advantage, as greater gain introduces more noise.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8022a8f3f7ee34d61006" />In comparison with the circuit from the paper that are no longer needed in our set up:

    1. <a id="3ffd65dd82be80bc8730e37eecbf2f93" />The input bias resistor

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be806bac70d823204be932" />

    <Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/86e8fef260ad566a9a7813eccc47d7ec.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80d6a89ee5c09bf12194" />The resistor is of equal value to R2 such that the small input bias current drawn into each input of the op-amp is now equal, and thus they cancel out, removing most of the error this has on the TIA gain output. Our new op-amp (AD8606) has 1pA max input bias current; which is much smaller than the OP07 op-amps from V1 which had 7nA max. Before this would effect the signal to noise ratio whereas now it is smaller than the signal itself and negligible

    1. <a id="3ffd65dd82be8074bde1c12ae3ba2e57" />The clipping diode

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be807dab43c3ad7175c196" />

    <Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/048c96c0455b5180b9d5e2102c6768d6.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8051905fcd0d03c88255" />The previous circuit was designed for voltage rails of +/-9V, which exceeds the input voltage range for the ADC they were using. The clipping diode works to override the incoming voltage from the gain stage if it exceeds 5V and replaces it with a steady 5V signal from the +5V voltage rail on the circuit. It essentially serves as protection for the ADC. In our case, our voltage rails for the circuit are equivalent to the rails on our ADC, being the Arduino itself, and therefore we no longer have to have a mechanism to cut off the voltage from exceeding +5V before entering the ADC on the Arduino.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8027bd1bfebb7d47dec5" />We must also choose a replacement op-amp.  We are looking for a replacement op-amp that better suits our setup since the OP07 op-amp used in the original circuit is not compatible.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be809f8696e9e69a58e912" />Requirements:

    - <a id="3ffd65dd82be80c4a789f9679ae8171a" />It needs to run on a single 5V supply, so we can power it from the arduino
    - <a id="3ffd65dd82be8033973dc51c9f7a539f" />It has to have rail to rail input and output voltage capabilities, since our signal starts off very small we need to be able to work with signals near 0V
    - <a id="3ffd65dd82be80bd91b5cbf7426a8a36" />Minimize input bias current and input offset voltage, as the larger these values are the more artifacts the op-amp will add into our signal

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f0a29ae9c4ab99b77c" />An op-amp designed with photodiode amplification in mind was found and it met our requirements. Below is a table comparing both the OP07 from the original circuit design, and the AD8606 we are using in our revised circuit.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8055802bfa6670947316" />

    |                                                                      |                    |                                  |
    | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | -------------------------------- |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be80d59e8ce4ea61fe2ae5" />Spec                      | OP07C (V1)         | AD8606 (V2)                      |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be80b9bb41e74e64ce91a6" />Supply                    | ±3V to ±18V        | 2.7 V to 5.5 V                   |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be8026bc44e3e07d05b388" />Input bias current @ 25 C | ±1.8 nA            | 0.2 pA, 1 pA max                 |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be80929742c1514309b085" />Input offset voltage      | 60 μV, 150 μV max  | 80μV, 300 μV max                 |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be80fcab07d41cc29b80cf" />Input common-mode range   | ±13 V min on ±15 V | 0 to 5 V (rail to rail)          |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be80ae9a34f4648a768128" />Output swing              | ± 12.8 V on ±15 V  | 4.98V high, 20 mV low (1mA load) |

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f491a7e2bd3dee3c78" />The AD8606 operates within our supply voltage range, has smaller input bias current, and operates with rail to rail input and output voltage ranges. It also includes two op-amps in one physical IC allowing us to use less components. Its only caveat is that its input offset voltage is larger, and as such we are addressing this limitation of the op-amp through implementing a reference voltage throughout our op-amp circuitry.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80c1a9e3f836bd17fde0" />We must also change our reference voltage to increase resolution.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8026a52fd5cff022e67b" />The input bias voltage is a small voltage offset on the inputs of the op-amp that can be positive or negative. If this offset is negative, the first part of our incoming signal will not be detected since it will exist below 0V, and the signal must reach 0V before it can be observed in the output signal. This can have a significant impact on the performance of the fluorescence detection since the photocurrent is so small to begin with.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80cb901ed3804a20a472" />The reference voltage is created using two resistors to form a voltage divider between the +5V and 0V rails on the circuit. Our objective with this reference voltage is to counteract the input offset voltage of the op-amp, and as such we want a small positive voltage offset. On the other hand we also want to leave as much headroom for reading the signal as possible, as our current range is 0V to 1.1V and we are cutting into that usable range.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be803884acfad809cb3ca9" />We decided to go with a reference voltage of \~0.2V to balance the headroom for the usable voltage for the signal, to make sure we clear the op-amp input offset voltage, and to be able to create the reference voltage using standard resistor values.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8070a80dc0d22f1995fe" />Voltage divider calculation, where RA is the top resistor and RB is the bottom resistor:

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80bfb408e54635f59a09" />

    $$
    V_{REF} = V_{in}\cdot\frac{R_B}{R_A+R_B} \\
    0.22 V = 5 V \cdot \frac{R_B}{R_A+R_B}\\
    \frac{R_B}{R_A+R_B} = 0.044 V \\
    \text{Choose $R_B = 1 K\Omega$} \\
    R_A +  1K\Omega =\frac{1 K\Omega}{0.044} = 22.7 K\Omega\\
    R_A = 22 K\Omega \\
    \text{Check $V_{REF}$}: \\
    V_{REF} = 0.217 V
    $$

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f09c21eb5b50a64724" />This creates the circuit shown in the schematic below:

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be801d9b02dd5843108f31" />

    <Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/b7d3d0e105c15881b5ec94a01f9c67c3.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8008a3eeeaa2ed7e246f" />The +5V rail for the circuit is supplied through the arduino from a laptop, and this power source carries noise with it. To account for this a capacitor is used as a low-pass filter, reducing high frequency noise present on the reference voltage by draining it to ground. Furthermore the +5V rail itself can drift based on what else it is being used for. The solution for this is to measure the reference voltage using the arduino and subtract the signal voltage from it. This way if the reference voltage shifts it will be accurately removed from the signal.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be809e8edec4fed6a8ee38" />This signal processing is as follows:

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be807b9383cc0f3ab1885f" />**Stage 1:** Op-amp A is the transimpedance amplifier to convert the photocurrent into a voltage so the arduino can measure it

    - <a id="3ffd65dd82be80b0a7f4eaf5c0cac753" />Using photovoltaic mode (zero bias voltage) rather than photoconductive mode (reverse bias) to prioritize low dark current for detecting very low light levels while having a slower response time (two ways to configure the circuitry around photodiodes)
    - <a id="3ffd65dd82be8044a475d4a3fb8b9d66" />R1 of 10Mohm to create the conversion of 1pA to 10uV

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8057869dd938b2e7d093" />

    $$
    V_{TIA} = V_{REF}+I_P\cdot R_1\\
    \text{Transimpedance Gain} = \frac{V_{TIA}-V_{REF}}{I_P}=R_1=10M\Omega \\1pA\cdot 10 M\Omega = 10 \mu V \\
    1 nA \cdot 10 M\Omega = 10 mV
    $$

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be802a8777e0f437a553bf" />

    <Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/f54da69b150455719c860d9fb5162234.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be801a8aa4c10a7b621aae" />**Stage 2:** Op-amp B is a **single ended gain amplifier** that applies a programmable gain to the voltage output of op-amp A, increasing the voltage output from the first op-amp to make it more readable by the ADC

    - <a id="3ffd65dd82be8087910fdcbc71efde0f" />Rv and R2 create settable gain ranging from unity to 101
    - <a id="3ffd65dd82be80c59843fa5b11e7e25b" />Show calculation:
      - <a id="3ffd65dd82be808bae76eb77fe2cb1f2" />V\_out formula
      - <a id="3ffd65dd82be80adbf40c31f20950633" />Then gain at min and max setting
      - <a id="3ffd65dd82be8043b5ddcf1e52d7cb2f" />Then small table shows common gain values we can use as starting points based on how much current we get

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80d2a5a7d0afb361f260" />Gain set by Rv and R2. The non-inverting amplifier is referenced to V\_REF

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be809b86f0e29997ea149a" />

    $$
    V_{OUT}=V_{REF}+G \cdot (V_{TIA}-V_{REF})\\
    G= 1 + \frac{R_v}{R_2} \\
    G_{min} = 1 + 0 = 1 \\
    G_{max} = 1 + \frac{1 M\Omega}{10 k\Omega} = 101
    $$

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be806fbf7ad7f6da23dfb5" />

    |                                                     |      |
    | --------------------------------------------------- | ---- |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be8095a075c5f1a29b4fee" />Rv       | Gain |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be801d9115d2ce393ddb5a" />0 Ohm    | 1    |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be80afb0f7f6a4fe507783" />10 KOhm  | 2    |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be80e091c4f8910a023af1" />100 KOhm | 11   |
    | <a id="3ffd65dd82be80b7ac47c6b4473b845a" />1 MOhm   | 101  |

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80519cf0c36073b35737" />This calculation helps us use most of the ADC’s resolution across a range of photocurrents. The Arduino measures in steps of about 1 mV. Because the circuit converts photocurrent to voltage using a 10 MΩ resistor and then applies gain, dividing one ADC step by (10 MΩ × gain) gives the smallest detectable current. At a gain of 101, this is about 1 pA. Dividing the usable voltage range of 0.88 V by (10 MΩ × gain) gives the largest current measurable before the reading saturates. Thus, higher gain allows detection of smaller currents but causes saturation at lower currents.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80edbaf3f95dec8a2ca5" />We’ve set the gain so the brightest sample lands near the top of the 0.883 V signal range.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80448849e86201078e3d" />

    <Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/b91d64da20f85969a1d2e375672919f5.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80129024e49ade0de622" />In addition to the capacitor to remove noise from the USB cable, there are capacitors throughout the op-amp circuitry to help reduce environmental high frequency noise. There is a four pin connector to transfer the supply voltage of +5V and ground from the arduino as well as to read two analog signals for the signal from the photodiode after conditioning and the reference voltage. Finally test points are added throughout to be able to probe the circuit at different points to make testing easier.

    2. <a id="3ffd65dd82be80869ba1fa019086c226" />Intentional placement of components the user interacts with to ensure they are easily accessible and convenient to use

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80e495e1fb04e5a8a45e" />We now move into the construction of an additional LED PCB so that the position can be precisely controlled.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80719ca8ce2e5fc78edb" />The LED circuit is much simpler compared to the fluorescence detector circuit. Its purpose is to control the current that flows through the LED to ensure safe operation of the LED. Otherwise unregulated current can exceed the 50mA maximum (cite datasheet) it can handle and possibly damage the LED. The LEDs typical operating current is 20mA with a forward voltage drop of 3.2V (cite datasheet).

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8033ba58ef294854ed01" />To determine how much resistance is needed we calculate the voltage remaining.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8008bf71c96388a7495d" />

    $$
    V_{PIN}=V_R +V_F \\
    5V = V_R + 3.2 V \\
    V_R = 5V - 3.2 V = 1.8V
    $$

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80678c2cc78e8ee9f724" />And divide this by the target current using Ohm’s Law.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80ed9e60c8338798dace" />

    $$
    R = \frac{V_R}{I} \\
    R = \frac{1.8 V}{0.020 A}
    R = 90 \Omega
    $$

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8042aa76e65607f8ae5d" />This resistance is rounded to 100 Ω as the nearest standard value we can use while being on the safe side with providing just under 20mA to the LED.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be8059b1b6dacc65c30729" />

    $$
    I_F = \frac{5V -3.2V}{100 \Omega}=18mA
    $$

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80c6b29fcc3011245616" />Finally a two header pin connector is used to send out a digital signal from the arduino using pulse width modulation so we can control the brightness of the LED along with the ground. Pulse width modulation is a technique where digital signals are rapidly turned on and off to simulate analog signals, allowing us to provide variable current levels to the LED rather than only 18mA or 0.

<details>
  <summary><a id="3fdd65dd82be80f6938fed3a667dc917" />Illumination</summary>

  <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f7b246d7a7eeccb673" />We selected a single-color blue LED with a peak wavelength of 488 nm, serving as a simplified alternative to the conventional light source and excitation filter combination. By emitting light within a narrow wavelength range, the LED helps reduce unwanted excitation wavelengths and improve signal quality.

  <a id="3ffd65dd82be80c69057f5b63cdcac1d" />Blue light was chosen because its relatively short wavelength corresponds to higher photon energy, making it suitable for exciting fluorescent molecules such as GFP. <details>

  <a id="3fdd65dd82be805abe67fcdc440f1f0b" />We selected a 500 nm long-pass filter to serve as our emission filter. As its name suggests, a long-pass filter blocks light at shorter wavelengths while allowing light at longer wavelengths to pass through. In our design, the filter suppresses the 488 nm blue excitation light while allowing the green fluorescence emitted by GFP, with an emission wavelength of approximately 510 nm, to reach the photodetector. This helps minimize interference from the excitation source and improve the reliability of fluorescence detection.
</details>

# <a id="3fbd65dd82be8034992cdf993e9976df" />Improvements for Future Iterations

<a id="3fcd65dd82be8016a5bddf3afc0838d6" />We plan to continue exploring the potential of our customized fluorescence detector. The following are some of our next steps:

<Callout icon="⚙️">
  <a id="3fed65dd82be80048fcde5b4d928cd07" />Implement a fully automated detection process by incorporating a rack-and-pinion mechanism, with the rack attached to the bottom of the chip holder and the pinion positioned directly beneath it. A DC motor will precisely control the pinion’s rotation, allowing the chip to move a fixed distance with each turn and ensuring consistent positioning during detection. Additionally, all detection results, indicating the presence or absence of fluorescence, will be automatically uploaded to a connected computer, allowing users to focus on other tasks while the system operates and retrieve the complete results at any time.
</Callout>

<Callout icon="🕶️">
  <a id="3fed65dd82be80f0b1f7ed79d49040c0" />Further improve the enclosure’s light-blocking capabilities by testing materials other than PLA to identify alternatives with better light-blocking properties. Additionally, incorporate baffles and ridges into the enclosure design to minimize light leakage and reduce stray light, improving the reliability and accuracy of fluorescence detection.
