# <a id="3f0d65dd82be80adb95cf1fb11123097" />Introduction & Overview of the Bioreactor

<a id="3f0d65dd82be8013b7c9c45b65fea97b" />A bioreactor is a vessel that contains a biological reaction; in our case, *E. coli* growt&#x68;*.* This vessel is meant to create an ideal environment where the organisms thrive. To create an ideal environment, one must control the temperature, pH, aeration, agitation, and supply of nutrients <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"BioreactorOverviewScienceDirect"}]} />. Bioreactors are ideal over traditional shaking culture plates, as they allow you to create a more uniform environment during culturing and between different culturing sessions. When there is variation between sessions, you can quantify the difference, as you can compare the change in pH, temperature, etc., over time <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"BioreactorOverviewScienceDirect"}]} />.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be8052b407e9132156ecbf" />Why for this project?

<a id="3fad65dd82be80e39714f6e9569d58c4" />We developed a bioreactor for several reasons. At the outset, we were unsure how many biosensor strains we would develop for our proof of concept. In the largest scenario, we would have five strains to culture separately. Because all strains would produce the same fluorescence and could therefore be analyzed using the same circuitry, we would need to run five shaker flasks at once, taking up considerable space in our 37°C room or on our bench. Bioreactors would let us use vertical space, keep cultures outside the 37°C room, off the bench, and closely monitor growth to optimize conditions and increase yield <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"CharacterizationFeasibilityMiniaturized"},{"n":3,"key":"hsuAdvancedMicroscaleBioreactor2012"},{"n":4,"key":"obomOperationBenchtopBioreactor2013"}]} />. This would provide enough bacteria for both the hardware and wet lab teams to use in their experiments using a comparably simple process.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be80b9b69ce42379b3fb66" />Identifying Needs

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80748a68f78586adfb55" />Last year, the 2025 UBC iGEM team successfully created a lab-top bioreactor; however, this endeavour resulted in a surplus of supplies, which we took advantage of this year. For example, we adopted our vessel from a leftover mason jar from 2025 and reused several different sensors. As a sub-team, we set an internal goal of increasing the quality of our manufacturing and functionality compared to last year. With our goals in mind, the following list of needs was created:

<a id="3fcd65dd82be8077b85fff335d12e5df" />While these needs are a helpful guide when considering what ideas to incorporate into our bioreactor, they do not allow us to compare components against each other in an objective way. If both a magnetic stir bar and an impeller are able to create agitation, how do we compare them against each other? As such, we translated certain needs into quantitative comparisons of functionality which we refer to as requirements.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be804a9c2ec6ef3e456461" />Translating Needs to Requirements

<a id="3fcd65dd82be800191f5f6176750b020" />To generate our requirements, we took our needs and added quantitative metrics. Certain needs, such as the requirement for a mason jar as a vessel, were binary and, as such, were not translated into needs.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be80079833f4eabe80eb2b" />Iteration 1

    <a id="3f9d65dd82be8023b7b3e7264488d2d2" />The goal behind building iteration 1 was to create a simple, operational version of the bioreactor to demonstrate that the core elements are functional. The elements incorporated include: aeration, agitation, and sampling. This iteration was intended to be a rapid prototype, aiming to expose any design flaws from the start before complex hardware is added. The prototype not only provides a baseline on the foundation of the design, but it also proves that a simple and compact bioreactor can be constructed without the need of highly technical equipment. A system that can be built without much space, specialized equipment, or technical support, is one that other teams can realistically reproduce and build on, thus contributing back to the iGEM community. The value also extends beyond the competition. If a working bioreactor can be assembled from common parts and a desktop 3D printer, rural areas and clinics that wish to implement the full FAST device can cultivate *E.     <a id="3f9d65dd82be80aeaef4c798154184b3" />**What worked:**

    - <a id="3f9d65dd82be8041b30ec33319fb9b8c" />The overall structure held together and accommodated the aeration, agitation, and sampling components in a single, compact assembly.
    - <a id="3f9d65dd82be8069b9d1c8d3690c7ec4" />All off-the-shelf parts were sourced from easy-to-access manufacturers, such as McMaster-Carr. Unique components were fabricated in-house via FDM manufacturing with a Bambu Lab P1S 3D printer.
    - <a id="3f9d65dd82be8005af2ded3ffa81a8c6" />Sampling proved to work and was a simple and effective process. No leakage was observed during the process of extracting a small volume and transferring to a cuvette. This process, including OD measurement time, took roughly 4 minutes.
    - <a id="3f9d65dd82be80b0bd22ec6f48bc5cf7" />The electronics compartment occupied nearly the same footprint as the mason jar vessel. No tipping occurred during experimentation, suggesting that the center of gravity was not offset by the electronics.

    <a id="3f9d65dd82be80d89fb2cfc2b8a8ff52" />**What did not work:**

    - <a id="3f9d65dd82be80fab39cdff01bdb176c" />There was a bit of leakage observed at the lid-to-cap interface, specifically where the 3D-printed lid met the threads on the mason jar. This resulted in small bits of solution leaking out when testing the vessel with water. Although this issue was mitigated during experimentation by sealing the edges with Parafilm, it is not a long-term solution that should be kept in further iterations. Leakage is a significant concern for a vessel intended to hold a bacterial culture, since it risks both contamination of the bacteria and the operator.
    - <a id="3f9d65dd82be809e90edf65ac071505c" />While all electronic components fit inside the electronics compartment, the interior was rather tight, making it difficult to maneuver components. This design is usable for iteration, as it has sufficient space for the level of complexity observed in iteration 1, but it could pose a problem for further versions that will incorporate increasingly more parts. Modularity is a key focus of the project, thus, it is vital that space does not become a roadblock for the objective.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be80beb517cedfe4b9429c" />Iteration 2

<a id="3fdd65dd82be8061bf4fe7be245cafaa" />

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80d48848ef2da17f6c9c" />The goal behind iteration 2 was to build on the foundation of iteration 1 by addressing the limitations and introducing the first monitoring components of the bioreactor. A temperature probe and a pH probe were added, along with an Arduino microcontroller to read outputs from them. The core features from iteration 1 were carried over unchanged so that the effect of the new additions could be assessed on their own. Temperature and pH are two of the main conditions that determine how well *E. Coli* grows, so measuring them gives users confidence that the culture is growing as intended and that environmental conditions are kept most suitable for growth. The pH probe in particular requires correct calibration and handling to give reliable readings, so a standard operating procedure (SOP) was developed for its use. This makes the measurement repeatable for any operator, regardless of laboratory experience, which supports the goal of a bioreactor that others can build and operate independently.

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be80648f96f1d55814b4b0" />Reassessment of the design was conducted against the following questions, while still preserving the points from iteration 1:

    - <a id="3fdd65dd82be8067a56acf822bf0cdcf" />Ease of use: Can a user assemble and operate the bioreactor without difficulty? *(iteration 1)*
    - <a id="3fdd65dd82be8098966ae621d1a3d3f0" />Benchtop standards: Is the bioreactor comparable to the standards and bacterial growth rates observed on a typical laboratory benchtop? *(iteration 1)*
    - <a id="3fdd65dd82be8017a772c4a304b11455" />Modularity: Does the design of the bioreactor consider components to be swapped, added, or adapted in later iterations? *(iteration 1)*
    - <a id="3fdd65dd82be8016a5abe185ba968771" />Monitoring: Can culture conditions be monitored in a way that is practical for the operator to setup and maintain?

    <a id="3fdd65dd82be8027a00ec4b164698aad" />Key components of the bioreactor (most critical to the development of iteration 2) can be described and noted in the photos below:

          <a id="3fdd65dd82be80d9a354d1991bf2aaad" />A pH probe only gives useful readings if it is calibrated correctly, so an SOP was written for the SEN0169 pH probe. The SOP covers calibration, using buffer solutions of pH 4.0 and 7.0.           <a id="3fdd65dd82be8069bd84fff335f1ffd8" />Selection of the SENS0169 pH probe is according to the following:

          - <a id="3fdd65dd82be80ca9f65c70165ece60e" />Measurement range: The probe measures from pH 0 to 14, which fully covers the range needed to monitor *E. Coli* growth, since cultures are generally kept near neutral pH.
          - <a id="3fdd65dd82be80758e97e37aa4545d0f" />Accuracy: The manufacturer lists an accuracy of ±0.1 pH at 25°C, which is sufficient to detect the gradual pH drift that occurs as a culture grows.
          - <a id="3fdd65dd82be80a2a1caf6ec8ed14c08" />Continuous use: The probe is designed for long-term, continuous monitoring, and this was confirmed through the datasheet. It can therefore stay submerged in the vessel for the full length of an experiment, without repeated removal that would add handling steps and contamination risk.
        </div>

        <div class="carousel-desc" data-index="1">
          <a id="3fdd65dd82be802cbd03c12358c1fca1" />Temperature is one of the main conditions that determine how well *E. Coli* grows, so a DS18B20 waterproof digital temperature probe is added to monitor the culture. Unlike the pH probe, the DS18B20 is a digital sensor that reports its reading directly with no additional signal conversion board, and does not require calibration from the operator. For general use of the probe, the procedure follows the manufacturer’s wiki page that comes with the sensor ([https://www.analog.com/media/en/technical-documentation/data-sheets/ds18b20.pdf](https://www.analog.com/media/en/technical-documentation/data-sheets/ds18b20.pdf)).

          <a id="3fdd65dd82be8094bae0d386bc69b483" />Selection of the DS18B20 temperature probe is according to the following:

          - <a id="3fdd65dd82be80ffb97ed9fa0158bcec" />Measurement range: The probe measures from -55 to 125°C, which comfortably covers 37°C, the temperature at which *E. Coli* is usually grown.

# <a id="3f0d65dd82be80a99bf0ea06cce646d7" />Next Steps for the Bioreactor

<a id="3f0d65dd82be8066aba5e6bfe6424685" />While the current bioreactor design is functional and capable of supporting reliable bacterial growth, there are a few features that could be improved for future iterations. The next stage of development is to move from a collection of individual components toward a more integrated and automated system.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be80958451d5cd5f753ef7" />Design Improvements

<Callout icon="💡">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be80748ebdd5a92561ef04" />**Integrated Electronics**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be8099be52e88f90c77d9e" />The current bioreactor uses several independently connected sensors, pumps, and electronic components, resulting in a large number of wires and separate circuits that must be assembled and debugged individually. Future iterations could integrate these systems onto a custom printed circuit board (PCB), with a microcontroller incorporated directly onto the board to control and communicate with each component. Additionally, a Raspberry Pi could be added for data storage, visualization, and increase user-friendly interface. However, adding a Raspberry Pi would not replace the microcontroller’s role in monitoring and controlling real-time systems — it would instead act as a supplementary feature. Overall, integrating the electronics into a single PCB would reduce the hazards of loose wiring and simplify troubleshooting, making the system more reproducible and reliable.
</Callout>

<Callout icon="📡">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be8016b70ac010659f01b3" />**Automated Monitoring and Feedback Control**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be80fca08bc90900d6a04c" />Temperature and pH are currently measured as separate processes, requiring the operator to monitor the system and manually respond when conditions change. These sensors could instead be connected to a central controller that continuously records their measurements and alerts the operator when a parameter shifts outside an acceptable range. An additional improvement would be to introduce feedback control, where the bioreactor automatically responds to these changes. For instance, temperature measurements could control a heating unit, while pH measurements could activate small solution pumps that add acid or base when required. *Feedback control is commonly used in bioreactors to maintain conditions around a desired set point rather than relying on periodic manual adjustments. This is especially critical if the sample bacteria are extremely sensitive to certain environmental parameters.* Not only does this reduce operator involvement, but it maintains a more consistent and steady growth condition throughout the duration of a test.

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be80fa9bd3e89c78adb9a7" />*need to cite*
</Callout>

<Callout icon="🧪">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be80c689dec45dd805161a" />**Automated Optical Density Sampling**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be806282faddf53845c22c" />Optical density (OD) measurements currently require a small culture sample to be manually withdrawn through tubing using a syringe before it can be measured by the custom OD sensor. This process could be automated by incorporating a small peristaltic or syringe pump into the sampling line. At pre-set intervals, the controller could draw a fixed volume of culture through the OD measurement chamber, collect the reading, record the result, return the sample, and then prepare the system for the next measurement. *Automated bioreactor sampling systems have been shown to reduce hands-on work, enable measurements at consistent intervals, and improve replicability compared to manual sampling.* Additionally, this method of sampling maintains a closed system for the bacteria, thus reducing contamination while ensuring biosafety for the operator by adhering to aseptic practices. For this bioreactor, automated sampling would allow a complete growth curve to be recorded as well as decrease operator involvement, thus reducing the chances of human error.

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be802c95d1d142db810ff6" />*need to cite*
</Callout>

<Callout icon="💉">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be80129865d784e25fb13d" />**Improved Sterility and Sampling Design**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be8057a313efa6f01c2839" />As the system becomes increasingly automated, maintaining a closed and clean culture environment becomes more important. Repeated manual sampling introduces additional exposed interfaces and handling steps where contamination can occur. Not only does this increase the risk of cross-contamination of the culture, but it also poses a biosafety risk if the operator does not follow proper protocol. Future versions could include permanent sampling tubing with sealed fittings, along with dedicated sample pathways that minimize the need to open or disconnect the system during an experiment. Examples of these include the use of Luer-Lock syringes and corresponding attachments to tubing — components designed to preserve sterility by maintaining a tight seal between in the inside and outside environments. Reducing the number of open connections would help maintain sterility throughout experimentation while ensuring the sampling process is safer and replicable.
</Callout>

<Callout icon="🛠️">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be8016b07cce72f93b242e" />**Improved Materials and Manufacturing**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be80b585bcf43e20cece99" />Fused deposition modelling (FDM) was well suited for the early stages of rapid prototyping because of its fast manufacturing time and low raw material costs. However, polylactic acid (PLA) based components are more suited for rapid prototyping than permanent long-term use, due to PLA’s weak mechanical structure and lack of durability to heat and environmental stressors. Larger structural components could instead be printed from a tougher and more temperature-resistant material such as polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG) or acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ASA). PETG offers improved temperature and chemical resistance compared with PLA while remaining relatively accessible and easy to manufacture, making it a practical upgrade for many of the current components. Components exposed more directly to the culture, tubing, cleaning solutions, or repeated handling could instead be manufactured from metals such as aluminum 6061 or even stainless steel for high corrosion resistance. Moving toward more durable materials would increase the lifetime of the system and reduce the likelihood of components deforming, cracking, or requiring frequent replacement.
</Callout>
