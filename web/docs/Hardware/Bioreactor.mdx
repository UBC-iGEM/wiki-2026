# <a id="3f0d65dd82be80adb95cf1fb11123097" />Introduction & Overview of the Bioreactor

<a id="3f0d65dd82be8013b7c9c45b65fea97b" />A bioreactor is a vessel that contains a biological reaction; in our case, *E. coli* growt&#x68;*.* This vessel is meant to create an ideal environment where the organisms thrive. To create an ideal environment, one must control the temperature, pH, aeration, agitation, and supply of nutrients <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"BioreactorOverviewScienceDirect"}]} />. Bioreactors are ideal over traditional shaking culture plates, as they allow you to create a more uniform environment during culturing and between different culturing sessions. When there is variation between sessions, you can quantify the difference, as you can compare the change in pH, temperature, etc., over time <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"BioreactorOverviewScienceDirect"}]} />.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be8052b407e9132156ecbf" />Why for this project?

<a id="3fad65dd82be80e39714f6e9569d58c4" />We developed a bioreactor for several reasons. At the outset, we were unsure how many biosensor strains we would develop for our proof of concept. In the most extreme scenario, we would have five strains to culture separately. Because all strains would produce the same fluorescence and could therefore be analyzed using the same circuitry, we would need to run five shaker flasks at once, taking up considerable space in our 37°C room or on our bench. Bioreactors would let us use vertical space, keep cultures outside the 37°C room, off the bench, and closely monitor growth to optimize conditions and increase yield <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"CharacterizationFeasibilityMiniaturized"},{"n":3,"key":"hsuAdvancedMicroscaleBioreactor2012"},{"n":4,"key":"obomOperationBenchtopBioreactor2013"}]} />. This would provide enough bacteria for both the hardware and wet lab teams to use in their experiments using a comparably simple process.

### <a id="3f0d65dd82be80099df1d6502485640f" />Design Decisions

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80a1ab8dd7e495413b60" />When finalizing which design components should appear in the project, several factors were considered. Considering the requirements, sampling method, agitation, and component organization became the options with the widest variety of possible approaches. Most of the concern stemmed from past experiences in working with bioreactors (UBC iGEM 2025) and wanting to learn from what worked and what could be improved. This resulted in the use of weighted decision matrices (WDMs). Each option was scored out of 10 against criteria weighted by importance to the project. Weighted scores were summed, and the highest total was selected. By listing out both the pros and cons of each option, the most objective choice can be selected with engineering reasoning. Other components such as aeration, monitoring systems, and material selection are directly justified.

<Callout icon="🧱">
  <a id="3ffd65dd82be80e2806dc267c2f0133a" />**Material Selection**

  <a id="3ffd65dd82be8020a6bac48c8cf53c99" />PLA is used for the early iterations because it is inexpensive, easy to source, and prints reliably on the Bambu Lab P1S, which allows parts to be reprinted quickly as the design changes. Its main limitations are a low heat tolerance and layer lines that can trap liquid, so PETG is being considered for final iterations because it resists chemicals such as bleach and has better layer adhesion for sealing. A 32 oz glass mason jar is used as the vessel because the same vessel worked in last year’s project, which makes it a proven choice. Glass is also inert to the culture and to cleaning agents, while being inexpensive and easily accessible (and can be substituted with adjacent glassware). The standard design can also allow components to be easily modelled around to well-specified dimensions.
</Callout>

<Callout icon="🗃️">
  <a id="3ffd65dd82be805a8885f393377b60fb" />**Component Organization**

  <a id="3ffd65dd82be8058a739c7107fedf7ec" />The electronics compartment houses the circuit components, so its position affects assembly, stability, and bench space. Placing it on top of the jar incorporates the components directly onto the vessel, which makes assembly easier and takes up less space. A bottom placement is more stable, but it takes up more space and requires components to be wired up to the top of the jar. A side placement makes the housing chunky to fit the cylindrical wall, blocks part of the jar, and requires components to be secured to keep them from falling out. The main concern with the top placement is a high center of gravity, which could cause tipping, but this can easily be mitigated by keeping the footprint of the holder close to that of the jar.

<Callout icon="🧫">
  <a id="3ffd65dd82be8031b826c2d39e7b6bc1" />**Sampling Method**

  <a id="3ffd65dd82be8094b1e8cfd504b8aa67" />Samples must be taken from the culture without compromising sterility. A dedicated tubing path is created, where the culture is exposed to the external environment only when the tube is opened and connected to a pipette. This also allows sampling at a controlled volume for OD measurement. Using a hinge or the entire jar lid would be easiest for the operator, as it gives the largest opening. However, this is not always the best choice, as it exposes a large area of the vessel to the outside environment, thus creating a high risk of contamination to the bacteria. However, using tubing that feeds directly from the vessel interior creates a designated pathway for sampling while maintaining an airtight seal when not in use. Concerns regarding complications with its interaction with the existing jar lid can be easily mitigated by redesigning the lid completely.

<Callout icon="🌪️">
  <a id="3ffd65dd82be80dea994dec25e54a38b" />**Agitation**

  <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f8af9dd1e8150c535b" />Agitation keeps nutrients and oxygen evenly distributed so that all cells have access to them. A magnetic stir bar is already available, is easy to use, and comes in different sizes that provide sufficient agitation. An impeller was considered because it provides more controlled and thorough agitation, but it is hard to assemble, is easy to fall off, and requires an additional motor and a way to secure it to the motor shaft. A custom stir bar could also be designed to the needs of the project, but it is not already available, and its higher center of gravity makes it unstable. A stir bar therefore gives sufficient agitation with the fewest added components. Because it is driven by an external stir plate, it also keeps extra wires and tubing out of the vessel.

<Callout icon="🫧">
  <a id="3ffd65dd82be8037962ac1fbf72204ab" />**Aeration**

  <a id="3ffd65dd82be801094eec55f02c115a3" />*E. Coli* is an aerobic bacterium, so sufficient oxygen must be delivered to all cells for optimal growth. A sparger produces fine bubbles, which give a high surface area to volume ratio for oxygen transfer. A shaker table was considered as an alternative but carries a risk of instability. As circuitry is attached to the bioreactor, continuous motion and vibration could dislodge parts and cause damage. A sparger therefore gives similar results in a controlled, stationary setting.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be8023bf5fca70980e42f3" />Iteration 3

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="3fdd65dd82be801597adec018c23d29a" />A key focus for iteration 3 is the addition of optical density (OD) monitoring. The planned design pairs a manual sample extraction with a sensor made from tubing, a light-tight box, and a syringe, informed by previously published low-cost designs (cite). OD is the standard measure of bacterial growth, so this addition would allow the growth of the culture to be tracked directly from the bioreactor, rather than requiring a spectrophotometer for external measurements. This is important to the project because temperature and pH only describe the conditions around the culture, while OD shows how the bacteria are responding to them. With OD included, the operator can tell whether the conditions in the bioreactor are producing growth, and the growth curves needed for validation can be generated by the bioreactor itself.

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80ca883bed288996fc3b" />To start, the sensor system itself must first be built, verified, validated, and tested such that result consistency is guaranteed. The following workflow shall be observed:

    1. <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f5a860c93f9763d555" />Construct the circuitry according to device documentation
    2. <a id="3ffd65dd82be80ca9920c4c9e49581b5" />Check for sensor function through verification testing
    3. <a id="3ffd65dd82be80c2835fd07bd948fef7" />Check for result precision through validation testing
    4. <a id="3ffd65dd82be80238471ccb19c815c21" />Adapt the sensor system to fit the needs of an OD sensor
    5. <a id="3ffd65dd82be804b8a3df9ac38d8184b" />Test for result accuracy by comparing sensor output with spectrophotometer output
    6. <a id="3ffd65dd82be80e5a45dc9582535547c" />Apply OD monitoring system to the bioreactor

    <a id="3ffd65dd82be80f5ba0df9dc22cb56d0" />**What worked:**

    - <a id="3ffd65dd82be80c298d1e37e6ca2c774" />Adjusting the voltage received by the LED by controlling the resistor between the power source and the part was successfully able to fine-tune the sensor.     - <a id="3ffd65dd82be802bb29af3b45bb9fa57" />Developing the OD sensor as a separate module allows it to operate independently of the bioreactor without requiring modifications or pauses in testing to the existing vessel. For monitoring bacterial growth, this becomes a concern, as reliable measurements must be consistently taken at different points in time.

Comparing the measured values against a laboratory spectrophotometer would help establish its accuracy and suitability for generating bacterial growth curves.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be804c80d7cc53d190decd" />Validation of Each Iteration

<a id="3fed65dd82be80c4b96af028d6808f24" />To assess our bioreactor’s performance, we conducted wet-lab validation by comparing it with the conventional flask-culturing method, which served as the control. # <a id="3fed65dd82be80cfabc8d49c615bfc53" />Impact of the Bioreactor

<a id="3f0d65dd82be808cbab4dffc98786ef9" />The completed bioreactor demonstrates that a small-scale system built from off-the-shelf components and using accessible manufacturing methods can support *E. Coli* growth comparable to conventional benchtop equipment.

<a id="3f0d65dd82be8071849cd6f11806723d" />Growth curves recorded from validation were compared against those produced from standard laboratory methods, providing a practical way to evaluate whether this simplified design could match, or better yet, exceed the expected bacterial growth behaviour. Beyond its engineering success within UBC iGEM 2026’s project scope, the nature of an open-source design marks it as a starting point for smaller teaching laboratories and research groups, or even medical centers in resource-limited environments that want to adapt and expand on this point-of-care device. The main objective of developing the bioreactor is not to replace high-end laboratory equipment, but to showcase that many functions required for bacterial culture can be recreated on a much smaller scale and at a lower cost.

# <a id="3f0d65dd82be80a99bf0ea06cce646d7" />Next Steps for the Bioreactor

<a id="3f0d65dd82be8066aba5e6bfe6424685" />While the current bioreactor design is functional and capable of supporting reliable bacterial growth, there are a few features that could be improved for future iterations. The next stage of development is to move from a collection of individual components toward a more integrated and automated system.

## <a id="3f0d65dd82be80958451d5cd5f753ef7" />Design Improvements

<Callout icon="💡">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be80748ebdd5a92561ef04" />**Integrated Electronics**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be8099be52e88f90c77d9e" />The current bioreactor uses several independently connected sensors, pumps, and electronic components, resulting in a large number of wires and separate circuits that must be assembled and debugged individually. Future iterations could integrate these systems onto a custom printed circuit board (PCB), with a microcontroller incorporated directly onto the board to control and communicate with each component. Additionally, a Raspberry Pi could be added for data storage, visualization, and increase user-friendly interface. However, adding a Raspberry Pi would not replace the microcontroller’s role in monitoring and controlling real-time systems — it would instead act as a supplementary feature. Overall, integrating the electronics into a single PCB would reduce the hazards of loose wiring and simplify troubleshooting, making the system more reproducible and reliable.
</Callout>

<Callout icon="📡">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be8016b70ac010659f01b3" />**Automated Monitoring and Feedback Control**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be80fca08bc90900d6a04c" />Temperature and pH are currently measured as separate processes, requiring the operator to monitor the system and manually respond when conditions change. These sensors could instead be connected to a central controller that continuously records their measurements and alerts the operator when a parameter shifts outside an acceptable range. An additional improvement would be to introduce feedback control, where the bioreactor automatically responds to these changes. For instance, temperature measurements could control a heating unit, while pH measurements could activate small solution pumps that add acid or base when required. *Feedback control is commonly used in bioreactors to maintain conditions around a desired set point rather than relying on periodic manual adjustments. This is especially critical if the sample bacteria are extremely sensitive to certain environmental parameters.* Not only does this reduce operator involvement, but it maintains a more consistent and steady growth condition throughout the duration of a test.

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be80fa9bd3e89c78adb9a7" />*need to cite*
</Callout>

<Callout icon="🧪">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be80c689dec45dd805161a" />**Automated Optical Density Sampling**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be806282faddf53845c22c" />Optical density (OD) measurements currently require a small culture sample to be manually withdrawn through tubing using a syringe before it can be measured by the custom OD sensor. This process could be automated by incorporating a small peristaltic or syringe pump into the sampling line. At pre-set intervals, the controller could draw a fixed volume of culture through the OD measurement chamber, collect the reading, record the result, return the sample, and then prepare the system for the next measurement. *Automated bioreactor sampling systems have been shown to reduce hands-on work, enable measurements at consistent intervals, and improve replicability compared to manual sampling.* Additionally, this method of sampling maintains a closed system for the bacteria, thus reducing contamination while ensuring biosafety for the operator by adhering to aseptic practices. For this bioreactor, automated sampling would allow a complete growth curve to be recorded as well as decrease operator involvement, thus reducing the chances of human error.

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be802c95d1d142db810ff6" />*need to cite*
</Callout>

<Callout icon="💉">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be80129865d784e25fb13d" />**Improved Sterility and Sampling Design**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be8057a313efa6f01c2839" />As the system becomes increasingly automated, maintaining a closed and clean culture environment becomes more important. Repeated manual sampling introduces additional exposed interfaces and handling steps where contamination can occur. Not only does this increase the risk of cross-contamination of the culture, but it also poses a biosafety risk if the operator does not follow proper protocol. Future versions could include permanent sampling tubing with sealed fittings, along with dedicated sample pathways that minimize the need to open or disconnect the system during an experiment. Examples of these include the use of Luer-Lock syringes and corresponding attachments to tubing — components designed to preserve sterility by maintaining a tight seal between in the inside and outside environments. Reducing the number of open connections would help maintain sterility throughout experimentation while ensuring the sampling process is safer and replicable.
</Callout>

<Callout icon="🛠️">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be8016b07cce72f93b242e" />**Improved Materials and Manufacturing**

  <a id="3f9d65dd82be80b585bcf43e20cece99" />Fused deposition modelling (FDM) was well suited for the early stages of rapid prototyping because of its fast manufacturing time and low raw material costs. However, polylactic acid (PLA) based components are more suited for rapid prototyping than permanent long-term use, due to PLA’s weak mechanical structure and lack of durability to heat and environmental stressors. Larger structural components could instead be printed from a tougher and more temperature-resistant material such as polyethylene terephthalate glycol (PETG) or acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ASA). PETG offers improved temperature and chemical resistance compared with PLA while remaining relatively accessible and easy to manufacture, making it a practical upgrade for many of the current components. Components exposed more directly to the culture, tubing, cleaning solutions, or repeated handling could instead be manufactured from metals such as aluminum 6061 or even stainless steel for high corrosion resistance. Moving toward more durable materials would increase the lifetime of the system and reduce the likelihood of components deforming, cracking, or requiring frequent replacement.
</Callout>
