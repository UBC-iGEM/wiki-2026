<a id="3ecd65dd82be808f9f29fcc392808d8c" />Strategic planning defines the process by which FAST can grow from a laboratory prototype to an established business. Through a lean business model that rapidly provides an overview of our product as a business and a SWOT analysis that shows our product’s competitive features in the market, we examined the business landscape and market gaps for our product. Concurrently, a risk assessment, revenue streams analysis, commercialization roadmap projection and intellectual property protection strategy is presented that lay the groundwork for determining actionable steps for FAST.

# <a id="3ecd65dd82be801394e4efdece3683de" />SWOT Analysis

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80c58d83d271880d2e1e" />

<Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/ea194611d84f5d90b2d46d48e5a2e419.svg" alt="" scale={50} align="center" />

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80db9ad9fa1f0a8ebd35" />Inherent in the product’s goal and mechanism, FAST’s strengths lie in its decentralized workflow as a portable device producing rapid results, its low consumable cost, and the modular design of our system for potential practical application to different infections. By reducing unnecessary downstream treatments, FAST contributes to antibiotic stewardship by supporting effective infection containment and reducing costs associated with additional treatments.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be804c891cc983a78b90c2" />In our current product design, FAST has limitations in what it can detect: it only detects antibiotic inactivation and cannot detect resistance via target mutation or efflux. It also still requires an incubation step and a fluorescence reader. Furthermore, as FAST utilizes live bacteria, shelf life, storage and biosafety must be considered, though future areas of research may address these through biosensor stabilization and biocontainment.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be8084bf0eebdcd90e9cbb" />Opportunities for FAST are clear. BC pharmacists’ ability to prescribe for UTIs creates a new, underserved decision maker, and national and global AMR policy priorities demonstrate the market for point‑of‑care diagnostic technologies. In addition, FAST’s microfluidic chip and biosensor fluorescence detection technology is designed for potential future application to other bacterial infection types and antibiotic classes.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80faae7ef4873966e6d8" />At the same time, there are external threats that could hinder the path to commercializing our product. Regulatory compliance with external mandates and industry specifications for biosafety, along with clinical validation for healthcare application, will require meticulous testing and adherence to technical specifications. Proof and indicators of novelty and competitive features will need to be projected clearly to stand out amongst established and emerging AST competitors and to earn clinicians’ trust in a novel result format. A lack of reimbursement for point‑of‑care testing in community settings could also slow adoption. Mitigating these circumstances will require early planning to lay out the framework of FAST’s potential challenges and actionable response measures.

# <a id="3ecd65dd82be808e862ecfd58aa859b3" />Revenue Streams

### <a id="3ecd65dd82be80c584ecf397c4faad9e" />**Beachhead Market**

<a id="3f2d65dd82be80d6be3df57db97a8548" />Our initial revenue will come from partnerships with local public healthcare providers and diagnostic labs. We would sell FAST directly to family physician clinics and community pharmacies on a razor‑and‑blade model. The reader (which we have created a low cost version of [Fluorescence Detector](/ubc-vancouver/hardware/fluorescence-detector)) is sold or leased at near cost, with the majority of revenue coming from from single‑use chips. Chip pricing would be set below the combined cost of a lab culture and a failed first course of antibiotics, so each test saves the clinic money (see [Market Research ](/ubc-vancouver/administration/market-research-) ). The beachhead is not meant to be highly profitable and instead serves to general real-world performance and health-economic data, build credibility of clinicians and stewardships programs, and produce the evidence needed to seek provincial reimbursement.

<Callout icon="">
  <a id="3fcd65dd82be80b78003f8317fd51b94" />We have focused extensively on the need for provincial approval in order to make this work. For most products, the end-user is also the payer, so if the product is worth the price to them, they will purchase it. However, **healthcare in single-payer systems do not work that way**. In Canada, which is under a single-payer system, each province runs a single public insurance plan that pays for medically necessary services, including laboratory tests. The province unilaterally determines what is covered and what is not under this plan, and thus the payer and the end-user become separated, each with different things they consider important. Take the decision to use/pay for *FAST.* We can split the decision across three groups:

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be8049bfd2f190759b3090" />

  | <a id="3fcd65dd82be805dbb8ce8c1afebc525" />Role                             | What they care about                                                 |
  | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
  | <a id="3fcd65dd82be8072a0ffe371eff0d10f" />User (physicians or pharmacists) | Workflow, speed, and confidence in the result                        |
  | <a id="3fcd65dd82be80d1b35dd474939dde9c" />Beneficiary (Patient)            | Getting the correct antibiotic the first time, no out-of-pocket cost |
  | <a id="3fcd65dd82be80ca9732d0957d487f03" />Payer (insurance plan)           | Total system cost and health outcomes across the population          |

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be80a09270dffb5e666105" />Under this system, the agreement of the payer is paramount: without them, users would have to pay for the test out-of-pocket, who would then pass the cost down the to the beneficiaries. Even if *FAST* were to offer faster results at lower costs, because of the lack of coverage, this would drastically slow adoption as the cost stays with the user. This is a common pattern in public healthcare systems across the world, such as the UK, Australia, France, etc.
</Callout>

### <a id="3f2d65dd82be80a29f39c2c0a5f0009c" />**Long-Term Market:**

<a id="3f2d65dd82be80c89731f5b8dcfd2a86" />Looking ahead, we aim for *FAST* to be widely adopted as a reliable, low‑cost, point‑of‑care test on antibiotic choice, used wherever antibiotics are prescribed rather than only where a lab is available. We plan to generate revenue through:

- <a id="3fcd65dd82be805cb87eec08d89d7eb0" />**Partnerships with public healthcare systems:** Once FAST has been approved for coverage in BC, we would extend to other provinces using the same clinical and economic evidence. We would also seek a national assessment through CDA-AMC to support coverage decisions across Canada. Partnerships with health authorities would allow us move beyond family practice and pharmacies into urgent care, long‑term care and emergency departments, which are all settings where UTIs are common.
- <a id="3fcd65dd82be807ea1b8d55c3b9e15c5" />**Government contracts:** Governments are the main buyers of tools that fight AMR, as it is a public health problem rather than an individual one. Contracts for rural and remote health services, where lab access is slowest, and procurement tied to national AMR action plans would provide stable, large‑volume demand. They would also put *FAST* where it stands to provide the most benefit.
- <a id="3fcd65dd82be80ceb40cd3268557135e" />**Expansion into international healthcare markets:** As mentioned above countries with single‑payer systems similar to Canada’s, such as the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand and the Nordic countries, assess and fund diagnostics in much the same way, so our evidence package can be adapted to each. The United States would follow, with a separate coverage strategy. In lo‑ and middle‑income countries, where resistant infections are common and lab capacity is limited, we would work through tiered pricing and global health partners such as [F](https://www.finddx.org/)IND, in line with the WHO Global Action Plan on AMR.

# <a id="3ecd65dd82be808da911e3b2e46aa382" />IP Protection Strategy

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80f2b5a7e44e54f88eac" />Intellectual property lies at the heart of any businesses and provides investors security that the core idea behind a business cannot simply be copied to dilute their market share. However, at the core of iGEM is the spirit of open science, this project was built from parts created by other iGEM teams, and in return, we are obligated to do the same too. However, these principles do not have to be contradictory to one another. Our IP strategy treats openness and intellectual property protections as complementary to one another instead of opposing. The key is to delineate between the science (open), and the product (protected).
