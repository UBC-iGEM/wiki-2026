# <a id="359d65dd82be81478045ffb9c0761818" />Organizational Structure

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80b181b6c2e2907e5e18" />To guide FAST strategically, technical execution and specialized expertise are closely connected. FAST would be led by a small executive team, guided by a Board of Directors and supported by specialist consultants. Consultants are important as they give us expertise we need early on but can’t yet afford full time.

Much of what the future company needs already exists in some form within that team. The wet lab group engineered and characterized the biosensor strains. The dry lab group designed the microfluidic chip, bioreactor and fluorescence reader and built the models behind them. Human practices led our expert interviews and outreach. Administration carried out the market research, financial model and business plan on these pages. The table below maps that experience onto the organizational structure above and shows where we would need to bring in people from outside.

# <a id="3f2d65dd82be800b9c3ff7f9fbda8eb9" />Financial Analysis and Projection

<a id="3fed65dd82be80bbb171df23fb6e7ede" />Our financial model follows from the beachhead and business model set out on our [Strategic Planning](/ubc-vancouver/administration/strategic-planning)  page. Revenue begins at BC launch in Year 5 of our commercialization roadmap, once the clinical performance study, Health Canada licence and provincial coverage are in place. Over the first five commercial years, *FAST* grows from 75 pilot sites to about 1,800 sites across two provinces. By then it brings in \$4.5 million a year and is close to breaking even.

<a id="3fed65dd82be8019906ed6fdcf0d4936" />

| <a id="3fed65dd82be80f18d4ff5ac46c8202f" />Assumption                 | Base case          | Basis                                                                                                       |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| <a id="3fed65dd82be80cc954bcac2dc21d534" />Chip price                 | \$35 per test      | Below the \$38.02 BC pays per tested UTI                                                                    |
| <a id="3fed65dd82be80e1b850cf0329c5ff00" />Chip cost at launch        | \$14 per test      | Low-volume production                                                                                       |
| <a id="3fed65dd82be80f1a7fecab413b0ef4c" />Chip cost at scale         | \$9 per test       | Cost-plus: chip, reagents, housing, assembly, QC                                                            |
| <a id="3fed65dd82be806f9981d2953fc36796" />Chip gross margin at scale | ≈74%               |                                                                                                             |
| <a id="3fed65dd82be807cadf6d31d1b7feb7c" />Reader price               | \$500, or leased   | Close to cost, so pharmacies can adopt it                                                                   |
| <a id="3fed65dd82be80e49293ea0b413c1317" />Reader cost                | \$400              | Target device cost suggested by Dr. Bob Hancock                                                             |
| <a id="3fed65dd82be8072a625d17edfb05d74" />Average use per site       | ≈64 tests per year | Average UTI assessments per BC pharmacy <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"healthPharmacistsPrescribingMinor"}]} /> |

<a id="3fed65dd82be80cea04df170e4f3b769" />*FAST* follows a razor-and-blade model. We sell or lease the reader at close to cost and make our margin on the single-use chips, so a pharmacy pays mostly per test. This suits sites that each see only about 64 UTI assessments a year.

<a id="3fed65dd82be807ca1bbfed4e3fe5adf" />We priced the chip against what the provincial health plan already pays for UTI testing. Under the BC Laboratory Services Outpatient Payment Schedule, a positive single-organism urine culture costs \$19.57 for the culture, \$6.84 for MALDI-TOF identification and \$11.61 for susceptibility testing, for a total of \$38.02 <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"governmentofbcOutpatientPaymentSchedule"}]} />. A \$35 chip is cheaper than this even before accounting for the failed first courses and return visits it could prevent. *FAST* is slower than the closest commercial comparator, the Sysmex PA-100, which returns results in 30 to 45 minutes, but the PA-100 needs its own dedicated analyzer while *FAST* runs on a low-cost reader. A 2026 decision-tree analysis from Japan, done from a healthcare payer’s perspective, found that a point-of-care susceptibility test for uncomplicated UTI was potentially cost-effective compared with empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics plus culture, which is common practice there <Cite refs={[{"n":3,"key":"niiroCosteffectivenessAnalysisRapid2026"}]} />.

<a id="3fed65dd82be806daf03e4a15833a2de" />The \$9 chip cost is a cost-plus estimate based on supplier pricing for the microfluidic chip, fluorescent reagents, housing and packaging, assembly and quality control. We assume it falls from about \$14 at launch to \$11 in the second commercial year and \$9 after that. The real figure will depend on manufacturing yield, biosensor shelf life and whether freeze-drying lets us avoid cold-chain storage.

<a id="3fed65dd82be8039b451f0ef2070ba16" />In the first three commercial years, *FAST* grows within BC from pilot sites to about 350 pharmacies and clinics, which reaches our serviceable obtainable market of roughly 22,000 chips a year. From Year 8, sales expand to a second province as coverage decisions follow, and by Year 9 *FAST* sells about 115,000 chips, roughly 10% of our Canadian serviceable available market. Year 8 assumes the new province contributes for half the year. Gross margin dips slightly that year because we place a large number of readers at close to cost, and it recovers as those readers start generating chip sales.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80e39fddc3e7503797c9" />Operating expenses pay for a small team of scientific, quality, regulatory and commercial staff, along with our ISO 13485 quality management system, post-market surveillance, clinical liaison and sales. Over the five commercial years, operating losses add up to about \$10.6 million, which our [growth strategy](/ubc-vancouver/administration/long-term-growth#359d65dd82be817abeefeac74ee60a7d) covers with a Series B round. Each chip earns about \$26 in margin, so *FAST* breaks even at roughly 154,000 chips per year, or 14% of the Canadian market. We expect to reach that shortly after Year 9 as more provinces add coverage.

<a id="3fed65dd82be800d903fc4e3f2843cbf" />Clinical validation is the largest single cost. This is the study comparing *FAST* against reference susceptibility testing on patient urine specimens, and both Health Canada and the provincial payer need its results before *FAST* can be licensed and covered. Roughly a third of the total capital has to be raised before there is any revenue, so our growth strategy leans on non-dilutive funding from programs such as Mitacs, NRC IRAP, New Ventures BC and CARB-X for as long as possible before taking on equity.

<a id="3fed65dd82be80fc8261fef5f93d1dd8" />The projection depends most on provincial coverage. Without it, pharmacies would pay out of pocket, and adoption would likely stall well below these volumes. Chip cost matters next. If biosensor stabilization fails and the chips need cold-chain storage, costs could rise well above \$9 and cut into the margin that gets *FAST* to break-even. Use per site could move the other way: we assume average pharmacy volumes, but partner clinics that also test physician-referred UTIs would bring in considerably more revenue per site. Our pilots are designed to test all of these.

# <a id="3f2d65dd82be80169b31fee620b39814" />Go-To-Market Strategy

<a id="3f2d65dd82be80619e88c0476e68e893" />As outlined in our [Market Research ](/ubc-vancouver/administration/market-research-), our beachhead market consists of family physician clinics, urgent care clinics, and community pharmacies in British Columbia, where UTIs are diagnosed and antibiotics are prescribed without timely susceptibility test results. Our approach has three parts. First, we would find early adopters by working with antimicrobial stewardship programs, pharmacy associations, and family practice associations to identify clinicians who want point-of-care susceptibility data. Second, we would run pilots directly with the provincial payer through health innovation programs, so that the province is part of generating the evidence it will eventually use to decide on coverage. Third, we would design each pilot to collect the specific evidence that each stakeholder group needs to see, summarized below.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be805d8888ff65ffd1a5aa" />

| <a id="3fdd65dd82be80838fa8e65faa91458f" />Stakeholder Group                       | Wants                                                                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| <a id="3fdd65dd82be806d832ac766b0432934" />Users (family physicians & pharmacists) | FAST can be conducted in a short window and tells them whether their chosen antibiotic will work |
| <a id="3fdd65dd82be8021ac7ad04e6edd128f" />Patients (beneficiaries)                | The right antibiotic is chosen the first time around, resulting in fewer return visits           |
| <a id="3fdd65dd82be8017b754f215e5590412" />Provincial Health Plan (payer)          | Lower total cost to the health care system from fewer failed treatments                          |

## <a id="3f2d65dd82be80ac8220d0bd562376f0" />First Product & Deliverables

<a id="3f2d65dd82be8090a6b3f62c8f9c9127" />Our first product, which we anticipate having ready for pilots by Year 4, would be a point-of-care UTI diagnostic platform consisting of an affordable fluorescence reader and single-use microfluidic test chips. With this product, we would run pilot programmes in community clinics and pharmacies, demonstrating the ability of FAST to integrate with existing workflows, improve clinical outcomes, and enhance antimicrobial stewardship.

## <a id="3f2d65dd82be80818897f3066ab7d303" />Investor Engagement & Demonstration

<a id="3f2d65dd82be8098bc36f5f8c00ee018" />To maintain investor interest and attract additional funding, we would tie each funding round to clear data and live technical demonstrations of our progress. These could include live point-of-care fluorescence readouts at scientific conferences, demonstrations of functional hardware prototypes, and validated metrics on cost savings, turnaround time, and reductions in empirical prescribing. This transparent approach ensures that early-stage capital directly drives key technical, regulatory, and commercial milestones, following the timeline below, which matches the roadmap on our [Strategic Planning](/ubc-vancouver/administration/strategic-planning) page.

<a id="3f2d65dd82be803cb8cdc950190b2be6" />

# <a id="359d65dd82be81eea81ade36d5337086" />Product Iteration Framework

## <a id="3f2d65dd82be8043917dc6226bc21dc5" />Customer Segment Determination DBTL

<a id="3f2d65dd82be80479751efd05e8f82a7" />Understanding who our solution serves, and why they care, was central to determining our customer segments.  We used our Design-Build-Test-Learn (DBTL) cycle to systematically identify and refine our key stakeholder groups.

<a id="359d65dd82be81009fc3f9ba8e065f71" />

<Dbtl>
  <Fragment slot="design">
    <a id="3f2d65dd82be805a90dcf152653661a1" />We began by listing every group involved in diagnosing and treating UTIs, from hospital microbiology laboratories and emergency departments to family physicians, community pharmacists, and researchers. For each, we mapped their current pain points against how *FAST* could improve their existing approach, and separated the user of the test from the party who would pay for it.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="build">
    <a id="359d65dd82be81938b31d803d6db4726" />We built a customer profile for each segment using the Value Proposition Canvas, connecting its diagnostic needs to the specific value *FAST* could provide. These profiles form the segment comparison on our [Needs Finding](/ubc-vancouver/administration/needs-finding) page.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="test">
    <a id="359d65dd82be81ca8794f20646b1425b" />We tested these profiles against our market research and our conversations with experts. Dr. Bob Hancock pointed out that in BC, many UTIs are treated by pharmacists or in remote communities without any testing, and that a result is only useful if it tells the prescriber which antibiotics still work. Infection control nurse and educator Lesley Kooner raised the opposite risk: urine specimens are often sent for the wrong reasons, such as cloudy urine alone, which means that easier testing could also lead to over-testing.
  </Fragment>

  <Fragment slot="learn">
    <a id="359d65dd82be816388bbdae142d31a70" />These findings are reflected in our choice of community primary care, and pharmacies in particular, as our beachhead market, and in our requirement that *FAST* support multiple antibiotics on a single chip. They also showed us that *FAST* must be paired with clear guidance on when to test, so that easier testing supports antimicrobial stewardship rather than undermining it. We reached out to family physicians, infectious disease physicians and the BC Centre for Disease Control (BCCDC) to test these assumptions directly, but were not able to schedule interviews before the end of the season. Our customer analysis therefore relies on BC government prescribing data and the expert input above. Speaking with these groups, along with BC pharmacists, is the first step of our next iteration.
  </Fragment>
</Dbtl>
