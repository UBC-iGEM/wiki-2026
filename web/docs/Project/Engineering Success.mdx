# <a id="1a0e5e20c4884f88aec7aa23fc31c43d" />What is Engineering Success?

<a id="65ff9f1b119c41f1ae35f32ae9a5274d" />In iGEM, Engineering Success is the record of how a project actually arrived where it did. It is demonstrated through the Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL) cycle: each turn of the cycle commits to a design, builds it, tests it against something real, and feeds what was learned into the next attempt. The attempts that failed matter as much as the ones that worked, because they are what the next design is built on.

<a id="c3bf03b7b8fb43a1a78dbfff6f776c8c" />FAST — our Fluorescent Antibiotic Susceptibility Test — is assembled from four tightly coupled lines of work. Wet Lab engineered the biosensor strains that report antibiotic activity and the patient simulant strains used to challenge them. Hardware built the microfluidic chip that holds the assay, the bioreactor that supplies its cultures, and the fluorescence detector that reads it out. Modelling narrowed an impractically large search space down to the promoters and incubation times worth testing at the bench. Human Practices shaped who the device is for and what it owes the people who will use it.

<a id="a02060e5aaf44146a66dcff5d31db63b" />These cycles are not independent. Modelling's degradation timescales set the exposure windows Wet Lab tested; Wet Lab's MIC ranges set the concentrations Modelling screened against; the chip's well geometry was redesigned around what the fluorescence detector could physically resolve. Grouping the cycles by subteam below keeps each thread readable, but the handoffs between them are where most of the learning happened.

# <a id="7aa63aaa76264a1db6799c7e8627b358" />Modelling

<a id="9a6a0b171283474fa7329dee381ac6c5" />Modelling's job on FAST was to decide what the wet lab should spend its time on. Three of the four cycles below share that shape: a search space too large to test exhaustively, a computational method for ranking it, and a handoff of a shortlist to the bench. The fourth, the degradation model, runs the other direction — it predicts a timescale that the wet lab then had to confirm.

      <a id="ba23a0f71a884617a2a94184fafa52ff" />The project objective was to develop a reproducible method for selecting a manageable promoter-screening panel from antibiotic-responsive genes.       <a id="2ae1535e70b24fca8597aa68fb867223" />The team developed a joint differential-expression workflow, integrated curated RegulonDB relationships, mapped candidates to promoters and transcription units, and stored the evidence in a searchable SQLite database and explorer.

      <a id="79698cac8b7a4f0eb09b799148d9d509" />The team assessed candidate stability under alternative thresholds, inspected biological replicates, compared responses to challenge antibiotics, checked promoter sequences against MG1655, and added automated tests for regulatory semantics, database integrity, and candidate queries.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="learn">
      ### <a id="39fcdaa6ddae4345a645271e49d65270" />Learn

      <a id="90c682ab052d4d1f969a09620de12433" />Expression strength alone identified more genes than could be tested and did not distinguish independent promoters from genes with shared regulatory context.     ## <a id="2faf4a7e99da4e2f932bcd2edd231423" />Degradation Model
  </summary>

  <a id="cc821f5d4f8f411fa2ea8b03115b63cf" />The degradation model answers a question the assay design depends on: how long must an antibiotic sit with a resistance enzyme before the drop in concentration is large enough to measure? It was built in three stages, each adding a coupled biological process the previous stage had assumed away. Read the full deliverable at [Degradation Model ](/ubc-vancouver/modelling/degradation-model-).

  ### <a id="3a7a2ca9c26b42559c50089d0e5038a9" />Stage 1 — Modelling antibiotic degradation

  <a id="3f18ebe1eb8245e48568352cc8f3bf6c" />

  <a id="1683a63b591d459e84362dfefe52c8b2" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="157ffc4ff3204ed4b18eb6c720d9b9ae" />Design: Determine the expected timescale of enzymatic antibiotic degradation and whether degradation could occur within a practical assay duration.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="build">
      <a id="0daa45751516459eadd9bc2de7bcd42d" />Build: Implement Michaelis-Menten degradation dynamics in Python
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="test">
      <a id="661819c4d9c34c5e8b86f0b84d103232" />Test: Simulate antibiotic concentration over time across a range of kinetic parameters, including ($k_{cat}$), ($K_m$), enzyme concentration, and starting antibiotic concentration.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="learn">
      <a id="5b49903c97b54355ad1903ec69d0403a" />Learn: The predicted degradation trajectory provided a useful basis of selecting experimental incubation periods, but did not account for changes in enzyme concentration over time. Enzyme induction therefore needed to be incorporated into the model.
    </Fragment>
  </Dbtl>

  ### <a id="7fc43eeb10724993999252259583a413" />Stage 2 — Coupling enzyme induction to degradation

  <a id="9185f4dfc5054e43ac47f28fdd3333c6" />

  <a id="e571d9075e324d86aac79d7a5ddf05b0" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="9f3085d1795b4174b33bd28c477a08aa" />Design: Improve the model by allowing the amount of resistance enzyme to change over time after antibiotic exposure.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="build">
      <a id="50535ae5d84a41c1a7596ca613d9317e" />Build: Add a Hill equation for enzyme induction and connect it to the Michaelis-Menten equation used for antibiotic degradation.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="test">
      <a id="30ed3852673a4b27a12cc511b5f8578e" />Test: Change different induction parameters and observe how they affect enzyme production and antibiotic degradation.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="learn">
      <a id="02d4dfba225745cc86824fb4a675ba5d" />Learn: ($K_{ind}$) had little effect when antibiotic concentration was already high enough for enzyme induction to be close to its maximum. Furthermore, using MIC as the degradation threshold was not useful when the simulation started as ($1\times MIC$), so percentage degradation was used instead.
    </Fragment>
  </Dbtl>

  ### <a id="9a99abaa93e7451b8db9a58d14ec0212" />Stage 3 — Connecting degradation to bacterial response

  <a id="cf4553234d50453ab56e9acef7396a59" />

  <a id="8d3034bbecb347478bcf38c609a8a250" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="1cde0238642a463d8d4336e2b21b07e0" />Design: Determine how antibiotic degradation can affect bacterial growth.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="build">
      <a id="893472e55fbb44c68bb12af90ae81d91" />Build: Add a sigmoidal Emax model that combines bacterial growth and antibiotic-mediated killing.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="test">
      <a id="eceb9c71c94d4a6a8c0bc73443be9261" />Test: Simulate enzyme concentration, antibiotic concentration, and bacterial population simultaneously.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="learn">
      <a id="028fa829a5ef454da1806578f0d67cd6" />Learn: As antibiotic concentration decreases through degradation, the antibiotic-mediated killing also decreases. If bacterial growth becomes greater than antibiotic-mediated killing, the bacterial population can begin to recover.
    </Fragment>
  </Dbtl>

# <a id="95f1550ec12c4dd2b8d3cd2db4fda64f" />Wet Lab

<a id="d0dc4cf096664a47bed03166aa89194d" />Wet Lab built both halves of the assay's biology: the biosensor strains that fluoresce in response to active antibiotic, and the patient simulant strains that degrade it. The two are tested against each other — a simulant's success is measured by how little the biosensor reacts to what it leaves behind — so the cycles in this section are best read as one conversation.

<details>
  <summary>
    ## <a id="bf69f4dc5cf343e1811c9d70f5e00c79" />Biosensor
  </summary>

  <a id="5e28b4b09c584a63b181c8598455b7fb" />Two stress-response reporter constructs — CpxP for cell-envelope stress and RecA for DNA damage — each driving sfGFP. The cycles run from cloning through plasmid retention and growth characterisation to four rounds of antibiotic challenge, where the headline finding reversed: the early conclusion that RecA was the more sensitive reporter did not survive the longer assay. Read the full deliverable at [Biosensor](/ubc-vancouver/wet-lab/biosensor).

  ### <a id="40c5ce3b0bec4a2b91aa69705c2eeca8" />Cloning

  <a id="d7fed6c173e44b349c24b6e6ee1cf9a8" />

  <a id="6f4dfa98ad034aefafad4fa433e17ed5" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="3e3079297ea14afdb2d391fe7fccc0ce" />Based on our literature review, selected two stress response promoters to detect the presence of active antibiotics. This informed our design of two fluorescent reporter constructs. The CpxP reporter construct (2940 bp) uses the *cpxP* promoter, which responds to cell envelope stress. The RecA reporter construct (2855 bp) uses the *recA* promoter and its SOS box, which respond to DNA damage.

      <a id="247272b6bbe04ee098f310ef4c62111b" />Both reporters are generated using the pSB1C3 backbone for 3 reasons:

      - <a id="aef5dd50a3b44cfcb8cbfd813507fdd4" />Host compatibility: it carries a pMB1 origin of replication which works in *E. coli*.
      - <a id="e43acb2680ed4d60ba5fbc7fa2a4de4e" />Copy number: pMB1 is a high-copy origin, which increases signal strength for an sfGFP reporter.
      - <a id="f4cc1fac8d4f44f68900f6f2955d06e6" />Selection marker: it carries chloramphenicol resistance (CmR). We avoided AmpR and KanR backbones because those resistance genes would affect our modelling of β-lactam and aminoglycoside induced stress.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="build">
      <a id="ed18330470794297ac441a7cac21d7b5" />For assembly, we ordered gBlocks gene fragments from IDT with Gibson overhangs built in:

      - <a id="b898b792f83f43fb984d2916c5ac8add" />Two linearized backbone fragments (2079 bp), each with overhangs specific to one construct.
      - <a id="96955b79efb2422ea15429a026b08cc2" />A CpxP reporter insert (947 bp) containing our $\mathrm{P}_\textit{cpxP}$-*sfGFP* reporter part ([BBa\_26A5JL4T](https://registry.igem.org/parts/bba-26a5jl4t)) and overhangs specific to the regions flanking the pSB1C3 backbone MCS
      - <a id="bea0c3ddd4a3419faea755b8062141ed" />A RecA reporter insert (862 bp) containing our $\mathrm{P}_\textit{recA}$-*sfGFP* reporter part ([BBa\_267O4B7H](https://registry.igem.org/parts/bba-267o4b7h)) with overhangs specific to the regions flanking the pSB1C3 backbone MCS

      <a id="b3ebc4864a9a4ecd8ecedd1958297ab2" />We assembled each reporter by Gibson assembly, combining the linearized backbone with the corresponding insert. We transformed the assemblies into competent *E. coli* DH5α and selected colonies grown on chloramphenicol plates.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="test">
      <a id="dab5a5b5230f43f1a9c0700fa2036e29" />We extracted plasmids from colonies of each construct and sequenced them to confirm successful cloning.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="learn">
      <a id="0955a7efdc9044b08a0462392991cf1a" />Sequencing confirmed that both reporters are assembled as designed. While some point mutations were identified, their impact was negligible. As such, we proceeded to transform our constructs into EPI300 *E. coli*.
    </Fragment>
  </Dbtl>

  ### <a id="da007431696e4aa38175a3a3673d9b95" />Plasmid Retention — Iteration 1

  <a id="106f59b42bfb4d2e894c5fe10c04c1b3" />

  <a id="6457567368b843108727df1c3d2ff7f3" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="8dfa2bf65c014a71be23ad6a2da8f78f" />Our biosensor reporter plasmids carry the chloramphenicol resistance cassette (CmR) for selection. However, the use of chloramphenicol in our media may confound the effects of other antibiotics in our validation assays. To avoid this potential confounding effect on biosensor stress response readouts, we investigated whether the biosensor strains could retain their reporter plasmids when cultured in chloramphenicol-free LB.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="build">
      <a id="df712965ec70482eb77919f864eea982" />We included the appropriate controls to investigate the use of Cm-free LB media.

      - <a id="a7e308bdf0e840a38a000e51b7d002d0" />Negative control group: WT EPI300 grown in LB media at 37˚C for 0, 1, 2, or 4 hours; then plated on LB-Cm plates for \~18 hours at 37˚C.
      - <a id="54a80a421cf14915ad05dca9fd7a7e6b" />Positive control group: CpxP or RecA biosensors were grown in LB-Cm media at 37˚C for 0, 1, 2, or 4 hours; then plated on LB-Cm plates for \~18 hours at 37˚C.
      - <a id="5b23e6cc2dcf497aafe05a3235c24941" />Test group: CpxP or RecA biosensors were grown in LB media at 37˚C for 0, 1, 2, or 4 hours; then plated on LB-Cm plates for \~18 hours at 37˚C.

      <a id="76fce47e59fe4b3d87a15fcaa0437a61" />Bacterial cultures were plated at 4 dilutions between 10^-3 and 10^-6.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="test">
      <a id="9bb6cde9c08f4ba1913cf82aaec7eb57" />As expected, the WT EPI300 strain did not grow in any condition. Colonies were observed in all LB-Cm plates inoculated with biosensor strains cultured in either LB or LB-Cm.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="learn">
      <a id="4f0af31dd85049a7815f62ee1620bb6f" />While the data shows that CpxP and RecA strains retain their reporter plasmids when cultured in Cm-free LB media for at least 1 hour, they displayed a lawn of growth. As such, it would be necessary to repeat this experiment using higher dilutions in order to prevent a lawn of growth.
    </Fragment>
  </Dbtl>

  ### <a id="37ce491f5e2843df9c39691ca62e3bdd" />Growth Curve

  <a id="1d6c350d2a8042e28d65e6b5005a430c" />

  <a id="32c1510ad8714c33b39ca00c8d0fb372" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="c13c27e2a1284a868283c3db8dd7e580" />Before validating our reporter strains, we needed to first generate a curve that measures the baseline growth kinetics of the WT EPI300 strain compared to the biosensor strains. This is helpful for determining the metabolic burden of the plasmid backbone as well as baseline GFP expression.

      <a id="f87612f37e124c9985b6493749f1bdae" />Additionally, for our microfluidic chip application, we needed to characterize bacterial growth and baseline GFP expression at ambient temperature compared to 37˚C.

  ### <a id="0481cce9279d4f7b9d22fa55db1d76c9" />Antibiotic-Induced Fluorescence Output — Iteration 1

  <a id="23188e4055334cfd80cbf8d1065f582e" />

  <a id="e006de7fb4104f9ba6f228f013118061" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="60cb4f3760314c00ad59e20e0b0361dd" />After transformation, we needed to validate our construct’s biological function as a sensor. To this end, we started by looking for differential expression of green fluorescent protein (GFP) to determine a range of subinhibitory antibiotic concentrations to use, as well its incubation time. We also considered the effect of optical density ($OD_{600}$) on sfGFP expression, and included this as a variable in the experiment.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="build">
      <a id="cc804a6348104eeab4e92eaa845166fb" />RecA and CpxP biosensor strains were inoculated and harvested, then diluted to 1x at $OD_{600}$ of 0.4. Antibiotics (Amoxicillin, Kanamycin, Gentamicin, Cephalexin) were reconstituted, and serially diluted twice. In a clear, U-bottom 96-well plate, LB-Cm media was added into the wells for antibiotic conditions, then given the associated serial dilution of antibiotic. This combination was diluted further using the biosensor strains at an equal volume. Wells were allotted for blank LB-Cm and unstimulated biosensor strain controls. Each condition was done in triplicate, including controls.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="test">
      <a id="03762d27804746459e200a6591979131" />We evaluated the plate using a microplate reader machine, exciting at 488 nm and reading fluorescence at 510 nm. The plate was incubated at 37˚C, and scanned every 10 minutes over one hour.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="learn">
      <a id="33e71c313c104545856f39f6b1c4c50f" />We had four major findings:

      1. <a id="2b8a92c2576e4345be36b97e8f677b7c" />The RecA biosensor appeared more sensitive, with significantly higher fold changes observed in the same timescale

  ### <a id="03ed88fd19cc45d89b844f197facb9b8" />Degradation Validation — Iteration 1

  <a id="ce706a154fe744b6ac0657ca5285a75b" />

  <a id="572f1f5335dc4a37bc96a72bc6f28cbf" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="51f7ec2a09024d2d8b6cd1f29cd2ee6c" />Our antibiotic susceptibility testing device is intended to intake a patient sample and an antibiotic of interest. In the case that the patient sample is resistant to the antibiotic, the bacteria are expected to degrade it.

      <a id="504b9a2945034c35a7258d8e7e1debf8" />To model this process, we designed an experiment to characterize the antibiotic degradation kinetics of the patient simulant strain, with the goal of determining the minimum exposure time required for antibiotic degradation and quantifying the extent of degradation over time.
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="build">
      <a id="98205d642e944822bf39fe28900fe66d" />To investigate this, each patient simulant strain (GmR, KanR, β-lactamase simulant) was exposed to 6 ug/ml of its corresponding antibiotic (gentamicin, tobramycin in place of kanamycin, or amoxicillin) for either 6 or 12 hours.       <a id="8c0ced4c104548f380ab485a00abd73e" />Following incubation of either 6 or 12 hours, the bacterial cells were removed via centrifugation and the remaining antibiotic-containing supernatants were applied to both our RecA and CpxP biosensors.     </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="test">
      <a id="d39e8b5e34ab43a4a811dc944221148a" />Following 24 hours of biosensor incubation, the tobramycin- and amoxicillin-containing supernatants produced $OD_{600}$ values similar to the untreated controls for both biosensor strains.       <a id="70e15998dd0543c18aa8f1bef6fc851a" />However, the fl**uorescence intensity measurement was lower than the untreated controls** despite the lack of observed growth inhibition.       <a id="bb4ab3f29ef341faa5d125f0f48acf7c" />In contrast to the above, the biosensor cultures treated with **gentamicin-containing supernatan**t showed an approximately **two-fold reduction in $OD_{600}$ values** when compared with untreated controls.     </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="learn">
      <a id="8bea8c9d6e9b4d1f9a73fd153f7ccc75" />However, we identified an important **confounding variable** in this iteration: **residual patient-simulant bacteria** remained in the supernatants following centrifugation.       <a id="19bfbc2b6285406192b49d9b1ed2bc66" />This led us to Iteration 2, where we introduced an additional filtration step to remove residual bacterial cells before the supernatants were applied to the biosensors.
    </Fragment>
  </Dbtl>

  ### <a id="7a9eb4d90496487d966b59c260bb9e22" />Mark Two — PDMS Attempt I

  <a id="0c03321029574d75b505cc15173f3ccd" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="547f70d47d324e8781fdd50208a31c37" />Many of the adjustments that were made to this chip were related to the issues with removing the epoxy chip in DBTL cycle 1. In order to remedy the issue with the cured chip bonding with the sides and base of the mold container, we attempted to make a microfluidic design without including any walls directly attached to the chip. This both removed the potential bonding issues that we anticipated with PDMS and also decreased printing time, allowing for a quicker build time for this chip. As an additional feature to lessen the effects of adhesion, all of the microfluidic channels were chamfered at the top.

      <a id="ce0a978d6e1346df9d4d57ca3e2a93ae" />With this new iteration of the chip, we wanted to experiment with alternative channel shapes and how effective they would be for manufacturing purposes. Using PDMS, we decided to fully cover the channels here, such that the channel pathways were only exposed on the bottom. With this, we are able to close the chips by using only one sheet of glass to be plasma-bonded to the chip rather than two that would have been needed otherwise.

      <a id="9aa3f3823079445b87867e81426c80d2" />Along with this change, both the mixing and degradation sections of the chips were altered. For the mixing section, a repeated L-shaped herringbone intrusion was added instead of the original zigzag section. Each of these herringbone patterns works to jostle all the laminar fluid streams together semi-randomly such that there are many small antibiotic streamlines flowing adjacent to the patient sample. This is done such that diffusion that happens along the course of the chip will result in the mixing of these two fluids in laminar flow, and the effectiveness of this pattern has previously been tested for its mixing capabilities at low Re <Cite refs={[{"n":6,"key":"liuTwofluidMixingMicrochannel2004"}]} />. The design was appealing due to its flat base, meaning it is compatible with our manufacturing process with PDMS pouring.

      <a id="410999bdc1804ba1a2de7e6f66ed1429" />The degradation section was changed to split into 4 separate sections. This was done as an alternative geometry to keep the small channel width while also maintaining high fluid volume with a shape that has wider gaps, which we believed would reduce the likelihood of a tear occurring. The change also allowed us to experiment with alternative chip designs, giving us better information on which designs work most effectively for FDM-based chip manufacturing, which we can use for future DBTL cycles.

    <Fragment slot="test">
      <a id="8d455307595549b3b16bb77a74a7d112" />To determine that our linear wells would not lead to a significant difference in filling (so that the bacteria are not exposed to different levels of antibiotic) we performed a series of calculations to see what the difference would be.

      <a id="66221d5261cb494c838a4c743af92c07" />Assumptions:

      - <a id="c765060eae264402bf912bd36facc692" />main channel is 1 x 1 x 2 mm
      - <a id="ec418a0c48fc415abeefbe4f75daa208" />outlets (7) are 0.5 x 0.5 mm^2
      - <a id="e3ccb0341d434ae6bd07fae1b9343ddb" />outlet length is 15 mm
      - <a id="873774892d104d8a8811aca2992441db" />outlet spacing 9.375 mm apart
      - <a id="32ead747dec24f879e04e437df3fa90c" />identical outlets
      - <a id="5ac054235da74094b6bca175a20eabaf" />There is no output from the wells (i.e. they never overflow)

      <a id="1cad278800ef413c935259377f03a9d2" />Hydraulic Resistance of main channel <Cite refs={[{"n":9,"key":"TheoreticalMicrofluidicsOxford"}]} />:

      <a id="7a9d5bb994fb49f2bde16cb6ff32356b" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned}
      R_M &= \frac{28.4\cdot \mu \cdot L}{h^{4}} \\[6pt]
         &= \frac{28.4\,(0.001\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s})(0.009375\ \mathrm{m})}{(0.001\ \mathrm{m})^{4}} \\[6pt]
          &\approx 2.7 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{\frac{Pa\cdot s}{m^{3}}}
      \end{aligned}
      \\[10pt]
      \text{where } \mu = \text{dynamic viscosity},\quad
      L = \text{channel length},\quad
      h = \text{channel side length}
      $$

      <a id="5763234f0b004f62bb8a38ff8d3b8c27" />Hydraulic Resistance of the Outlet Channel

      <a id="d89588b027694cf988ed4ce4bb3a567d" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} R_O &= \frac{28.4\,\cdot \mu  \cdot L}{h^{4}} \\[6pt] &= \frac{28.4\,(0.001\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s})(0.0015\ \mathrm{m})}{(0.0005\ \mathrm{m})^{4}} \\[6pt] &\approx 6.8 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{\frac{Pa\cdot s}{m^{3}}} \end{aligned}
      $$

      <a id="9d59b9077b3d4ff8a2972e3cda8cd2e1" />The outlet channel is therefore 3 times more resistive than the main channel.

      <a id="e954d6836c7d44f4aae637183a971226" />

      $$
      \frac{R_{O}}{R_{M}} \approx 2.56
      $$

      <a id="ea289c5a57b647d2b5b942a22ff6f44d" />To split the flow throughout the wells based on our hydraulic resistance calculation, we can set up an equivalent circuit with all of the main and outlet channel resistances listed

      <a id="bc71177381924ad0985320b787706a41" />

      <Figure imgs={[{"url":"https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/232c67c2e8b0515c9f489eb3256a8e2f.avif","alt":"","scale":0}]}>
        <Fragment slot="content">
          <a id="153df3f2488a47b0b5fadc59e0b27b6e" />Figure 23: Resistor network circuit parallel for the output section of the microfluidic chip
        </Fragment>
      </Figure>

      <a id="f4af36e3655843f9ac7287ff3c0f90c3" />We used this to solve for equivalent resistance in this portion of the chip.

      <a id="d6bda4ff0b7a4e67bf3e2a089ba035b0" />

      $$
      R_{T}=3.13 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{\frac{Pa\cdot s}{m^{3}}}
      $$

      <a id="d4b20675bce64540a8e885d02441b0a1" />Based on this value, we then solved for the flow rate going into this channel as a function of pressure drop

      <a id="045ba5f3b4a84ff489ae1b95e9cea588" />

      $$
      Q=\frac{\Delta p}{R_{T}}=3.19\cdot10^{-9}\cdot\Delta p
      $$

      <a id="4c33376da8e748999337dc051ea7ae6e" />From here, we can solve the resistor system for the pressure drop and flow rate over all of the wells for an arbitrary pressure difference.

      <a id="c36f2abb2b8d44a6b98a3542953c190d" />

      $$
      \begin{gathered} \def\arraystretch{1.6} \begin{array}{|c|c|c|} \hline \textbf{Well} & \textbf{Flow Rate} & \textbf{Pressure Drop} 
      \\ \hline 1 & 1.47\cdot10^{-9} \cdot\Delta p & \Delta p 
      \\ 2 & 7.94\cdot10^{-10 }\cdot\Delta p & 5.41\cdot\Delta p
      \\ 3 & 4.30\cdot10^{-10} \cdot\Delta p & 2.93\cdot\Delta p 
      \\ 4 & 2.35\cdot10^{-10} \cdot\Delta p & 0.160\cdot\Delta p 
      \\ 5 & 1.31\cdot10^{-10} \cdot\Delta p & 0.0892\cdot\Delta p 
      \\ 6 & 7.83\cdot10^{-11} \cdot\Delta p & 0.0534\cdot\Delta p 
      \\ 7 & 5.63\cdot10^{-11} \cdot\Delta p & 0.0384\cdot\Delta p \\ \hline \end{array} \end{gathered}
      $$

      <a id="b0eff463712f430bbab13a4c6e5ad21a" />From this, we are able to calculate the difference between the largest and smallest pressure drops along the wells

      <a id="d0709ac477954a38b64ed6d727657c4a" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned}
      Difference &=\frac{P_{O1}-P_{O7}}{\frac{P_{O1}+P_{O7}}{2}}\cdot100\% \\[6pt]
      &=\frac{(1-0.0384)\Delta p} {\frac{(1+0.0384)\Delta p}{2}} \cdot 100\% \\[6pt] &= 185\%
      \end{aligned}
      $$

      <a id="e9e43a2589a44bb7a59d57d69300fc06" />Therefore, the relative difference between the first well and the last well is 185%. We can assume this relationship holds up for fluid output as well due to the direct relationship between pressure and flow. With the given parameters, we can see that there will be a noticeable difference between separate wells, however it is still worth testing the chips capability to deliver fluids to all the channels.

      <a id="407389a39a894e2d81d43006702b676d" />Furthermore, to verify the mathematical model above, we ran a fluid flow simulation. The objective was observe flow behavior at the millimeter scale, and confirm that channel size did not matter due to great resistance.

      - <a id="158e178058e647de91582754f4337e18" />Wells will need to be deeper or sealed off when they are full. Even though our math showed that the pressure difference would not lead to uneven flow, it did not account for the situation where the wells would overflow.

  ### <a id="f2378235c4c84becb3eca6142848deca" />Iteration 3 — Optical density monitoring

  <a id="e1d6b9eecb8846b39c0950716d9c2bd4" />

  <a id="8300d16da36f448f925a04ed680fddf4" />

  <Dbtl>
    <Fragment slot="design">
      <a id="9876e0e5bdf44a5baf853f3942e2d944" />A key focus for iteration 3 is the addition of optical density (OD) monitoring. The planned design pairs a manual sample extraction with a sensor made from tubing, a light-tight box, and a syringe, informed by previously published low-cost designs (cite). OD is the standard measure of bacterial growth, so this addition would allow the growth of the culture to be tracked directly from the bioreactor, rather than requiring a spectrophotometer for external measurements. This is important to the project because temperature and pH only describe the conditions around the culture, while OD shows how the bacteria are responding to them.       | <a id="0ada45094d024a36a5944354bee0b6df" />ARD\_IN             | \~0.726 V | 0.729 V       | 2.541 V     |

      <a id="1c9875046ebb4e57b29a85c0ace11407" />**Expected value calculations:**

      <a id="ff75ddd1b8a94c6a83700f3639c42bc0" />Based on the properties of an op-amp, the U1 input difference should be \~0V.

      <a id="4e11bc0efdae477f81e014d40c731708" />V\_TIA: The voltage node that converts the photodiode’s current into a voltage.

      <a id="157893f513634326b90d63a87d008188" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} V_{TIA} &= I_p \cdot R_2 \\[6pt] &= (18\ \mathrm{nA})(1\ \mathrm{M\Omega}) \\[6pt] &= 18.0\ \mathrm{mV} \end{aligned}
      $$

      <a id="3e1cf9089480479d89e0a14c790986df" />V\_GAIN: This voltage node follows a non-inverting amplifier, meaning it multiplies V\_TIA by the value set by R5 (our potentiometer) and R3. The op-amp will adjust it’s output until the R5/R3 divider feeds back a voltage equal to V\_TIA.

      <a id="c1d018665d6543588e5b2c1864303c39" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} \text{Gain} &= 1 + \frac{R_5}{R_3} \\[6pt] &= 1 + \frac{20\ \mathrm{k\Omega}}{20\ \mathrm{k\Omega}} \\[6pt] &= 2 \end{aligned}
      $$

      <a id="da083b44dad345a4849e7ef60f36a4e9" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} V_{GAIN} &= \text{Gain} \cdot V_{TIA} \\[6pt] &= 2 \cdot 18.0\ \mathrm{mV} \\[6pt] &= 36.0\ \mathrm{mV} \end{aligned}
      $$

      <a id="e5514275775b4f948ecf914a77aa0fac" />ARD\_IN: R4 creates a potential difference with ARD\_IN which pulls this voltage node’s current through R4 and the diode into the gain stage output. Because a conducting diode will hold a fixed voltage drop across itself ARD\_IN sits one diode drop above V\_GAIN.

      <a id="2716b29c617e4589a82c9aac9302d73b" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} V_{\text{ARD\_IN}} &= V_{GAIN} + V_F \\[6pt] &= 36.0\ \mathrm{mV} + 0.69\ \mathrm{V} \\[6pt] &\approx 0.726\ \mathrm{V} \end{aligned}
      $$

      <a id="1cf2fc019f4749dfa50d5fe661b68287" />We know V\_F as a diode will forward drop at \~4 mA corresponding to 0.69 V <Cite refs={[{"n":18,"key":"1N4148WE308"}]} />.

      - <a id="65a8d76e9dbe44b89b4dd534e595c6d9" />A working op-amp holds its inputs within nanovolts of each other, the difference between the test indicates that under +5V/0V supply voltage conditions, the op-amp isn’t working properly
      - <a id="5105a65890f24b4da2c05fcc2cb00436" />This aligns with the hypothesis as the supply rails of +5V/0V are outside of what this op-amp is rated for

We also use an op-amp designed for photodiode amplification that meets our system requirements, simplify the circuit by removing two unnecessary components, and better account for the sensitivity of the initial signal. The circuit was made using Altium Designer, a PCB design software and was manufactured through JLC PCB.

      <a id="c84cabd459594212b2803ac8afb5935c" />ADC Step Size:

      <a id="d2b39e854bff42b9a5888baf5923ae33" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} 5\ \mathrm{V}\ \text{ref:} &\quad \frac{5\ \mathrm{V}}{1024} \approx 4.88\ \mathrm{mV\ per\ count} \\[6pt] 1.1\ \mathrm{V}\ \text{ref:} &\quad \frac{1.1\ \mathrm{V}}{1024} \approx 1.074\ \mathrm{mV\ per\ count} \end{aligned}

      \begin{aligned} G &= \frac{\text{step size}}{I \cdot R_F} \\[10pt] 5\ \mathrm{V}\ \text{ref:} \quad G &= \frac{4.88\ \mathrm{mV}}{10\ \mathrm{\mu V}} = 488 \\[10pt] 1.1\ \mathrm{V}\ \text{ref:} \quad G &= \frac{1.074\ \mathrm{mV}}{10\ \mathrm{\mu V}} \approx 107 \end{aligned}

Our new op-amp (AD8606) has 1pA max input bias current; which is much smaller than the OP07 op-amps from V1 which had 7nA max. Before this would effect the signal to noise ratio whereas now it is smaller than the signal itself and negligible

      1. <a id="8467f0122afe4f37a83148efb285d528" />The clipping diode

      <a id="c220331bc4124469adf01e25be7df00f" />The previous circuit was designed for voltage rails of ± 9 V, which exceeds the input voltage range for the ADC they were using. The clipping diode works to override the incoming voltage from the gain stage if it exceeds 5 V and replaces it with a steady 5 V signal from the +5 V voltage rail on the circuit. It essentially serves as protection for the ADC. In our case, our voltage rails for the circuit are equivalent to the rails on our ADC, being the Arduino itself, and therefore we no longer have to have a mechanism to cut off the voltage from exceeding +5 V before entering the ADC on the Arduino.

      <a id="0c6cf57555e246f2b17f9051bb9189cf" />We must also choose a replacement op-amp.  We are looking for a replacement op-amp that better suits our setup since the OP07 op-amp used in the original circuit is not compatible.

      <a id="f34fd0ab8a764fbdb2799ef4b3ccd250" />Requirements:

      - <a id="238b7e771b834a1aa78f19c15e6141d1" />It needs to run on a single 5 V supply, so we can power it from the Arduino
      - <a id="00472597d70c4cc9b90571ef6d026658" />It has to have rail to rail input and output voltage capabilities, since our signal starts off very small we need to be able to work with signals near 0 V
      - <a id="847d3bce228a4f21a201689f26d23150" />Minimize input bias current and input offset voltage, as the larger these values are the more artifacts the op-amp will add into our signal

      <a id="e73c07aedc414ad490a85fe9bd74353c" />An op-amp designed with photodiode amplification in mind was found and it met our requirements. Below is a table comparing both the OP07 from the original circuit design, and the AD8606 we are using in our revised circuit.

      <a id="ead26e5b044f45379a2041e4c3bcd29d" />The AD8606 operates within our supply voltage range, has smaller input bias current, and operates with rail to rail input and output voltage ranges. It also includes two op-amps in one physical IC allowing us to use less components. Its only caveat is that its input offset voltage is larger, and as such we are addressing this limitation of the op-amp through implementing a reference voltage throughout our op-amp circuitry.

      <a id="1a08198b97c040b397de6441393f4f10" />We must also change our reference voltage to increase resolution.

      <a id="a027e674fa714a799a0fb521647e12fc" />The input bias voltage is a small voltage offset on the inputs of the op-amp that can be positive or negative. If this offset is negative, the first part of our incoming signal will not be detected since it will exist below 0 V, and the signal must reach 0 V before it can be observed in the output signal. This can have a significant impact on the performance of the fluorescence detection since the photocurrent is so small to begin with.

      <a id="175dabbb0653413989a6f0cf47ab9712" />The reference voltage is created using two resistors to form a voltage divider between the +5 V and 0V rails on the circuit. Our objective with this reference voltage is to counteract the input offset voltage of the op-amp, and as such we want a small positive voltage offset. On the other hand we also want to leave as much headroom for reading the signal as possible, as our current range is 0 V to 1.1 V and we are cutting into that usable range.

      <a id="7bba5c7bef274c358b487df867e691ea" />We decided to go with a reference voltage of \~0.2 V to balance the headroom for the usable voltage for the signal, to make sure we clear the op-amp input offset voltage, and to be able to create the reference voltage using standard resistor values.

      <a id="09c67684c72a4149829493c2ec230aa7" />Voltage divider calculation, where RA is the top resistor and RB is the bottom resistor:

      <a id="72206440d6a94ef8aba5cd52bc6db62c" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} V_{REF} &= V_{in} \cdot \frac{R_B}{R_A + R_B} \\[6pt] 0.22\ \mathrm{V} &= 5\ \mathrm{V} \cdot \frac{R_B}{R_A + R_B} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{R_B}{R_A + R_B} = 0.044 \\[10pt] &\text{Choose } R_B = 1\ \mathrm{k\Omega} \\[6pt] R_A + 1\ \mathrm{k\Omega} &= \frac{1\ \mathrm{k\Omega}}{0.044} \approx 22.7\ \mathrm{k\Omega} \\[6pt] R_A &\approx 21.7\ \mathrm{k\Omega} \;\rightarrow\; 22\ \mathrm{k\Omega}\ \text{(standard value)} \\[10pt] \text{Check: } V_{REF} &= 5\ \mathrm{V} \cdot \frac{1\ \mathrm{k\Omega}}{22\ \mathrm{k\Omega} + 1\ \mathrm{k\Omega}} \approx 0.217\ \mathrm{V} \end{aligned}
      $$

      <a id="cb23edd80cd64b76bb8d5943bfec142f" />The +5 V rail for the circuit is supplied through the Arduino from a laptop, and this power source carries noise with it. To account for this a capacitor is used as a low-pass filter, reducing high frequency noise present on the reference voltage by draining it to ground. Furthermore the +5 V rail itself can drift based on what else it is being used for. The solution for this is to measure the reference voltage using the Arduino and subtract the signal voltage from it. This way if the reference voltage shifts it will be accurately removed from the signal.

      <a id="13d13f18f13c42e699e8b89cd2e6bbf4" />This signal processing is as follows:

      <a id="309780d7c6c043ca93f5f167e3df7a75" />**Stage 1:** Op-amp A is the transimpedance amplifier to convert the photocurrent into a voltage so the Arduino can measure it

      - <a id="20902d69d1404b3b9363e0ac9299d28a" />Using photovoltaic mode (zero bias voltage) rather than photoconductive mode (reverse bias) to prioritize low dark current for detecting very low light levels while having a slower response time (two ways to configure the circuitry around photodiodes)
      - <a id="2e49f399f6ff479d93aaa14ace25e3b8" />R1 of 10 MΩ to create the conversion of 1 pA to 10 μV

      <a id="50b5bb438e4b4390b6472a96e415730f" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} V_{TIA} &= V_{REF} + I_P \cdot R_1 \\[6pt] \text{Transimpedance gain} &= \frac{V_{TIA} - V_{REF}}{I_P} = R_1 = 10\ \mathrm{M\Omega} \\[10pt] (1\ \mathrm{pA})(10\ \mathrm{M\Omega}) &= 10\ \mathrm{\mu V} \\[6pt] (1\ \mathrm{nA})(10\ \mathrm{M\Omega}) &= 10\ \mathrm{mV} \end{aligned}
      $$

      <Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/f54da69b150455719c860d9fb5162234.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

      <a id="55a4843b995140649fd593ef26091e1c" />**Stage 2:** Op-amp B is a **single ended gain amplifier** that applies a programmable gain to the voltage output of op-amp A, increasing the voltage output from the first op-amp to make it more readable by the ADC

      - <a id="b69913842bd84be3b1fc9229f59ed2b5" />R1 and R2 create settable gain ranging from unity to 101
      - <a id="9b515272d56247ee861e90cefc1596cf" />Show calculation:
        - <a id="d693388eb2644ce9a385f886b398bd14" />V\_out formula

        - <a id="60b6a7f8a07e40aa9532ebe30448a9b8" />Then small table shows common gain values we can use as starting points based on how much current we get

      $$

      <a id="77b1377400a24c83927c09cb83e4ef0d" />This calculation helps us use most of the ADC’s resolution across a range of photocurrents. The Arduino measures in steps of about 1 mV. Because the circuit converts photocurrent to voltage using a 10 MΩ resistor and then applies gain, dividing one ADC step by (10 MΩ × gain) gives the smallest detectable current. At a gain of 101, this is about 1 pA. Dividing the usable voltage range of 0.88 V by (10 MΩ × gain) gives the largest current measurable before the reading saturates. Thus, higher gain allows detection of smaller currents but causes saturation at lower currents.

          <a id="5a1ac97f5ea9469fa48c77701a079052" />Figure 21. Simulated 3D view of the fluorescence detection PCB.

      <a id="ec4973dda6d44452beb42bc116127864" />We now move into the construction of an additional LED PCB so that the position can be precisely controlled.

      <a id="10f6b246b223400780aeff4e3b02a85d" />The LED circuit is much simpler compared to the fluorescence detector circuit. Its purpose is to control the current that flows through the LED to ensure safe operation of the LED. Otherwise unregulated current can exceed the 50mA maximum (cite datasheet) it can handle and possibly damage the LED. The LEDs typical operating current is 20mA with a forward voltage drop of 3.2V (cite datasheet).

      <a id="59bc2ff205624f5cb160e40875594d09" />To determine how much resistance is needed we calculate the voltage remaining.

      <a id="90ea6c1cd82745329047268c0735d9c0" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} V_{PIN} &= V_R + V_F \\[6pt] 5\ \mathrm{V} &= V_R + 3.2\ \mathrm{V} \\[6pt] V_R &= 5\ \mathrm{V} - 3.2\ \mathrm{V} = 1.8\ \mathrm{V} \end{aligned}
      $$

      <a id="0dc1877acb4b4f948100b297ba4951cd" />And divide this by the target current using Ohm’s Law.

      <a id="8d6c98fc03e44d09b8027b04d23a1109" />

      $$
      \begin{aligned} R &= \frac{V_R}{I} \\[6pt] &= \frac{1.8\ \mathrm{V}}{0.020\ \mathrm{A}} \\[6pt] &= 90\ \Omega \end{aligned}
      $$

      <a id="016119da45a74dfca24170f0e67a966c" />This resistance is rounded to 100 Ω as the nearest standard value we can use while being on the safe side with providing just under 20 mA to the LED.

      <a id="0d9e930dbc114338bb216f312f9812ff" />

      $$
      I_F = \frac{5\ \mathrm{V} - 3.2\ \mathrm{V}}{100\ \Omega} = 18\ \mathrm{mA}
      $$

      <a id="330bcb60000f429b8b31d799967dea83" />Finally a two header pin connector is used to send out a digital signal from the Arduino using pulse width modulation so we can control the brightness of the LED along with the ground. Pulse width modulation is a technique where digital signals are rapidly turned on and off to simulate analog signals, allowing us to provide variable current levels to the LED rather than only 18 mA or 0.

      <a id="0c6cc2a735214c1c88a7b99b5b0c3b67" />The LED circuit schematic:

      <Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/d8e410039f8b51be98dcc9b1e96ea572.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

      <a id="86fefe4cf32a4908837b628b9be8329d" />The layout of the LED PCB is a 30 mm by 30 mm square, with the LED itself in the center. This aligns the LED over the photodiode when the circuits are aligned by their mounting holes.

      <a id="400d65dd82be8037af72f0c234d1a3e0" />The overall electrical system includes the user’s laptop, photodiode circuit, LED circuit, and the arduino acting as the center of the system. The laptop provides the power to the system while allowing the user to view the data. The arduino controls the output to the LED alongside with the physical switch, and reads the signal and reference from the photodiode circuit. The LED circuit shines 490nm light onto the sample to excite the GFP, and the emission light is quantified and passed through signal conditioning in the photodiode circuit. Overall this electronics system creates a functioning fluorescence detector.

      <a id="400d65dd82be80388bb3f2324506b117" />

      <Img url="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6279/wiki/assets/054d80cada1959859eb94db444a2c781.avif" alt="" scale={0} align="center" />

      <a id="400d65dd82be805cbb53de064b8dea4d" />The digital pulse width modulation (PWM) pin is used to control the brightness of the LED, and can be connected to any digital arduino pin with PWM capabilities. The conditioned signal and reference voltage lines from the photodiode circuit are connected to analog pins on the arduino to read these signals as voltages. The arduino must be programmed to subtract the reference voltage from the signal to obtain the usable signal that is referenced throughout the testing and calibration of the system. The arduino code must also use the internal 1.1V reference to improve the resolution of our signal from around 5mV per ADC step to around 1mV per step.

      <a id="9f25fd30cda44515845795084738b764" />Calibration of System

      - <a id="92be6c7d719b46eea10bff8c78af0712" />LED on and LED off subtraction for baseline signal before data collection
      - <a id="9e03ae3fb8164da4aacd905c7dba8203" />Read signal and reference voltage and subtract them during data collection
      - <a id="50e87a2f24a4438da33de7d6baefe716" />Start with low gain and increase until signal is seen
      - <a id="011354d86e544ca094c5c290d82a521c" />Can adjust gain and LED brightness for optimization
    </Fragment>

    <Fragment slot="test">
      <a id="3ee59fb526384d018c635dbf783b34c8" />Our product is still under development at the time of Wiki Freeze, but we have a structured plan in place to complete the remaining work.

      <a id="400d65dd82be805fb4c5ddd50234a25e" />Once the circuit is assembled and the system is connected together, a series of tests should be performed to verify the performance of the circuitry before the initial calibration of the system. The objective of the testing is to ensure the circuit is responsive to changes in light and that the programmable gain is working as intended independent of a sample.

      <a id="400d65dd82be801e81c4e1351b1a55ad" />Testing Steps:

      1. <a id="400d65dd82be802cb59ff99cb7514801" />Start with the system assembled with both circuits in the enclosure and record the signal from the arduino with the LED off. This should return a very small signal.
      2. <a id="400d65dd82be80e0ab22f25370dc4dd0" />Slowly increase the brightness of the LED from 0 to 255 using pulse width modulation and record how the signal changes as the LED brightness increases. The signal should increase linearly with LED brightness.
      3. <a id="400d65dd82be8077b752dfc171e9d6d7" />Turn the potentiometer once and repeat step two.
      4. <a id="400d65dd82be809ca300c22be3f81d0a" />Turn the potentiometer eleven more times (twelve turns in total) and repeat step two again.
      5. <a id="400d65dd82be8045ba90f140275682d8" />Compare the slopes of how fast the signal increases across the trials from steps 2, 3, and 4. The slope should be roughly 10 times steeper in step 3 and 100 times steeper in test 4 when compared to the slope from step 2.
    </Fragment>

# <a id="36a52a8b636c4f14bb4592364ac8a9d0" />Human Practices

<a id="269ede56883141d38c6f4d557b669539" />Human Practices iterated the way the other subteams did, with stakeholder review standing in for the test step. The AMR patient-support website went through review by a health-sociology researcher and then by a patient partner, and changed substantially at each round.

Read the full deliverable at [Barrier 1 - Companionship Support](/ubc-vancouver/human-practices/barrier-1---companionship-support).
