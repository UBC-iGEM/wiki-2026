# <a id="400d65dd82be8043bea8da4115ee3761" />Fluorescence as an Antibiotic Resistance Reporter

<a id="400d65dd82be8081b5a0efae7f8fca18" />**A point-of-care platform for rapid antibiotic susceptibility testing**

<a id="400d65dd82be8025a814c03858f89ed1" />FAST tests one thing from a patient sample: can the bacteria in it inactivate the antibiotic we are about to prescribe? We engineered *E. coli* biosensors to tell us the answer using fluorescence. This year, we’re turning the tide against antimicrobial resistance and changing how clinical staff prescribe and treat patients.

# <a id="400d65dd82be80ed83c7d403a2ab883d" />**Introduction: Antimicrobial Resistance and the Diagnostic Gap**

## <a id="400d65dd82be8096ba50e533c54b21be" />**The Need for Rapid Susceptibility Testing**

<a id="400d65dd82be80b2b15aedc85f5c6342" />Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is among the most pressing global health challenges of this century. Bacterial AMR was associated with 4.71 million deaths worldwide in 2021, and forecasting models project 39 million cumulative deaths attributable to resistant infections between 2025 and 2050 <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"cragoUnderstandingCanadiansKnowledge"}]} />. Roughly 1 in 6 laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections in 2023 were resistant to the antibiotics used to treat them.

<a id="400d65dd82be80df91bdc56bb9838ae7" />These numbers are usually framed as a drug discovery problem. We came to see it as a diagnostic one. In routine practice, an antibiotic is chosen before anyone knows whether it will work. Empiric prescribing is a reasonable response to a slow laboratory protocol, but it is also an evolutionary accelerant. Every course given to an organism that was never susceptible applies selective pressure without producing a cure <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"regoesPharmacodynamicFunctionsMultiparameter2004"},{"n":3,"key":"johnsonCenturyEnzymeKinetic2013"}]} />.

<a id="400d65dd82be80f9ad21fa7d80d7b4fd" />Unfortunately, the clinical consequences are felt. In a study of complicated skin and soft tissue infections in Central Australia, 36.7% of patients received empiric antimicrobials that were inactive against the pathogen actually isolated from them <Cite refs={[{"n":4,"key":"harchHighBurdenComplicated2017"}]} />. The treatment failed, a second antibiotic was chosen, and the resistance that caused the failure was frequently never recorded.

<a id="400d65dd82be8034833bc10fd4a146a6" />That conversation changed the shape of our project. We stopped designing a test that explains a treatment failure after the fact, and started designing one that runs **before** the prescription is written.

## <a id="400d65dd82be8025bd5dd9196c04a308" />**Why Antibiotic Susceptibility Testing Is Slow**

<a id="400d65dd82be807196c1db153b2ca21f" />Clinically, susceptibility is determined using the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial capable of inhibiting bacterial growth <Cite refs={[{"n":5,"key":"kowalska-krochmalMinimumInhibitoryConcentration2021"}]} />. The MIC is compared against a clinical breakpoint to decide whether standard dosing can achieve inhibitory concentrations at the site of infection.

<a id="400d65dd82be80b396cbdc70406d4be5" />The problem is not the logic but the latency. Conventional antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) requires isolating the organism, growing it to a standard amount for inoculation, and then measuring growth inhibition across a dilution series. Gold-standard AST effectively takes two days <Cite refs={[{"n":6,"key":"smithRapidSusceptibilityTesting2019"}]} />. For urine specifically, culture plus susceptibility takes 48 to 72 hours, which means most patients have already started and maybe even finished, a course of antibiotics before the result arrives <Cite refs={[{"n":7,"key":"narayanRapidIdentificationSusceptibility"}]} />. Because the test is slow, it is used sparingly. And because it is used sparingly, resistance accumulates unobserved.

<a id="400d65dd82be802ab0fdf01299994f58" />Urinary tract infections (UTIs) make this gap concrete. They are among the most common bacterial infections in both community and hospital settings <Cite refs={[{"n":8,"key":"foxmanEpidemiologyUrinaryTract2010"}]} />, and the majority are managed empirically. Since June 2023, pharmacists in British Columbia have been able to prescribe for uncomplicated UTIs under the Minor Ailments and Contraception Service, with no laboratory test indicating whether the infection is resistant <Cite refs={[{"n":9,"key":"harnettPharmacistsNowPrescribing2023"}]} />. Pharmacist UTI assessments are now the most common minor-ailment assessment in the province with roughly 96,000 per year and many often are uninformed about susceptibility results.

## <a id="400d65dd82be800eb175ca32199f5a5a" />**Detecting Resistance Through Antibiotic Inactivation**

<a id="400d65dd82be80ecb6c5f4f80f31d78c" />Bacteria resist antibiotics through several distinct strategies: enzymatic degradation or chemical modification of the drug, alteration of the drug target, reduced membrane permeability, and active efflux <Cite refs={[{"n":10,"key":"abbasAntibioticResistanceKey2024"},{"n":11,"key":"wrightBacterialResistanceAntibiotics2005"}]} />.

<a id="400d65dd82be804a84afef5daf97e031" />One of these is unusually tractable for a diagnostic. When resistance is enzymatic, the antibiotic itself is physically destroyed or chemically altered. β-lactamases hydrolyse the four-membered β-lactam ring, abolishing the drug's ability to acylate penicillin-binding proteins <Cite refs={[{"n":11,"key":"wrightBacterialResistanceAntibiotics2005"},{"n":12,"key":"pandeyBetaLactamAntibiotics2023"}]} />. Aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes acetylate, phosphorylate, or adenylate their substrate, with the resulting loss of activity depending on the specific enzyme–antibiotic pair <Cite refs={[{"n":11,"key":"wrightBacterialResistanceAntibiotics2005"}]} />.

<a id="400d65dd82be80ed8e92edec08a4f531" />In both cases, resistance leaves a trace *in the medium*, not only in the genome. A resistant sample consumes the antibiotic. A susceptible sample does not. This is the observation FAST is built on. Rather than culturing the patient's organism to inhibition, which is what makes conventional AST slow, we ask whether the antibiotic survived contact with the patient's sample, and we use a second, engineered organism as the instrument that reports the answer.

## <a id="400d65dd82be80f5b80fc18d96ba1df3" />Choosing our Antibiotic Panel

<a id="400d65dd82be800cae10f834cafc17b2" />This mechanism also constrains what FAST can test. A platform that detects antibiotic inactivation will detect enzymatic and modification-based resistance, and will **not** detect resistance arising from target-site mutation or efflux. We selected our antibiotic panel accordingly, and we state the limitation plainly rather than designing around it.

<a id="400d65dd82be803880c9f83f08b67334" />Following our consultation with Dr. Hancock, we narrowed to the two classes most relevant to UTI treatment whose dominant resistance mechanisms are compatible with our readout:

<a id="400d65dd82be808498c5eb0e61ba8185" />**β-lactams** share a four-membered β-lactam ring and include the penicillins and cephalosporins <Cite refs={[{"n":12,"key":"pandeyBetaLactamAntibiotics2023"}]} />. They act by binding penicillin-binding proteins, including the transpeptidases that cross-link peptidoglycan, preventing formation of a stable cell wall. We selected **amoxicillin** and **cephalexin** as representatives. β-lactamase-mediated hydrolysis is the resistance route most relevant to FAST.

<a id="400d65dd82be806487b2c1f625c4ad80" />**Aminoglycosides** act on the bacterial ribosome, disrupting accurate protein synthesis <Cite refs={[{"n":13,"key":"tsaiImpactAminoglycosidesDynamics2013"}]} />. We selected **gentamicin** and **tobramycin**. They act on a different cellular target from β-lactams and can remain useful against some β-lactam-resistant organisms, so resistance to one class does not establish susceptibility to the other. Antibiotic-specific testing is therefore essential, and a single-drug test is not sufficient.

## <a id="400d65dd82be802c8a00d37ae5c1cca2" />**Challenges with Current AST Technologies**

<a id="400d65dd82be80a7929ad8865753e57c" />Broth microdilution, agar dilution, and disk diffusion are inexpensive, well-standardized, and interpretable against established breakpoints <Cite refs={[{"n":14,"key":"wiegandAgarBrothDilution2008"},{"n":15,"key":"hattabRapidPhenotypicGenotypic2024"}]} />. They are also slow (48–72 h including isolation) and require a microbiology laboratory, trained personnel, and specimen transport <Cite refs={[{"n":6,"key":"smithRapidSusceptibilityTesting2019"},{"n":7,"key":"narayanRapidIdentificationSusceptibility"}]} />. In British Columbia's outpatient payment schedule, a positive urine culture is reimbursed at \$38.02: \$19.57 for culture, \$6.84 for MALDI-TOF identification, and \$11.61 for susceptibility <Cite refs={[{"n":16,"key":"governmentofbcOutpatientPaymentSchedule"}]} />.

<a id="400d65dd82be80fe8b7be95db93fca8f" />Automated platforms shorten the susceptibility step itself, but still begin from an isolated colony, so the upstream culture delay remains <Cite refs={[{"n":6,"key":"smithRapidSusceptibilityTesting2019"},{"n":15,"key":"hattabRapidPhenotypicGenotypic2024"}]} />. bioMérieux's VITEK 2 returns MICs in a manufacturer-reported 6–8 hours, and Gradientech reports 2–4 hours for bloodstream isolates <Cite refs={[{"n":17,"key":"AntimicrobialStewardshipSolutionsa"},{"n":18,"key":"GradientechUltrarapidAST"}]} />. Both are laboratory instruments rather than point-of-care devices.

<a id="400d65dd82be80a8b77fe5126a9eee75" />Direct-from-specimen systems are already quite quick. The Sysmex PA-100 AST System returns UTI susceptibility results in a manufacturer-reported 30–45 minutes using a nanofluidic cartridge <Cite refs={[{"n":19,"key":"PA100ASTSystem"}]} />. QolorPhAST, developed at McGill, reported a 36-minute turnaround with 91.81% average categorical agreement and 86.4% average essential agreement on blinded UTI patient samples <Cite refs={[{"n":20,"key":"mUltrarapidNanoplasmonicColorimetry"}]} />. Both, however, depend on a dedicated proprietary analyzer, and these instruments require substantial upfront capital investment.

<a id="400d65dd82be8005b4c3dcce7d267af1" />This is the gap we aim to fill. Conventional testing is cheap but slow. Automated systems are faster but lab-bound. Rapid point-of-care platforms are fast but instrument-expensive. A pharmacy counter in a community clinic, or a nursing station in a remote community, sits outside all three.

## <a id="400d65dd82be802db16cfabc3a3ba163" />**FAST: Leveraging Bacterial Stress Responses as a Susceptibility Readout**

<a id="400d65dd82be80a3a3d2ee40a3dec30b" />To close that gap, we designed **FAST**: a microfluidic platform that determines whether the bacteria in a patient sample can inactivate a candidate antibiotic, using engineered *E. coli* biosensors as the readout.

<a id="400d65dd82be809e9d8df362c471ef3e" />Our biosensors do not detect the antibiotic molecule directly. They detect the cellular stress that an active antibiotic causes, by placing superfolder green fluorescent protein (sfGFP) under the control of a stress-responsive promoter. sfGFP matures in approximately 13.6 minutes, which is fast enough that fluorescence is not the rate-limiting step in our readout <Cite refs={[{"n":15,"key":"hattabRapidPhenotypicGenotypic2024"}]} />.

<a id="400d65dd82be80229e74f13c5fb0fc90" />**The *cpxP* promoter.** The envelope stress response is a Gram-negative pathway activated when the integrity of the cell envelope is disrupted <Cite refs={[{"n":21,"key":"raivioCpxEnvelopeStress1999"}]} />. Under unstressed conditions, the small periplasmic protein CpxP interacts with the sensor kinase CpxA and holds the pathway inactive. Under envelope stress, CpxP is degraded, CpxA autophosphorylates and transfers phosphate to the response regulator CpxR, and phosphorylated CpxR activates downstream genes including *cpxP* itself <Cite refs={[{"n":22,"key":"surmannMolecularProteomeAnalyses2016"}]} />. Because β-lactams act by inactivating the enzymes required for cell wall synthesis, this pathway is a direct route from β-lactam activity to transcriptional output. We built a $\mathrm{P}_\textit{cpxP}$–*sfGFP* reporter to exploit it.

<a id="400d65dd82be80e28a8acbc5c89d21d4" />**The *recA* promoter.** The bacterial SOS response is a conserved global DNA-damage response <Cite refs={[{"n":20,"key":"mUltrarapidNanoplasmonicColorimetry"}]} />. When antibiotic exposure generates reactive metabolites and single-stranded DNA accumulates, RecA forms an active nucleoprotein filament that promotes self-cleavage of the LexA repressor, relieving repression of LexA-regulated genes including *recA* <Cite refs={[{"n":23,"key":"lambertSuperfolderGFPFPbasea"}]} />. We built a $\mathrm{P}_\textit{recA}$–*sfGFP* reporter as a second, mechanistically independent sensor.

# <a id="400d65dd82be80ad9986e25be7d38df2" />**How the FAST device works**

<a id="400d65dd82be80beb907d1890d226002" />The test separates degradation from detection into two chambers.

<a id="400d65dd82be80ab8e60e10281cdde22" />**Chamber A — degradation.** The patient sample and a candidate antibiotic are loaded together and incubated. If the patient's bacteria carry a β-lactamase or an aminoglycoside-modifying enzyme, the antibiotic is inactivated here.

<a id="400d65dd82be809ab928e265ff0c9bf6" />**Chamber B — detection.** The mixture is filtered and the remaining antibiotic reaches the biosensor strain.

- <a id="400d65dd82be8056bbdcfd5b896b56bb" />**Susceptible sample →** the antibiotic survives Chamber A, arrives intact, triggers the Cpx or SOS pathway, and sfGFP is expressed. This leads to some degree of fluorescence that is proportional to cell stres&#x73;**.**
- <a id="400d65dd82be807eb7bde3c04af06b0b" />**Resistant sample →** the antibiotic was degraded or modified in Chamber A, the stress pathway is not sufficiently induced, and sfGFP expression stays near baseline. **No fluorescence.**

<a id="400d65dd82be8093acd2f33a8471ef63" />The output is a binary, visually interpretable readout: fluorescence means the drug still works against this patient's infection.

# <a id="400d65dd82be80a087dfe707a3b17e0b" />**Our Models: PRISM and the Degradation Model**

<a id="400d65dd82be8085825dec158c76c988" />**PRISM (**&#x50;romoter Response Index and Selection Map) is a relational database and analysis pipeline that integrates RNA-seq expression data (GSE220559), RegulonDB regulatory annotations, and a reference genome to produce a traceable shortlist of antibiotic-responsive promoters for experimental screening <Cite refs={[{"n":21,"key":"raivioCpxEnvelopeStress1999"},{"n":22,"key":"surmannMolecularProteomeAnalyses2016"}]} />. It reduced 4,176 measured genes to 925 candidates and then to a proposed panel of **12 promoters**, six per antibiotic class. Every selection traces backwards: a panel entry links to a promoter, which links to a candidate gene, which links to the differential-expression result and source locus that produced it, with reference releases and file checksums recorded at each stage. Manual curation is stored separately from measured evidence.

<a id="400d65dd82be80d29ed1e8871fef3d41" />PRISM exists because expression strength alone was not a sufficient selection criterion, it returned more genes than could be tested and failed to separate independent promoters from genes sharing regulatory context. It also has its limits as gene-level RNA is not promoter activity, 49.3% of our candidates have no promoter annotation at all, and no panel promoter has yet been shown to drive reporter output.

<a id="400d65dd82be80bead64c3373cf5152a" />**The degradation model** is a non-spatial system of three coupled ODEs answering a design question: how long must an antibiotic be exposed to a resistance enzyme before enough degradation occurs to be measurable? It couples Hill-type enzyme induction, Michaelis–Menten degradation, and a sigmoidal $E_{max}$ pharmacodynamic term for bacterial kill and regrowth <Cite refs={[{"n":24,"key":"zgur-bertokDNADamageRepair2013"},{"n":25,"key":"shapiroAntimicrobialInducedDNADamage2015"}]} />. It predicts that the bacterial population may fall under antibiotic exposure and then recover once degradation drops killing below growth, a behaviour that directly affects how long Chamber A should run. The model currently provides theoretical predictions rather than experimentally validated degradation times.

# <a id="400d65dd82be8003b9cbc5c69b7deac5" />**Our Hardware: Chip, Detector, and Bioreactor**

<a id="400d65dd82be80e9a9b3e3b6b7601cec" />**The microfluidic chip** incubates the patient sample with the candidate antibiotic and delivers the result to the biosensor wells. It is cast in PDMS from FDM-printed PLA molds and plasma-bonded to a standard glass microscope slide, costing approximately **\$1–5 per chip**, with no cleanroom and no resin printer. Seven design cycles got us there, four of which failed. Two findings generalize: mold walls are necessary, or the channel floats during curing and becomes encased in set PDMS; and surface finish determines whether the chip leaks, with the printer's ironing function producing a surface that plasma-bonds reliably against a non-ironed control that leaked significantly. A hydraulic resistance model, solved as an equivalent resistor network, predicted a 185% pressure-drop difference between the first and last well in a seven-well linear design, which fell to 32% after moving to two wells. Fluid can be driven through the chip by hydrostatic pressure from a cut pipette tip, removing the need for a syringe pump.

<a id="400d65dd82be8029a133e0f5c415026a" />**The fluorescence detector** reads the output for **\$204.48**, against commercial instruments starting around \$630 and reaching beyond \$42,000. The saving comes from the optical layout. Conventional detectors arrange source, sample, and detector at 90° and use both excitation and emission filters, where a single excitation filter alone can cost around \$300. We aligned a 488 nm LED, the sample, a 500 nm long-pass filter, and a photodiode along one vertical axis, eliminating a filter entirely. A 10 MΩ transimpedance stage with programmable gain and the Arduino's 1.1 V internal reference gives approximately 1 pA resolution. We document our first board's failure in full: it was adapted from a published circuit designed for ±9 V rails and powered instead from 5 V, which drove the op-amps outside their rated supply and saturated the output, so the circuit did not respond to light at all.

<a id="400d65dd82be8015a700ed8adff9f994" />**The bioreactor** produces the biosensor cultures for **\$106.68** from off-the-shelf components, monitoring temperature and pH with an Arduino. Across three validation experiments it reached **2.38 to 2.74 times** the normalized growth of a conventional flask control.

## <a id="400d65dd82be80c8b42fde85984a2a5a" />**Standardization of Measurement**

<a id="400d65dd82be802db0cdd41234f482db" />Measurement is not a supporting activity for FAST, it is the readout the entire platform depends on. A susceptibility call means something only if fluorescence can be measured reproducibly and read against controls.

<a id="400d65dd82be802ca72ee7074055aee5" />We have documented the full chain: baseline growth and basal expression for both strains and wild type, $OD_{600}$ calibration curves, the MIC titration that established the subinhibitory window, same-plate untreated controls as the fold-change denominator, blank media subtraction, and a per-run LED-on-minus-LED-off baseline on the detector.

# <a id="400d65dd82be800aa688ec221a711257" />**Impact and Advocacy**

<a id="400d65dd82be80fe8dbff769295dd04e" />We spoke with nine clinicians, researchers, and patient partners about how resistance affects people in British Columbia specifically, because the patient-oriented evidence base for Canada is thin. Those conversations reached every level of the project.

<a id="400d65dd82be80b884f4da01229bd4bd" />Dr. Hancock's input determined which antibiotics our biosensors detect and moved our validation target to UTIs. Dr. Larry Mróz, Patient and Community Engagement Lead at Health Research BC, determined how we were permitted to engage patients at all. Remodelling the work around patient engagement rather than research and producing the consent form that governed every subsequent conversation reinforced strong practices of respectful research. Sunny Loo, a patient representative at BCCDC and a retired pharmacist, reviewed our patient-facing website and his feedback reached our front-end code, setting font sizes and animation speeds. What came up in nearly every conversation was not the science. It was isolation.

<a id="400d65dd82be80388dc6f1241e8b9e00" />Dr. Richard Lester, an infectious disease physician at Vancouver General Hospital, described patients who believe *they* are resistant rather than the bacteria. Dr. Anthony Lieu described patients labelled, isolated, and visited only in PPE — fewer assessments, in a more hostile environment. Dr. Edith Blondel-Hill described travel-history questions that leave patients feeling ethnically targeted. Lesley Kooner described the burden following patients home, to families bleaching bathrooms.

<a id="400d65dd82be803d8decf8516654f0e7" />*View our* [*Human Practices pages*](/ubc-vancouver/human-practices/investigation-2---integrated-human-practices-ihp) *for more information on these experts and stakeholders.*

<a id="400d65dd82be80a88d44f0f8542d5f63" />Because the diagnostic alone does not address what patients told us, we built two things alongside it. **"AMR: the human side"** is a patient-facing website with a visit-preparation tool, provider-sourced educational content, attributed patient stories, and a moderated peer space. Built to a hard specification, we made sure to include no personal data, no tracking, no third-party embeds, no accounts, and no auto-published comments. We also drafted plain-language printable pamphlets, guided by Dr. Chan's recommendation, covering MRSA and VRE, transmission, cleaning, precautions, and when to seek care. A draft went to Fraser Health to explore distribution options.

## <a id="400d65dd82be80b28756f51c03b5e3e5" />Education

<a id="400d65dd82be80a29ca8cc87f6243bf7" />Misinformation about AMR is not evenly distributed. Canadian survey data show knowledge gaps concentrated among adults aged 18–24, people with high school education or less, and non-English-speaking households <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"cragoUnderstandingCanadiansKnowledge"}]} />. We aimed our outreach accordingly: seven workshops with UBC Geering Up, the **@amrexplained** Instagram campaign, and a 40-minute documentary-style interview with Dr. Lester.

<a id="400d65dd82be80b2ad7ec4f1dce3d26d" />Our educational work also produced two resources other teams can adopt directly: a six-part wet lab training video series, and **Building BioBricks**, a workshop delivered for UBC Science 101, a free course offered to residents of Vancouver's Downtown Eastside, in which participants model a promoter, RBS, coding sequence, and terminator in Lego, assemble a fluorescent biosensor, and swap parts to model the Parts Registry. It requires no laboratory, no reagents, and no prior biology background.

# <a id="400d65dd82be8099a91eda9fca27fb96" />**Beyond UTIs**

<a id="400d65dd82be807db520d78350ca0fb8" />FAST was built as a platform, not a single test. Stress-responsive biosensors, low-cost microfluidics, and accessible fluorescence detection are not specific to any one infection or antibiotic class. UTIs are our initial validation target, chosen because urine is easy to simulate and because British Columbia's prescribing-pharmacist channel makes the diagnostic gap unusually visible there.

<a id="400d65dd82be801a9262d5c90a8758e1" />The same reader accepts new chip panels. Extending FAST means validating additional resistance enzymes and antibiotic classes against the existing hardware, then additional infection types and settings: urgent care, long-term care, emergency departments. Each new panel requires its own validation, and we do not claim otherwise.

<a id="400d65dd82be801ea6d3dd66dafd5a4c" />There are other future applications that we have not yet explored. Our chip is not limited to clinical applications but also for environmental and bioremediation applications like testing for antibiotic resistance in nature or wastewater.
