# <a id="a99cf92d5cd24e8ab7c39123ac33ac2a" />What is integrated Human Practices?

<a id="ac4b5b45705441b1870acee1089b0ba4" />In iGEM, integrated Human Practices (iHP) means engaging with experts, stakeholders, and communities so that our work is informed by people in the field and reflects the voices of the groups it affects. By holding these conversations throughout our Design-Build-Test-Learn cycles, our team came to understand the broader context, impacts, and real-world needs surrounding *FAST*.

<a id="07d46dd217a549db8a8cdb4a0015d6ae" />We counted our Human Practices work as integrated only where the project would have been different without it, so each conversation below is paired with what it changed.

<a id="56496107ac6247708ffbd1818c777534" />Antimicrobial resistance is a social problem as well as a clinical one. A faster susceptibility test helps only if it reaches the people who need it and the system around it can act on the result. Our Human Practices work therefore moved through three stages, from an [Investigation 1 - Literature Review](/ubc-vancouver/human-practices/investigation-1---literature-review) of the scientific and clinical literature, to [Investigation 2 - integrated Human Practices (iHP)](/ubc-vancouver/human-practices/investigation-2---integrated-human-practices-ihp) with clinicians and patients, and finally into concrete action in response to what we heard.

# <a id="ceb4bf9db82e47ffbc16119e33bc3b53" />Stakeholder Finding

<a id="3a8331abb201468d8553dadf08ce0b01" />Our literature review familiarized the team with the mechanisms and clinical management of resistance, but it told us little about what living with AMR is like for patients. We chose whom to approach based on what each person could tell us that the literature could not. Researchers knew what a test needed to detect, and infectious disease physicians and a bedside nurse knew what happens to patients once resistance is found. Patient partners could describe that experience firsthand, and a patient engagement specialist could advise a student team without ethics approval on how to involve patients at all. In total we spoke with nine clinicians, researchers, and patient partners.

## <a id="1bc796801fe44c8886c7aebe6f32a182" />Companionship Support

<a id="cbedfcb1724b46feb8d0215f47b9f4fd" />Isolation came up in nearly every clinical interview, and our patient partners confirmed that there was nowhere for people with resistant infections to find one another. In response we built a website, AMR: the human side, where each section is scoped to a specific need raised by a specific voice. The narrative homepage and Stories section carry patient testimony in patients' own words, the Learn section draws on provider and literature-sourced descriptions, the Visit-Prep tool responds to Dr. Lester's point that informed patients participate more in their own care, and the Community section provides a moderated peer space.

<a id="64214e7ae2b649d9aed9b1a861c4286a" />The site went through three documented review cycles before reaching patients broadly, each recorded as a Design-Build-Test-Learn iteration. Dr. Mróz reviewed it twice, first validating the overall approach while flagging that we had not been clear enough about what happens to information patients provide, and then identifying audience clarity, moderation disclosure, and terms of use as priorities. Sunny Loo reviewed the revised version from a patient's perspective and pushed us on accessibility, asking for larger text, slower panning in the narrative, clearer still images, and an explicit acknowledgement step before anyone posts. He also advised keeping the forum focused on sharing experiences rather than exchanging contact details until we understand how people use it, and recommended seeking legal guidance before expanding further. We are currently iterating on the site with Norma and will share it with local patient networks through Dr. Mróz once it is ready.

<a id="f95128b450a2482aa56af7c789d50a96" />Visit our [Barrier 1 - Companionship Support](/ubc-vancouver/human-practices/barrier-1---companionship-support) page to learn more!

## <a id="8b196507faf44aef9b930a6dc797213e" />Informational Support

<a id="a9a664554bea4895ac2304c19b9b00df" />Dr. Chan and Lesley Kooner both recommended plain-language printed materials in patients' first languages as the most effective way to reach patients directly, Dr. Lieu reinforced that education should be personalized and delivered on admission, and Norma's experience of receiving antibiotics without explanation showed us what happens when it is not. We therefore focused this work on printable materials. Our work on closing this gap is documented on our [Barrier 2 - Informational Support ](/ubc-vancouver/human-practices/barrier-2---informational-support-) page.

## <a id="8e11909ad0e24a3687d0eca145b87100" />Demystifying AMR

<a id="125bbf9fefab45c392c1217840cf8c22" />Our literature review identified adolescents and young adults aged 18 to 24 as a group where misinformation about antimicrobial mechanisms is concentrated, and our iHP interviews corroborated that gaps in public understanding remain a barrier. Dr. Hancock's advice to lead with consequences rather than statistics, and Dr. Lester's account of common misconceptions, shaped how we responded on two fronts.

<a id="46aa8cafa5f34de0ba430e0ee3cd23e4" />For adolescents, we partnered with UBC Geering Up Engineering Outreach to run workshops during their K-12 summer camps. We built the workshops around active-learning activities. Students played a mutation game translating DNA sequences and sorting mutations, toured the Hallam Lab to see gel electrophoresis, and worked through a treatment plan activity in which each group cared for a patient facing a real barrier such as cost, distance from a pharmacy, or a penicillin allergy. The debrief made the point, drawn directly from our clinical interviews, that resistance is shaped by access, affordability, and who gets listened to in healthcare. In the Explorations in Engineering Jr workshop, students designed their own microfluidic channels in crayon on paper and tested them with coloured water. We found that the paper absorbed fluid poorly and leaked, so for the Girls in BMEG workshop we rebuilt the activity using paper towels and paint.

<a id="a1f82ee0e8ca4f729c27327f67c44b05" />For the general public, we developed an Instagram campaign, @amrexplained, combining carousel posts on foundational concepts, short-form reels produced with our Design subteam covering the psychosocial impacts of resistant infections, and the long-form interview with Dr. Lester for viewers seeking greater depth.

<a id="dca0f5aa5d78483e80c231a39593b582" />Visit our [Demystifying AMR](/ubc-vancouver/human-practices/demystifying-amr) page to learn more!

## <a id="85a5fb96f287463f8bdcc735239d9c15" />Wet Lab

<a id="99b286f997224367b9597c792b455f94" />Integration reached the bench as well. Our conversation with Dr. Hancock determined which antibiotics our biosensors needed to detect, and his point about clinicians switching drugs rather than recording resistance is what led us toward a test designed to be run before prescribing rather than after a failure.

<a id="82573a2493b44aa3a6c33dd6ea02571e" />For our promoter library screen, we consulted the lab manager at Biofactorial, UBC's High-Throughput Biology and Automation Facility, throughout the design of the experiment. Their feedback covered both scientific design, such as how many source wells to use as a representative subset and how many concentration levels were needed to capture a complete curve, and practical feasibility, including how to structure a CSV for the antibiotic source plate and which equipment at the facility suited our protocol. We held meetings as needed and iterated the experimental design until we were confident in the results it would produce.

# <a id="d6ce8e9f62c840f0a4fb5cae82cc10e1" />Integration Throughout the Season

<a id="1d51f485a33c46ce8cc1efda131ac87c" />Our Human Practices work ran through the whole season. It began with a literature review that set out the clinical and scientific picture, moved into conversations that filled in what the literature missed, and then fed back into the work at every level. Dr. Hancock's input determined which antibiotics our biosensors detect, and Dr. Mróz's determined how we were permitted to engage patients. Sunny Loo's review changed the font sizes and animation speeds in our front-end code, and Amin Abdolkhani's advice kept our chip molds in PLA.

<a id="f5f6a3a2d89f42259153b7c849c47418" />Several stakeholders also returned to the project more than once. Dr. Mróz advised on our approach and then reviewed the website twice, Sunny was interviewed and later reviewed the site as a patient, and Norma is now co-designing its next iteration. Going back to the same people let us check our response against what they had originally told us. Our [Our Journey](/ubc-vancouver/human-practices/our-journey) page records how our own thinking changed along the way.

# <a id="cf08f1f443b040c8a158fb1c3af2f02c" />Responsible Practice

<a id="9ebea4e7825a432c833df4bd70d1caad" />Asking patients to share difficult experiences comes with obligations to them.

<a id="db0fbeafbc5144f5a91b43b84c3b62cb" />Our engagement with patients was governed by an informed consent form, reviewed and signed by both Sunny and Norma before their interviews, which stated the topics in advance and made clear that no medical or physical information would be collected. Both were told they could skip any question or withdraw at any point. Norma's visibility on our Instagram campaign was deliberately limited to protect her privacy.

<a id="818f5be7cf914a06bea7ac234d4146bf" />The same principle governed the website. We set the site's privacy constraints before writing any code. The site collects and stores no personal health information, uses no tracking or third-party embeds, and keeps the visit-prep document entirely in the user's browser. The community forum requires no account, assigns each person a made-up username, and runs submissions through an obscenity-filtering library alongside a custom term list, so that comments are never auto-published. Nothing reached patients broadly until Dr. Mróz had reviewed it twice and a patient had reviewed it once.

<a id="6df772dc57d64d9d863326f4a7305218" />Several things remain unfinished or untested. The website remains in active iteration with Norma and has not yet been released to patient networks. Our informational support work is still in progress. We have not measured whether any of our educational materials change understanding or behaviour, and we have documented our outreach as activity undertaken rather than as impact demonstrated.

# <a id="9211e27e631e443ca5c459a4f429ecf8" />What Other Teams Can Reuse

<a id="a9a2f54017bd49918405d08999d4aa0d" />The most portable part of this work is our approach to patient consent and the framing behind it. Any iGEM team without ethics approval that wants to engage patients faces the same constraint we did, and Dr. Mróz's distinction between patient engagement and qualitative research is what made our approach defensible. We have documented that distinction, the consent form, and the limits we placed on ourselves, and the consent form is available on our [Investigation 2 - integrated Human Practices (iHP)](/ubc-vancouver/human-practices/investigation-2---integrated-human-practices-ihp) page for other teams to adapt.

<a id="dfa514597eee4d6aa9972ec9dfded586" />Our barrier methodology is also reusable. We scoped every section of our website to one evidenced need traced back to one named voice, and recorded that mapping in the table above. A team can apply the same structure to a different condition and a different set of stakeholders.
