# <a id="3e5d65dd82be80f0ae2bc5554786b6b0" />Abstract

<Callout icon="">
  <a id="3fdd65dd82be800fa6b1eca1360f5bcd" />As our project this year was to develop an *E. coli* promoter-GFP biosensor, we sought to identify high quality promoters—characterized by dynamic range, low basal expression, and low cross-activation—that enable quantifying presence of antibiotic in solution. Our biosensor depends on a promoter that produces a strong, rapid, and specific fluorescence when a relevant antibiotic is present, therefore promoter performance is critical to the success of FAST’s accuracy: a leaky promoter would yield false susceptibility calls, a slow promoter would reduce our advantage over culture-based testing, etc. However, rather than relying solely on literature review, we wanted to validate our findings through a library screen. We screened a promoter-GFP library against four antibiotics: Amoxicillin, Cephalexin, Gentamicin, and Tobramycin. These antibiotics were chosen as they are β-lactams and aminoglycosides, pertinent to our project for their clinical relevance to UTIs.

  <a id="3fdd65dd82be80cfa295f1cb77877fc7" />To characterize over 200 promoter candidates, we developed the experimental design of a high-throughput experiment executed in collaboration with Biofactorial, an automation facility. Our experiment consisted of three components: assay development, the library screen, then sequencing. By exposing the promoter library to each antibiotic at a selected sub-inhibitory concentration, we collected GFP and OD600 measurements and ranked candidates based on sustained induction, response speed, and low baseline expression. Through our criterion we were able to identify the top ten promoters from the library, and sequenced the top candidates back to its well for further validation.
</Callout>

# <a id="3e5d65dd82be8079af05c75febc5617c" />Method

<a id="3e8d65dd82be80878d35e3da3a754df1" />The objective of the Promoter Library was to identify top promoter candidates out of a library of over 200 promoter candidates. The library consisted of promoter-GFP constructs on well plates. The antibiotic response of these constructs were unknown, as well as the promoter barcodes. Our experiment consisted of three parts: an assay development to select sub-inhibitory concentrations for each antibiotic, the high-throughput library screen exposing the promoter-GFP plates to the antibiotics at the selected concentrations, and then finally Oxford Nanopore sequencing the top candidates. As we had over 200 promoter candidates, doing this experiment on our own bench top would not be effective. Thus, we collaborated with Biofactorial, a High-Throughput Biology and Automation Facility here at UBC’s Life Sciences Institute. Biofactorial was able to execute the assay testing and library screen for us, so we worked in collaboration to develop the proposal and experimental design.

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80bdb51cf62054830b9f" />**Note: complete protocols can be found under** [**Wet Lab - Lab Notebook**](/ubc-vancouver/wet-lab/lab-notebook/biofactorial-part-1-mic-assay)

## <a id="3f9d65dd82be8014888ace9e8aff5203" />**Part 1: Identifying Sub-inhibitory Concentrations**

<a id="3f9d65dd82be80958a9eeb4fa987baf4" />Before beginning the library screen, it was critical to first develop an assay to select sub-inhibitory concentrations for each antibiotic that we would expose the library to during the subsequent screening. We used literature minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values as a reference point for our experimental design, creating a dilution series around the literature MIC values to capture a curve.  Literature review was also conducted on all of the antibiotics to ensure solubility at low concentrations.

## <a id="3e5d65dd82be807eabedf015927c482f" />**Part 2: High-throughput Library Screen**

<a id="3e5d65dd82be8082b49fd23996e892d8" />The objective of part 2 was to now conduct the entire library screen by exposing all promoter-GFP constructs to the antibiotics at the selected concentration levels determined from part 1. This required creating 5 replicates of the library plates, so we could expose the library to each of the four antibiotics while having one control plate with no antibiotic exposure. In total, we had 30 plates. Biofactorial was able to carry out the plate replication for us, however, the library plates were not compatible with the Echo Acoustic Liquid Handler. Instead, an alternative pinning machine was used, but this could only dispense 70 nL rather than the original 50 nL of our *E. coli* cells. This simply meant that our starting $OD_{600}$ was slightly higher, but this did not impact our results. From here, the *E. coli* plates were incubated with LB media and the antibiotic for 8 hours, following the same data collection guidelines as part 1: measure $OD_{600}$ every 30 minutes along with GFP fluorescence with 488 nm excitation and detected at 510 nm emission.

<a id="400d65dd82be804abdddc8b4b5aa6435" />The above graph was produced by normalizing each promoter’s GFP response by $OD_{600}$ and its control. Using the above graph, we can derive a handful of conclusions. First, most of the promoters are clouded around growth = 1 and GFP induction = 1, meaning most of the library was unaffected by the sub-inhibitory concentrations. However, the top candidates are recognizable as they are outliers. The top candidates were hits with high GFP induction (\~ 3x to 12x), with the top hit being 11.68x induction under Amoxicillin. We can also see that majority of the top candidates were for Cephalexin, especially without inhibiting growth (candidates lie more within the right side of the cloud). On the other hand, Tobramycin lies mostly to the lower left, meaning growth is slowed and GFP is lower per cell. Amoxicillin is mostly induced without slowing growth.

<br id="400d65dd82be803b9ba1e36c2603ae86" />

<a id="400d65dd82be809ba680ec392f5e4609" />However, given that the library was initially on 96-well plates and had been re-mapped onto our 384-well plate, we also needed to account for this. Through this theory, each single well in the 96-well plate corresponds to a 2x2 block of four wells in the 384-well plate. Therefore we aimed for 4 wells from each 2x2 quadrant across the 384-well plate. Furthermore, we deliberately picked four wells from plate 6 (one from each quadrant) to determine the quadranting order.

# <a id="3fcd65dd82be806c8729e1abcaac83d4" />Errors, Limitations, and Future Work

<a id="400d65dd82be8047b929d2b42190fcef" />**Errors**

<a id="400d65dd82be80b6b63fc8a73e77be91" />Our experimental outputs came out generally as expected until we reached sequencing. The first error we ran into was growing the cultures of the top 10 candidates, resulting in only 7 successful inoculations. As a result, this limited the range of promoters we could sequence. Furthermore, upon receiving the GeneBank files from Plasmidsaurus, only candidates 1, 4, and 7 were successfully sequenced. Therefore we ultimately only determined sequencing for 3 promoters. The remaining samples generated sequencing data but were unable to assemble a plasmid sequence. This can be attributed to a variety of reasons such as if there are multiple plasmids in one sample, or if the structure is unusually complex.

<br id="400d65dd82be80089bf3f878f33c0be3" />

<a id="400d65dd82be8084aaf7d54f7cc87654" />**Limitations**

<a id="400d65dd82be80569e60fd9a2e1c2eb9" />Ultimately, various limitations affect the confidence in our conclusions. First, our final promoter sample size was small. Only three candidates were successfully sequenced, and although the malE-malK region appeared twice among them, this is too few data points to generalize to the broader promoter library. Furthermore, determining the reporter-facing gene of the three promoters was done through inference (from the insert’s position relative to GFP) and was not experimentally validated. So, further testing would strengthen these conclusions. Third, some of our assumptions throughout the project can be subjective. For example, the weighting in the WDM used to select top promoter candidates was based on our own individual team’s needs, and could produce a different ranking under a different team’s priorities. Lastly, any noise or interference with the measurements collected from the library screen would carry through to our selection and characterization of top candidates.

<br id="400d65dd82be80f19fd0d1515fe6ebbc" />

<a id="3e8d65dd82be80bd9608e9da7e045123" />**Future Work (addressing our errors)**

<a id="400d65dd82be807bbc49e46d9965ffcc" />Due to time constraints we were not able to carry out our troubleshooting, but we have identified the steps we would like to take to continue building upon this. To continue refining our results, particularly the sequencing aspect, we would like to re-prep and re-sequence the remaining candidates. If the failed assembly were in fact a result of mixed colonies, re-streaking can ensure a fresh culture is grown from the new isolated colony. If re-prepping were not an option (as re-sending our samples to a sequencing company can have time or financial constraints) it would also be useful to locate a reference backbone to aid the failed-sequences and troubleshoot from there. Lastly, to strengthen confidence in measurements from the library screen, sequencing additional clones and/or replicating the screen can increase reliability.
