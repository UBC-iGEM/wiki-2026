# <a id="3fcd65dd82be8018b984cd9454040b94" />Abstract

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80abace3f8c39492880e" />Bacterial biosensors have potential applications in detecting biological relevant signals, such as infection biomarkers or antibiotics in clinical samples <Cite refs={[{"n":1,"key":"capinMicrobialBiosensorsDiagnostics2024"}]} />. However, their performance can be heavily influenced by the chemical and biological conditions of the sample environment in which they operate. In our project, an *E. coli* promoter-GFP biosensor is used to detect pathogen-associated signals in urine. However, urine introduces uncharacterized environmental variability that can potentially alter biosensor output.

<a id="3fcd65dd82be808db2dcecc17e9642c6" />Therefore, this study aims to investigate the performance of the *E. coli* promoter-GFP biosensor under simulated patient-infection conditions and characterize matrix factors that may influence their response including antibiotic concentration, bacterial resistance backgrounds, and environmental conditions such as sodium and glucose concentrations. Using a Design of Experiments (DOE) approach n collaboration with Biofactorial <Cite refs={[{"n":2,"key":"BiofactorialBioLife"}]} />, an automation facility at the University of British Columbia, we systematically evaluate biosensor responses across selected experimental conditions. The resulting data found a significant interaction between antibiotic concentration and glucose for both CpxP and RecA biosensors (p \< 0.0001). Glucose was negatively associated with maximum GFP for CpxP, but not significantly for RecA. Sodium showed no significant effect on either sensor. In a separate growth-medium analysis, LB-Cm was associated with higher maximum GFP per OD for both biosensors, suggesting that growth medium can influence normalized sensor output.

# <a id="3fcd65dd82be806eb174ef24ed0cff57" />Background

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80d39a00d948d7dd42a3" />Urine consists of primarily water, some salts and metabolic byproducts , and other compounds whose concentrations vary between individual and physiological conditions <Cite refs={[{"n":3,"key":"ferraoQuantificationIonsHuman2024"}]} />. These differences can influence the growth of bacteria and the performance of bacterial biosensors intended for use in patient samples. To investigate these effects, we decided to focus determining urine-associated factors that could be experimentally controlled and incorporated into an automated screening workflow.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80b7b079cd636e58491e" />The experimental factors were selected to represent different aspects of the urinary environment. Sodium concentration was included to investigate the effect of  ionic conditions on bacterial growth and biosensor output, while glucose concentration was included to examine whether the availability of an additional carbon source influences bacterial activity or sensor performance. Although pH was initially considered because it can affect bacterial physiology, it was excluded from the final design due to the buffering capacity of LB broth. Finally, antibiotic concentration was investigated to characterize biosensor responses across different exposure levels relevant to antibiotic detection and susceptibility-related experiments.

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80d8a8ffef0d62979a43" />To characterize biosensor performance under simulated infection conditions and guide future validation and optimization, a DOE using the Box Behnken method was used via the JMP software. The Box-Behnken design is especially useful as it approach supports the evaluation of individual factor effects and potential interactions between factors using fewer runs to help identify conditions that may substantially affect the measured response.

## <a id="400d65dd82be8001ac0ef902203e0bf1" />Automation via Biofactorial

<a id="400d65dd82be80e6a878d8bd260054e7" />Physical pipetting of multi-factor matrices across numerous experimental conditions is prone to human error and high variability. Therefore, we partnered with Biofactorial, an automation facility at the University of British Columbia, to execute autonomous liquid handling. Automation allowed experimental conditions to be prepare systematically across multiple wells, supporting consistent implementation of the experimental design and subsequent comparison of biosensor responses.

## <a id="400d65dd82be80babc0dd9b5cae96e34" />2. Experimental Design

<a id="400d65dd82be80bea7a0fd64d57b2375" />The number of possible experimental conditions depends on the number of factors and their respective levels. In a full factorial design, every possible combination of factor levels is tested. The number of possible combinations increases rapidly as the number of factors increases. For (F) factors, each with (L) levels, the total number of combinations is:

<Callout icon="">
  <a id="3fcd65dd82be8060aadbce4372c8c614" />**Factorial Design**

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be80c8860cd6e7378ed988" />let:

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be80348cd2c5ff57f38a7e" />***N*** is the number of experimental conditions

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be80d88c98f92d9e4efc3d" />***L*** is the number of levels per factor

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be8022adb8c2066b697a90" />***F*** is the number of factors

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be805f846de23a2cb4b99e" />then:

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be8008904adcbb07a2b6d0" />$N = L^F$

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be8077860ff1f5a4e55438" />

  ***

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be802ca41bc2bf01f9bda8" />let:

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be80e1849ac8179a869420" />***N*** is the number of experimental conditions

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be803990c3e7cc332a07f6" />$L_n$ is the number of levels for factor *n*

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be80958e53e7a77d9ad5c0" />***F*** is the number of factors

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be80b8852ad817e30f98ed" />then:

  <a id="3fcd65dd82be80d8bbcdd5507251a161" />$N = \Pi^F_{n=1} L_n$
</Callout>

<a id="400d65dd82be80b0b5f5f4f3037d923b" />As the number of factors increases, the number of required experimental conditions grows rapidly. The team therefore adopted a Box–Behnken-style design to investigate selected factor combinations while reducing the number of conditions compared with a full factorial approach.  This approach was chosen over a full factorial design to reduce the number of experimental conditions while allowing the evaluation of multiple factors.

<a id="400d65dd82be80bdb736cae9b5bba34b" />The study examined the *E. coli* biosensor’s fluorescence output, which was evaluated under different simulated patient-infection conditions.

<a id="400d65dd82be8015b508d16c3adde509" />Experimental conditions were established using two liquid-handling systems. Multichannel pipetting delivered bacterial cultures and media, while the Echo dispensed chemical treatments. Fluorescence was measured as the reporter readout to assess biosensor responses across the tested conditions.

### <a id="400d65dd82be80c1ad81ea3ba7e7f7a4" />Biological Factors - Multichannel Pipette Delivery

<a id="400d65dd82be807e86f4e6b2df0b79b4" />The biological factors included the biosensor promoter, culture medium, and patient-infection condition. The patient-infection conditions included a no-infection control, untransformed *E. coli* EPI300, and bacterial cultures representing different antibiotic-resistance backgrounds: β-lactamase, gentamicin resistance (GMR), and kanamycin resistance (KanR). LB and LB-Cm media were used as applicable to the corresponding cultures.

### <a id="400d65dd82be80d3a8d8fd3818a05fc6" />Chemical Factors - Echo delivery

<a id="400d65dd82be800f91ddc5069de87f9d" />The chemical factors were introduced using the Echo liquid-handling system. Three antibiotics, amoxicillin, gentamicin, and tobramycin, were tested at different combinations. Sodium and glucose were also used as chemical variables, each with three planned concentration levels. A plate map was developed to define the chemical conditions assigned to each destination well and guide the Echo liquid-handling process. Using this map, the Echo transferred the corresponding chemical solutions from their source plates into the same destination plate, combining the assigned chemical factors according to the experimental design.

# <a id="3fcd65dd82be80c5b7f1c3f33e86a52b" />Discussion

## <a id="400d65dd82be8080a0c5fc358b92d3a2" />Response Surface Analysis

<a id="400d65dd82be80efb767c1e45a51cd4f" />Standard biosensor characterization often evaluates individual factors separately, whereas the response-surface analysis revealed a significant interaction between antibiotic concentration and glucose for both CpxP and RecA (p \< 0.0001). The positive interaction coefficients (+2,706 for CpxP and +232.5 for RecA) indicate that the relationship between antibiotic concentration and maximum GFP changes with glucose level. This highlights the value of evaluating combinations of experimental conditions when characterizing biosensor performance. Although mechanisms such as altered growth or cellular metabolism could contribute to this interaction, further experiments would be needed to establish its biological basis.

<a id="400d65dd82be8055a539f13f4a7a89e1" />The growth-medium analysis further demonstrated the importance of interpreting fluorescence alongside bacterial growth. LB-Cm was associated with higher maximum GFP per $OD_{600}$ for both sensors, but  CpxP showed increased maximum GFP, whereas RecA showed lower maximum $OD_{600}$ without a significant change in maximum GFP. Thus, an increased GFP/$OD_{600}$ ratio does not necessarily indicate increased reporter expression; it may also reflect differences in cell density. Considering both fluorescence and growth measurements is therefore important when evaluating biosensor output.

## <a id="400d65dd82be805b84eceed03440045b" />Antibiotic Concentrations

<a id="400d65dd82be8096bc35d9723d450f30" />All three antibiotics produced similar $OD_{600}$ trends. Each antibiotic reduced growth of the CpxP and RecA sensors, particularly after the cells started to exponentially grow (around the 3 hour mark). Higher antibiotic concentrations were found to reduce $OD_{600}$ levels.

<a id="400d65dd82be80f78a11d0672ce01a34" />The two aminoglycosides,  gentamicin and tobramycin had clear separation between each dose. At the highest concentration of antibiotic (8ug/mL), both the gentamycin and tobramycin curves start to decline, suggesting cell killing.

<a id="400d65dd82be807384b7db7bc22f5247" />In comparison, the $OD_{600}$ curves for amoxicillin overlap between increasing doses. This may be due to the fact the results average across a mix of resistant (β-lactamase–producing) and susceptible strains.

<a id="400d65dd82be80b59118cb62eed0d56a" />For the CpxP sensor, GFP was similar across all concentrations until about 5–6 hours, after which the curves began to separate. With amoxicillin, GFP increased as the concentration increased, with the highest dose giving the strongest signal by the end of the run. With gentamicin and tobramycin, GFP decreased as the concentration increased, with the highest dose giving the lowest signal.

<a id="400d65dd82be8020b9a7e76dcf08a24c" />For the RecA sensor, GFP stayed low and nearly flat for all three antibiotics. The curves for each concentration overlapped throughout the run, with no clear difference between doses.

## <a id="400d65dd82be805d9a03d8d5b2632f01" />Sodium

<a id="400d65dd82be805ebb70ef468845328d" />Across the tested sodium conditions, $OD_{600}$ levels remained relatively consistent, suggesting that sodium variation had little observable effect on bacterial growth under the conditions tested. Similarly, GFP fluorescence showed limited variation across sodium concentrations under the different antibiotic treatments. These findings suggest that sodium concentration may have a limited influence on biosensor performance within the range investigated. However, further testing would be needed to determine whether this trend holds under the more complex conditions found in actual urine samples.

## <a id="400d65dd82be80039283c4edfdb2bdce" />Glucose

<a id="400d65dd82be802f975cc503098ef592" />Glucose concentration appeared to influence GFP fluorescence at later measurement time points, with higher glucose concentrations associated with lower fluorescence levels. This trend suggests that glucose may affect biosensor output over time. However, additional analysis is needed to determine whether the observed changes are related to bacterial growth, changes in reporter expression, or other factors affecting the fluorescence signal. Understanding this relationship will help determine whether glucose should be considered an important variable when optimizing biosensor performance in simulated urine conditions.

## <a id="400d65dd82be80e793b2f3cf1da0a3bb" />LB/LB-Cm

<a id="400d65dd82be80ebb5fdef05c2de862b" />The sensor plasmids carry a chloramphenicol (Cm) resistance marker. Growing cells in LB-Cm kills any cells that don’t contain the plasmid, so only transformed cells survive. Without Cm, there’s nothing to stop cells from losing the plasmid over time, and cells that lose it also lose their sensing ability. To test this, the sensor was run in both LB and LB-Cm, with each culture mixed 1:1 with the patient strains. If the sensor’s GFP behaves the same in both media, the plasmid was retained even without Cm selection.

<a id="400d65dd82be80bd92e7c12ca058465d" />For $OD_{600}$, the LB and LB-Cm curves followed the same overall shape on both plates. $OD_{600}$ in LB-Cm was slightly lower than in LB from the start of exponential growth through the end of the run. However, the confidence intervals overlapped for most of the run.

<a id="400d65dd82be804a837bd8fc30a37813" />For GFP, the LB and LB-Cm curves were almost identical on both the CpxP and RecA plates. They followed the same trend over the whole run, and their confidence intervals overlapped completely. Because GFP behaved the same with and without Cm, this suggests the sensor plasmids were retained over the 12-hour experiment even without antibiotic selection.
