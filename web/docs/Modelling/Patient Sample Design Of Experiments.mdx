# <a id="3fcd65dd82be8018b984cd9454040b94" />Abstract

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80abace3f8c39492880e" />Bacterial biosensors have potential applications in detecting biological relevant signals, such as infection biomarkers or antibiotics in clinical samples. However, theri performance can be heavily influenced by the chemical and biological conditions of the sample environment in which they operate. In our project, an *E. coli* promoter-GFP biosensor is used to detect pathogen-associated signals in urine. However, urine introduces uncharacterized environmental variability that can potentially alter biosensor output.

<a id="3fcd65dd82be808db2dcecc17e9642c6" />Therefore, this study aims to investigate the performance of the E. coli promoter-GFP biosensor under simulated patient-infection conditions and characterize matrix factors that may influence their response including antibiotic concentration, bacterial resistance backgrounds, and environmental conditions such as sodium and glucose concentrations. Using a Design of Experiments (DOE) approach n collaboration with Biofactorial, an automation facility at the University of British Columbia, we systematically evaluate biosensor responses across selected experimental conditions. The resulting data will directly inform upstream sample preparation and filtration design, allowing for reliable biosensor performance in clinical applications.

# <a id="3fcd65dd82be806eb174ef24ed0cff57" />Background

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80d39a00d948d7dd42a3" />Urine consists of primarily water, some salts and metabolic byproducts , and other compounds whose concentrations vary between individual and physiological conditions . These differences can influence the growth of bacteria and the performance of bacterial biosensors intended for use in patient samples. To investigate these effects, we decided to focus determining urine-associated factors that could be experimentally controlled and incorporated into an automated screening workflow.

<a id="3fdd65dd82be80b7b079cd636e58491e" />The experimental factors were selected to represent different aspects of the urinary environment. Sodium concentration was included to investigate the effect of  ionic conditions on bacterial growth and biosensor output, while glucose concentration was included to examine whether the availability  of an additional carbon source influences bacterial activity or sensor performance. pH was also included as a chemical factor as acidity and alkalinity can affect the bacterial physiology and chemical environment in which the biosensors operate. Finally, antibiotic concentration was investigated to characterize biosensor responses across different exposure levels relevant to antibiotic detection and susceptibility-related experiments.

<a id="3fcd65dd82be80d8a8ffef0d62979a43" />To characterize biosensor performance under simulated infection conditions and guide future validation and optimization, a DOE using the Box Behnken method was used via the JMP software. The Box-Behnken design is especially useful as it approach supports the evaluation of individual factor effects and potential interactions between factors using fewer runs to help identify conditions that may substantially affect the measured response.

#### <a id="400d65dd82be8001ac0ef902203e0bf1" />Automation via Biofactorial

<a id="400d65dd82be80e6a878d8bd260054e7" />Physical pipetting of multi-factor matrices across numerous experimental conditions is prone to human error and high variability. Therefore, we partnered with Biofactorial, an automation facility at the University of British Columbia, to execute autonomous liquid handling. Automation allowed experimental conditions to be prepare systematically across multiple wells, supporting consistent implementation of the experimental design and subsequent comparison of biosensor responses.

### <a id="400d65dd82be80babc0dd9b5cae96e34" />2. Experimental Design

<a id="400d65dd82be802d899bd9f165141537" />A reduced DOE approach using Box Behnken design was developed using JMP in collaboration with Biofactorial to investigate the effects of bacterial and environmental conditions on the promoter-GFP biosensor fluorescence output. This approach was chosen over a full factorial design to reduce the number of experimental conditions while allowing the evaluation of multiple factors.

<a id="400d65dd82be80bea7a0fd64d57b2375" />The number of possible experimental conditions depends on the number of factors and their respective levels. In a full factorial design, every possible combination of factor levels is tested. As the number of factors increases, the number of required experimental conditions grows rapidly. The team therefore adopted a Box–Behnken-style design to investigate selected factor combinations while reducing the number of conditions compared with a full factorial approach.

<a id="400d65dd82be80bdb736cae9b5bba34b" />The study examined the *E. coli* biosensor’s fluorescence output, which was evaluated under different simulated patient-infection conditions.

<a id="400d65dd82be8015b508d16c3adde509" />Experimental conditions were established using two liquid-handling systems. Multichannel pipetting delivered bacterial cultures and media, while the Echo dispensed chemical treatments. Fluorescence was measured as the reporter readout to assess biosensor responses across the tested conditions.

#### <a id="400d65dd82be80c1ad81ea3ba7e7f7a4" />Biological Factors -Multichannel Pipette Delivery

<a id="400d65dd82be807e86f4e6b2df0b79b4" />The biological factors included the biosensor promoter, culture medium, and patient-infection condition. The patient-infection conditions included a no-infection control, untransformed *E. coli* EPI300, and bacterial cultures representing different antibiotic-resistance backgrounds: β-lactamase, gentamicin resistance (GMR), and kanamycin resistance (KanR). LB and LB-Cm media were used as applicable to the corresponding cultures.

<a id="400d65dd82be800f91ddc5069de87f9d" />The chemical factors were introduced using the Echo liquid-handling system. Antibiotic was used as a factor, and was varied as a categorical condition, either with no antibiotic or varying concentrations. Amoxicillin, gentamicin, and tobramycin were the antibiotics used. Sodium and glucose were also used as chemical variables, each with three planned concentration levels.
